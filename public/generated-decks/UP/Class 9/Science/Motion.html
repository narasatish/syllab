What is Motion?

  • An object is in motion if its position changes with time.
  • It's all about observing change!

🎙️ Think about a car moving on a road, a bird flying in the sky, or even you walking to school. All these are examples of motion. Simply put, if an object changes its place over a period of time, we say it is in motion.

Rest vs. Motion: A Relative Concept

  • An object at rest does not change its position.
  • Motion and rest are not absolute, they depend on the observer.

🎙️ Imagine you are sitting in a moving bus. To you, the other passengers are at rest. But to someone standing outside the bus, both you and the other passengers are in motion! This shows that rest and motion are relative terms, depending on your 'frame of reference'.

Describing Motion: The Need for a Reference Point

  • To describe motion, we need a starting point.
  • This fixed point is called the origin or reference point.

🎙️ How do we tell someone where something is moving from? We need a reference point. For example, if you say 'the school is 2 km north', you are using your current location as the reference point. A railway station or a landmark is often used as a reference point.

Distance: How Far Has It Travelled?

  • The total path length covered by an object.
  • It is a scalar quantity (only magnitude, no direction).
  • SI unit is metre (m).

🎙️ Distance is simply the total length of the path an object takes to move from one point to another. If you walk from your home to a shop, then to a friend's house, and then back home, the total length of your journey is the distance you covered. It doesn't care about direction.

📏
Total Path

Displacement: The Shortest Path

  • The shortest straight-line path between initial and final positions.
  • It is a vector quantity (magnitude AND direction).
  • SI unit is metre (m).

🎙️ Displacement is different from distance. It's the straight-line distance from where you started to where you ended up, along with the direction. If you walk 5m east and then 5m west, your total distance is 10m, but your displacement is 0m because you ended up at your starting point.

Distance vs. Displacement: Key Differences

  • Distance is path-dependent; Displacement is path-independent.
  • Distance is always positive; Displacement can be positive, negative, or zero.

🎙️ This is a crucial distinction. Remember, distance tells you 'how much ground was covered', while displacement tells you 'how far and in what direction is the final position from the initial position'. For example, running one lap around a circular track means you covered a distance, but your displacement is zero.

Worked Example 1: Distance & Displacement

  • A person walks 4 km East, then 3 km North.
  • Find the total distance covered.
  • Find the magnitude of displacement.

🎙️ Let's solve this. The total distance is simply 4 km + 3 km = 7 km. For displacement, imagine a right-angled triangle. The hypotenuse is the displacement. Using Pythagoras theorem, displacement = √(4² + 3²) = √(16 + 9) = √25 = 5 km. The direction would be North-East.

Types of Motion: Uniform and Non-uniform

  • Motion can be classified based on how speed changes.
  • Two main types: Uniform Motion and Non-uniform Motion.

🎙️ Not all movements are the same. Sometimes a car moves at a steady speed, and sometimes it speeds up or slows down. We classify these different ways of moving into two main types, which helps us understand them better.

Uniform Motion: Constant Speed

  • An object covers equal distances in equal intervals of time.
  • Speed remains constant throughout the journey.

🎙️ Think of a train moving on a straight track without changing its speed. If it covers 100 meters every 5 seconds, it's in uniform motion. This means its speed is constant. In real life, perfect uniform motion is rare, but it's a good concept to understand.

Non-uniform Motion: Changing Speed

  • An object covers unequal distances in equal intervals of time.
  • Speed changes, meaning it can accelerate or decelerate.

🎙️ Most motions we see around us are non-uniform. When you ride a bicycle, you speed up, slow down, stop, and start again. This is non-uniform motion because your speed is constantly changing. A car in city traffic is another great example.

Speed: How Fast is It Moving?

  • The rate at which an object covers distance.
  • Formula: Speed = Distance / Time.
  • SI unit: metre per second (m/s).

🎙️ Speed tells us how quickly an object is moving. If two cars travel the same distance, the one that takes less time has a higher speed. It only cares about the magnitude, not the direction, making it a scalar quantity.

⚡
Rate of Distance

Average Speed: Over the Whole Journey

  • Calculated when an object's speed is non-uniform.
  • Formula: Average Speed = Total Distance / Total Time.

🎙️ Since most motion is non-uniform, we often talk about average speed. It gives us an overall idea of how fast an object moved during its entire journey, even if its speed varied at different points. It's like finding the 'mean' speed.

⏱️
Overall Rate

Worked Example 2: Calculating Average Speed

  • A car travels 100 km in 2 hours, then 120 km in 3 hours.
  • What is the average speed of the car?

🎙️ First, calculate the total distance: 100 km + 120 km = 220 km. Next, find the total time: 2 hours + 3 hours = 5 hours. Finally, divide total distance by total time: Average Speed = 220 km / 5 hours = 44 km/h. Simple, right?

Velocity: Speed with Direction

  • The rate of change of displacement.
  • Formula: Velocity = Displacement / Time.
  • SI unit: metre per second (m/s), with direction.

🎙️ Velocity is a vector quantity because it includes both speed and direction. If a car is moving at 60 km/h towards the North, that's its velocity. If it turns East but maintains 60 km/h, its speed is the same, but its velocity has changed because the direction changed.

Speed vs. Velocity: A Key Distinction

  • Speed is a scalar quantity; Velocity is a vector quantity.
  • Speed only has magnitude; Velocity has magnitude and direction.

🎙️ Remember, speed tells you 'how fast', while velocity tells you 'how fast AND in what direction'. If an object moves at a constant speed in a circular path, its speed is constant, but its velocity is continuously changing because its direction is always changing.

Acceleration: Change in Velocity

  • The rate of change of velocity.
  • Formula: Acceleration (a) = (Final Velocity - Initial Velocity) / Time.
  • SI unit: metre per second squared (m/s²).

🎙️ Acceleration is not just about speeding up! It's any change in velocity. So, if an object speeds up, slows down, or even changes direction while maintaining the same speed, it is accelerating. Think of a car pressing the accelerator or the brakes.

🚀
Velocity Change

Types of Acceleration: Positive, Negative, Zero

  • Positive acceleration: Velocity increases (speeds up).
  • Negative acceleration (Retardation/Deceleration): Velocity decreases (slows down).
  • Zero acceleration: Velocity is constant (uniform velocity).

🎙️ When you press the gas pedal in a car, you experience positive acceleration. When you apply the brakes, that's negative acceleration or deceleration. If you're cruising at a steady speed on a highway, your acceleration is zero.

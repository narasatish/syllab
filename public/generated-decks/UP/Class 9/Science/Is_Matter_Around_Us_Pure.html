🎙️ Hello students! Today, we're going to explore a very interesting question: Is everything we see around us truly pure? We often hear about 'pure' milk or 'pure' gold, but what does 'pure' actually mean in science? Let's find out!

🎙️ Before we dive into purity, let's quickly recall what matter is. Matter is anything that has mass and occupies space. Everything around us, from the air we breathe to the books we read, is made up of matter.

Pure vs. Impure Substances

  • Scientific meaning of 'pure'.
  • Distinguish between pure and impure.
  • Understand 'mixture' as an impure substance.

🎙️ In our daily lives, 'pure' might mean no artificial additives. But in science, a 'pure substance' has a uniform composition throughout. An 'impure substance' or 'mixture' has different components mixed together.

What are Pure Substances?

  • Consist of a single type of particle.
  • Uniform composition.
  • Fixed properties.

🎙️ A pure substance consists of a single type of particle. These particles can be atoms or molecules, but they are all identical. Pure substances have a fixed composition and distinct properties.

💎
Uniform Composition

Elements: The Building Blocks

  • Simplest form of pure substance.
  • Cannot be broken down chemically.
  • Examples: Gold, Oxygen, Iron.

🎙️ Elements are the simplest form of pure substances that cannot be broken down into simpler substances by chemical reactions. Examples include gold, oxygen, and iron. Each element is unique.

🌟🌟🌟
Gold, Oxygen, Iron

Compounds: Elements Joined Together

  • Two or more elements chemically combined.
  • Fixed ratio by mass.
  • Examples: Water (H₂O), Salt (NaCl).

🎙️ Compounds are pure substances formed when two or more elements chemically combine in a fixed ratio. For example, water (H₂O) is a compound of hydrogen and oxygen, and salt (NaCl) is a compound of sodium and chlorine.

🧪
Fixed Ratio

What are Mixtures?

  • Two or more pure substances physically combined.
  • Components retain individual properties.
  • Can be separated by physical means.

🎙️ Mixtures are impure substances formed when two or more pure substances (elements or compounds) are physically combined. The components retain their individual properties and can be separated by physical means.

Combined, Not Joined

🎙️ Mixtures are broadly classified into two types: homogeneous and heterogeneous. The main difference lies in whether their composition is uniform throughout or not. Let's look at each type in detail.

Homogeneous Mixtures: Uniform Throughout

  • Uniform composition and properties.
  • Components are indistinguishable.
  • Examples: Saltwater, Air.

🎙️ A homogeneous mixture has a uniform composition and properties throughout. You cannot see the different components separately. Examples include saltwater, sugar solution, and air.

💧
Evenly Mixed
10 / 25

Heterogeneous Mixtures: Non-Uniform

  • Non-uniform composition.
  • Components are often visible.
  • Examples: Sand in water, Oil and water.

🎙️ A heterogeneous mixture has a non-uniform composition, and its components can often be seen separately. The properties vary in different parts of the mixture. Examples include sand in water, oil and water, or a mixture of salt and iron filings.

🚰
Unevenly Mixed

Solutions: Special Homogeneous Mixtures

  • Homogeneous mixture of two or more substances.
  • Appear transparent.
  • Particles are very small and stable.

🎙️ A solution is a homogeneous mixture of two or more substances. It appears transparent, and its particles are extremely small, making it stable – the particles don't settle down over time.

🥤
Clear & Stable

Solute and Solvent

  • Components of a solution.
  • Solute: substance that dissolves.
  • Solvent: substance that dissolves the solute.

🎙️ A solution has two main components: the solute and the solvent. The solute is the substance that dissolves, and the solvent is the substance that dissolves the solute. Usually, the solvent is present in a larger amount.

Everyday Solutions

  • Sugar water, Saltwater.
  • Air (mixture of gases).
  • Alloys (e.g., brass, bronze).

🎙️ Many everyday items are solutions. Tea, lemonade, and even air are common examples. Alloys, like brass (copper and zinc), are also solid solutions. They are uniform throughout.

☕☕☕☕
Tea, Lemonade, Alloys

Key Properties of Solutions

  • Particle size < 1 nm.
  • Do not scatter light (no Tyndall effect).
  • Stable; particles do not settle.

🎙️ Solution particles are smaller than 1 nm in diameter, so they cannot be seen with the naked eye. They do not scatter a beam of light (Tyndall effect is not shown), and they are stable, meaning the particles do not settle down.

🔍
Tiny Particles

Suspensions: Visible Particles

  • Heterogeneous mixture.
  • Solid particles dispersed in liquid.
  • Particles are visible to the naked eye.

🎙️ A suspension is a heterogeneous mixture where solid particles are dispersed in a liquid without dissolving. The particles are large enough to be seen with the naked eye. Muddy water is a classic example.

🌫️
Muddy Water
17 / 25

Key Properties of Suspensions

  • Particle size > 100 nm.
  • Particles settle down (unstable).
  • Scatter light (Tyndall effect shown).

🎙️ Suspension particles are large enough to be seen and settle down when left undisturbed, making them unstable. They scatter a beam of light passing through it, making the path visible (Tyndall effect).

💡
Scatters Light
18 / 25

Colloids: In Between Solutions & Suspensions

  • Heterogeneous, but appear homogeneous.
  • Intermediate particle size (1-100 nm).
  • Examples: Milk, Fog, Ink.

🎙️ Colloids are heterogeneous mixtures, but they appear homogeneous to the naked eye. Their particle size is intermediate, larger than solutions but smaller than suspensions. Milk, fog, and ink are common examples.

🥛
Milk & Fog
19 / 25

Key Properties of Colloids

  • Particles visible under microscope.
  • Stable; do not settle down.
  • Scatter light (Tyndall effect shown).

🎙️ Colloidal particles are too small to be seen individually but large enough to scatter light (Tyndall effect). They are stable and do not settle down when left undisturbed. They cannot be separated by simple filtration.

🌈
Tyndall Effect

Evaporation: Separating Solids from Liquids

  • Separates non-volatile solid from volatile liquid.
  • Liquid turns into vapor.
  • Solid is left behind.

🎙️ Evaporation is used to separate a non-volatile solid component from its volatile liquid solvent. For example, we can separate salt from saltwater by heating the solution; the water evaporates, leaving the salt behind.

Filtration: Separating Insoluble Solids

  • Separates insoluble solid from liquid.
  • Uses a filter medium (e.g., filter paper).
  • Solid remains on filter, liquid passes through.

🎙️ Filtration is a method used to separate insoluble solid particles from a liquid or gas. A filter paper or sieve allows the liquid to pass through while retaining the solid particles. Think of making tea.

☕
Coffee Filter
24 / 25

Centrifugation: Spinning for Separation

  • Separates very fine suspended particles.
  • Mixture rotated rapidly.
  • Denser particles settle at bottom.

🎙️ Centrifugation is used to separate very fine suspended particles from a liquid. The mixture is rotated rapidly in a centrifuge, forcing denser particles to the bottom and lighter particles to stay at the top. This is used in dairies to separate cream from milk.

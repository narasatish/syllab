Ancient Indian Wisdom: 'Panchtatva' and 'Parmanu'

  • Ancient Indian thinkers proposed 'Panchtatva' (five elements).
  • Maharishi Kanad introduced 'Parmanu' (indivisible particle).
  • He believed matter is composed of these ultimate, indivisible particles.

🎙️ Long, long ago, even before Western philosophers, Indian sages contemplated the nature of matter. Maharishi Kanad, around 6th century BCE, gave us the concept of 'Parmanu', suggesting that matter is made of tiny, indestructible particles that cannot be divided further. This was a remarkable insight!

Greek Philosophers: The Idea of 'Atomos'

  • Democritus and Leucippus (5th-4th century BCE) proposed 'atomos'.
  • 'Atomos' means 'uncuttable' or 'indivisible'.
  • They suggested matter is made of tiny, indivisible particles.

🎙️ Across the world, at a similar time, Greek philosophers like Democritus and Leucippus also arrived at a similar conclusion. They coined the term 'atomos', from which our modern word 'atom' comes. They believed these 'atomos' were solid, uniform, and could not be further divided.

The Atom: A Modern Definition

  • An atom is the smallest particle of an element.
  • It may or may not exist independently.
  • It always takes part in a chemical reaction.

🎙️ So, bringing it to modern science, an atom is defined as the smallest particle of an element. While some atoms can exist on their own, many prefer to combine with other atoms. Crucially, atoms are the fundamental units that participate in all chemical changes.

Can We See Atoms?

  • No, atoms cannot be seen with the naked eye.
  • Even powerful optical microscopes cannot resolve them.
  • Specialized instruments like Scanning Electron Microscopes (SEM) can image surfaces where atoms are present.

🎙️ Due to their minuscule size, it's impossible to see individual atoms with our eyes or even with regular optical microscopes. Scientists use advanced tools like Scanning Electron Microscopes (SEM) to get an idea of atomic arrangements on surfaces, but we don't 'see' them like we see a ball.

Early Symbols: Dalton's Atomic Theory

  • John Dalton (early 19th century) proposed symbols for elements.
  • He used specific shapes and patterns for each element.
  • These symbols were unique but difficult to draw and remember.

🎙️ When John Dalton developed his atomic theory, he also came up with symbols for elements. For example, a circle with a dot in the middle represented Oxygen, and a simple circle for Hydrogen. While innovative, these symbols were not practical for widespread use due to their complexity.

Modern Symbols: The IUPAC System

  • Berzelius suggested using one or two letters of the element's name.
  • The International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC) approves symbols.
  • The first letter is always capital, the second (if any) is small.

🎙️ To make things simpler and universal, a Swedish chemist named Berzelius proposed using letters. Today, the IUPAC is responsible for approving these symbols. For example, 'H' for Hydrogen, 'O' for Oxygen, 'Na' for Sodium (from its Latin name Natrium), and 'Cl' for Chlorine.

Atomic Mass: The Weight of an Atom

  • Atoms are extremely light, so we use a relative atomic mass scale.
  • One atomic mass unit (amu) is defined as 1/12th the mass of a Carbon-12 atom.
  • This helps us compare the masses of different atoms easily.

🎙️ Since atoms are so tiny, their actual masses are incredibly small and inconvenient to work with. So, scientists developed a relative scale. They decided to use the mass of a Carbon-12 atom as a standard and assigned it 12 atomic mass units. All other atomic masses are compared to this standard.

How Do Atoms Usually Exist?

  • Most atoms do not exist independently.
  • They combine with other atoms to form molecules.
  • They can also form ions by gaining or losing electrons.

🎙️ While some noble gases like Helium or Neon exist as single atoms, most atoms are reactive. They achieve stability by either joining together to form larger units called molecules, or by gaining or losing electrons to become charged particles called ions.

Molecules: Group of Atoms

  • A molecule is a group of two or more atoms.
  • These atoms are chemically bonded together.
  • Molecules can exist independently and show the properties of the substance.

🎙️ Imagine several friends holding hands – that's similar to how atoms bond to form a molecule! A molecule is the smallest particle of a substance that can exist independently and retain all the chemical properties of that substance. For example, a single water molecule still behaves like water.

Molecules of Elements: Same Atoms

  • Composed of two or more identical atoms.
  • Examples: Oxygen (O2), Nitrogen (N2), Ozone (O3).
  • Can be mono-, di-, or poly-atomic based on the number of atoms.

🎙️ When atoms of the *same* element combine, they form molecules of an element. A single Helium atom is a molecule (monoatomic). Two oxygen atoms form an Oxygen molecule (diatomic). Three oxygen atoms form Ozone (triatomic), and four phosphorus atoms form a phosphorus molecule (polyatomic).

Molecules of Compounds: Different Atoms

  • Composed of two or more different types of atoms.
  • These atoms are joined in a fixed ratio.
  • Examples: Water (H2O), Carbon Dioxide (CO2), Ammonia (NH3).

🎙️ Now, if atoms of *different* elements combine in a fixed proportion, they form molecules of a compound. Think of water, H2O, where two hydrogen atoms are always bonded to one oxygen atom. Or carbon dioxide, CO2, with one carbon and two oxygen atoms. These ratios are constant.

Ions: Charged Particles

  • An ion is an atom or group of atoms that has a net electrical charge.
  • Formed by losing (cation, positive charge) or gaining (anion, negative charge) electrons.
  • Example: Na+ (Sodium ion), Cl- (Chloride ion).

🎙️ Sometimes, atoms can become electrically charged. If an atom loses one or more electrons, it becomes positively charged, called a cation. If it gains one or more electrons, it becomes negatively charged, called an anion. These charged particles are known as ions.

Ionic Compounds: Held by Charge

  • Compounds formed by the transfer of electrons between atoms.
  • Consist of oppositely charged ions held together by electrostatic forces.
  • Example: Sodium Chloride (NaCl) – Na+ and Cl- ions.

🎙️ When cations and anions attract each other due to their opposite charges, they form ionic compounds. Common table salt, Sodium Chloride, is a perfect example. Sodium atoms lose an electron to become Na+ ions, and chlorine atoms gain an electron to become Cl- ions, and then they attract each other to form NaCl.

Writing Chemical Formulae: The Language of Chemistry

  • A chemical formula represents the composition of a molecule or ionic compound.
  • It uses symbols of elements and subscripts to show the number of atoms.
  • We use the 'criss-cross' method for ionic compounds.

🎙️ Chemical formulae are like short-hand notes for chemists. They tell us exactly what atoms are present in a compound and in what ratio. For ionic compounds, we often use a simple 'criss-cross' method involving the valencies (combining power) of the elements to derive the formula.

Worked Example: Formula of Water

  • Elements: Hydrogen (H), Oxygen (O)
  • Valency of H = 1
  • Valency of O = 2
  • Criss-cross valencies: H1 O2 becomes H2O

🎙️ Let's apply the criss-cross method for water. Hydrogen has a valency of 1, and Oxygen has a valency of 2. We write the symbols, then their valencies below them. Then, we criss-cross the valencies, giving Oxygen's valency to Hydrogen and Hydrogen's to Oxygen. This gives us H2O.

Worked Example: Formula of Sodium Chloride

  • Elements: Sodium (Na), Chlorine (Cl)
  • Valency of Na = 1
  • Valency of Cl = 1
  • Criss-cross valencies: Na1 Cl1 becomes NaCl

The Mole Concept: Counting Atoms

  • Atoms and molecules are too small to count individually.
  • The 'mole' is a unit to count a large number of particles (atoms, molecules, ions).
  • One mole contains Avogadro's number (6.022 x 10^23) of particles.

🎙️ Since atoms are incredibly tiny, counting them one by one is impossible. So, chemists use a special unit called the 'mole'. Just like a 'dozen' means 12, a 'mole' represents a specific, very large number of particles – Avogadro's number, which is 6.022 x 10^23.

The mole is a unit of amount of substance. One mole contains Avogadro's number of particle

Molar Mass: Mass of One Mole

  • The molar mass is the mass of one mole of a substance.
  • It is numerically equal to the atomic/molecular mass in grams.
  • Example: Molar mass of Water (H2O) = 18 g/mol.

🎙️ Molar mass is simply the mass of one mole of a substance. If an atom has an atomic mass of 'x' amu, then one mole of that atom will have a molar mass of 'x' grams. For example, the atomic mass of Oxygen is 16 amu, so its molar mass is 16 grams per mole.

Recap: Atoms and Molecules

  • Atoms are the smallest indivisible particles of an element.
  • Molecules are groups of atoms bonded together.

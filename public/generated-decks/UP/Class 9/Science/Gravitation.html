Newton's Universal Law of Gravitation

  • Proposed by Isaac Newton
  • Every object attracts every other object
  • Force is directly proportional to product of masses

🎙️ Sir Isaac Newton was the first to formulate this law. He realized that the same force causing an apple to fall also keeps the Moon in orbit around the Earth. This law is truly universal, applying throughout the cosmos, from tiny particles to massive galaxies.

The Gravitational Force Formula

  • F = G * (m₁ * m₂) / r²
  • F is the force of gravitation
  • m₁ and m₂ are the masses of objects
  • r is the distance between their centers

🎙️ This formula is the heart of Newton's Law of Gravitation. It tells us exactly how to calculate the gravitational force between any two objects. Notice that the force gets weaker very quickly as the distance 'r' increases, because 'r' is squared in the denominator.

Universal Gravitational Constant (G)

  • G is a constant value
  • G = 6.674 x 10⁻¹¹ Nm²/kg²
  • Its value never changes, anywhere in the universe

🎙️ The 'G' in our formula is a very important number. It's called the Universal Gravitational Constant. This value was precisely determined by Henry Cavendish. It's truly universal, meaning it's the same whether you're on Earth, the Moon, or in a distant galaxy.

Free Fall: Falling Under Gravity Alone

  • When an object falls only under gravity
  • No air resistance or other forces
  • All objects accelerate at the same rate

🎙️ Imagine dropping a feather and a stone in a vacuum. They would hit the ground at the exact same time! This is because, in free fall, the acceleration is constant for all objects, regardless of their mass. Air resistance is usually what makes lighter objects fall slower.

Mass vs. Weight: What's the Difference?

  • Mass: Amount of matter in an object (constant)
  • Weight: Force of gravity on an object (changes)
  • Weight = Mass × g

🎙️ This is a common point of confusion! Mass is an intrinsic property of an object and stays the same everywhere. Weight, however, is a force and depends on the gravitational acceleration. So, your mass is the same on Earth and the Moon, but your weight would be much less on the Moon.

Worked Example 1: Calculating Gravitational Force

  • Problem: Find the gravitational force between two objects.
  • Object 1: 50 kg, Object 2: 100 kg
  • Distance between them: 1 meter

🎙️ Let's apply our formula: F = G * (m₁ * m₂) / r². We know G, m₁, m₂, and r. Substitute these values carefully into the formula. Remember to use the correct units for each quantity for an accurate result.

Worked Example 1: Solution

  • G = 6.674 x 10⁻¹¹ Nm²/kg²
  • F = (6.674 x 10⁻¹¹) * (50 * 100) / (1²)
  • F = 3.337 x 10⁻⁷ N
  • This force is very small!

🎙️ As you can see, the gravitational force between two everyday objects is extremely tiny. This is why we don't feel ourselves being pulled towards our friends or furniture. The force only becomes significant when one or both objects have a very large mass, like Earth.

Buoyancy: The Upward Push

  • Upward force exerted by a fluid on an immersed object
  • Acts opposite to the force of gravity
  • Makes objects feel lighter in water

🎙️ Have you ever felt lighter when you're in a swimming pool? That's due to buoyancy! When an object is submerged in a fluid, the fluid pushes it upwards. This upward force is called the buoyant force, and it counteracts gravity to some extent.

Archimedes' Principle

  • States the buoyant force equals the weight of fluid displaced
  • Explains why objects float or sink
  • Crucial for ship design

🎙️ Archimedes' principle is a fundamental law of fluid mechanics. It provides a way to calculate the buoyant force. An object floats if the buoyant force is greater than or equal to its weight, and sinks if its weight is greater than the buoyant force.

Real-Life Example: Why Ships Float

  • Ships are designed to displace a lot of water
  • The weight of this displaced water creates a large buoyant force
  • This force supports the ship's massive weight

🎙️ Even though ships are made of steel, which is denser than water, they float because of their shape. They are hollow, allowing them to displace a large volume of water. The buoyant force generated by this displaced water is sufficient to support the ship's entire weight, keeping it afloat.

Density: How Much 'Stuff' in a Space?

  • Measure of how much mass is contained in a given volume
  • Density = Mass / Volume
  • Units: kg/m³ or g/cm³

🎙️ Density helps us understand how 'packed' the matter is in an object. A small, heavy object has high density, while a large, light object has low density. This concept is crucial for understanding why some objects float and others sink.

Relative Density: Comparing to Water

  • Ratio of an object's density to the density of water
  • Relative Density = Density of object / Density of water
  • Has no units (dimensionless quantity)

🎙️ Relative density is a convenient way to compare how dense an object is compared to water. If an object's relative density is less than 1, it will float in water. If it's greater than 1, it will sink. If it's equal to 1, it will remain suspended.

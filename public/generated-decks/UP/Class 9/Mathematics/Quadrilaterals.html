Defining a Quadrilateral

  • A polygon with four sides.
  • It has four vertices and four angles.
  • The word 'quad' means four, 'lateral' means side.

🎙️ A quadrilateral is simply any closed shape that has exactly four straight sides. Think of it like a four-sided fence. Every time you see a shape with four straight edges, it's a quadrilateral!

Parts of a Quadrilateral

  • Sides: The four line segments.
  • Vertices: The four points where sides meet.
  • Angles: The four angles formed inside.
  • Diagonals: Lines connecting opposite vertices.

🎙️ Just like a human body has different parts, a quadrilateral also has specific components. We have sides, corners called vertices, angles at these corners, and lines connecting opposite corners called diagonals. Knowing these parts helps us describe them better.

The Angle Sum Property

  • The sum of all interior angles is 360°.
  • This is true for ANY quadrilateral.
  • Can be proven by dividing into two triangles.

🎙️ A very important property of all quadrilaterals is that if you add up all four of its interior angles, the total will always be 360 degrees. Imagine cutting a quadrilateral into two triangles; each triangle's angles sum to 180°, so two triangles make 360°.

➕

Example: Finding an Unknown Angle

  • Given three angles: 80°, 100°, 90°.
  • Let the fourth angle be 'x'.
  • Sum = 360° => 80+100+90+x = 360.

🎙️ Let's try an example. If we know three angles of a quadrilateral are 80, 100, and 90 degrees, how do we find the fourth? We simply use the angle sum property: add the known angles, subtract from 360, and you'll get the missing angle. It's like finding a missing piece of a puzzle!

Parallelogram: The Parallel Family

  • A quadrilateral with opposite sides parallel.
  • Opposite sides are also equal in length.
  • Opposite angles are equal.

🎙️ Our first special type is the Parallelogram. Its defining feature is that both pairs of its opposite sides are parallel to each other. This parallelism also leads to other interesting properties, such as opposite sides being equal in length.

Key Properties of a Parallelogram

  • Opposite sides are equal.
  • Opposite angles are equal.
  • Diagonals bisect each other.
  • Adjacent angles are supplementary (sum to 180°).

🎙️ Beyond parallel sides, parallelograms have a set of distinct properties. Their opposite sides are not only parallel but also equal in length. Their opposite angles are equal, and importantly, their diagonals cut each other exactly in half. Adjacent angles always add up to 180 degrees.

Example: Angles of a Parallelogram

  • One angle is 70°.
  • Find all other angles.
  • Use properties: opposite angles equal, adjacent angles supplementary.

🎙️ If one angle of a parallelogram is 70 degrees, can you find the others? Yes! Since opposite angles are equal, the angle opposite 70° is also 70°. And since adjacent angles add up to 180°, the other two angles must each be 110°. Simple, isn't it?

Rectangle: The Right-Angled Parallelogram

  • A parallelogram with all angles equal to 90°.
  • Opposite sides are equal and parallel.
  • Diagonals are equal in length and bisect each other.

🎙️ A rectangle is a very special type of parallelogram. What makes it special? All of its four angles are exactly 90 degrees! This also means its diagonals are equal in length, a property not found in all parallelograms.

Rhombus: The Equal-Sided Parallelogram

  • A parallelogram with all four sides equal in length.
  • Opposite angles are equal.
  • Diagonals bisect each other at 90°.

🎙️ Next, we have the Rhombus. It's another type of parallelogram, but its unique feature is that all four of its sides are equal in length. Think of a diamond shape! Its diagonals also have a special property: they meet at a perfect right angle.

Square: The Perfect Quadrilateral

  • A rectangle with all four sides equal.
  • A rhombus with all four angles 90°.
  • All sides equal, all angles 90°, diagonals equal and bisect at 90°.

🎙️ The square is like the superstar of quadrilaterals! It combines the best features of both rectangles and rhombuses. All its sides are equal, all its angles are 90 degrees, and its diagonals are equal and bisect each other at right angles. It's perfectly symmetrical!

The Quadrilateral Family Tree

  • Every Square is a Rectangle and a Rhombus.
  • Every Rectangle is a Parallelogram.
  • Every Rhombus is a Parallelogram.
  • Not all Parallelograms are Squares.

🎙️ It's important to understand how these shapes relate. Think of it like a family tree. All squares are also rectangles and rhombuses. All rectangles and rhombuses are also parallelograms. But remember, a parallelogram isn't necessarily a square, rectangle, or rhombus!

Trapezium: One Pair of Parallel Sides

  • A quadrilateral with exactly one pair of parallel sides.
  • These parallel sides are called bases.
  • The non-parallel sides are called legs.

🎙️ Now, let's look at the Trapezium. Unlike a parallelogram which has *two* pairs of parallel sides, a trapezium has *only one* pair of opposite sides that are parallel. Imagine a ladder, where the two vertical sides are parallel, but the slanted sides are not.

Kite: Adjacent Sides are Equal

  • A quadrilateral where two pairs of adjacent sides are equal.
  • One diagonal is the perpendicular bisector of the other.
  • One pair of opposite angles are equal.

🎙️ Finally, we have the Kite. Just like the kites we fly in the sky! Its unique property is that it has two distinct pairs of equal-length sides that are *adjacent* to each other, not opposite. Its diagonals also intersect at a right angle.

Revisiting: Mid-point Theorem (for Triangles)

  • The line segment joining the mid-points of two sides of a triangle is parallel to the third side.
  • And is half of the length of the third side.

🎙️ Before we move to an advanced concept related to quadrilaterals, let's quickly recall the Mid-point Theorem from our triangles chapter. It states that if you connect the midpoints of two sides of a triangle, that new line segment will be parallel to the third side and exactly half its length. This theorem is surprisingly useful for quadrilaterals!

Converse of Mid-point Theorem

  • A line drawn through the mid-point of one side of a triangle.
  • Parallel to another side.
  • Bisects the third side.

🎙️ The converse of the Mid-point Theorem is also very powerful. It says that if you start from the midpoint of one side of a triangle and draw a line parallel to another side, that line will definitely hit the third side exactly at its midpoint. It's like working backwards from the original theorem.

Example: Mid-point Theorem in Quadrilaterals

  • ABCD is a quadrilateral.
  • P, Q, R, S are mid-points of AB, BC, CD, DA.
  • Show PQRS is a parallelogram.

🎙️ Let's see how the Mid-point Theorem helps us with quadrilaterals. If we connect the midpoints of the sides of any quadrilateral, the resulting inner quadrilateral formed by these midpoints will always be a parallelogram! We can prove this by dividing the quadrilateral into two triangles using a diagonal and applying the theorem twice. This is a classic application!

1Join AC (diagonal)
2Apply Mid-point Theorem in ΔABC
3Apply Mid-point Theorem in ΔADC
4Conclude PQRS is parallelogram

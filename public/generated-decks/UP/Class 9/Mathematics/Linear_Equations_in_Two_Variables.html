Recall: What is an Equation?

  • An equation is a mathematical statement showing that two expressions are equal.
  • It always contains an 'equal to' sign (=).
  • Example: 5 + 3 = 8 or x + 2 = 7.

🎙️ Think of an equation like a balanced scale. Whatever is on one side must be exactly equal in value to what is on the other side. This balance is key to solving equations.

Introducing: Why Two Variables?

  • Many real-life situations involve more than one unknown quantity.
  • We need a way to represent relationships between these two unknowns.
  • For example, the cost of two different items.

🎙️ Imagine you go to a shop to buy pens and notebooks. The total cost depends on the number of pens AND the number of notebooks. Here, we have two unknowns, and a single variable equation just won't cut it. We need two variables to describe such scenarios.

Defining Linear Equations in Two Variables

  • An equation that can be written in the form Ax + By + C = 0.
  • Here, A, B, and C are real numbers, and A and B are not both zero.
  • x and y are the two variables, and their highest power is 1.

🎙️ This is the core definition! A linear equation in two variables, like '2x + 3y = 7', has two unknowns, usually 'x' and 'y', and importantly, neither 'x' nor 'y' is raised to a power higher than 1. When graphed, it forms a straight line, hence 'linear'.

The Standard Form: ax + by + c = 0

  • This is the general form for linear equations in two variables.
  • 'a' is the coefficient of x, 'b' is the coefficient of y.
  • 'c' is the constant term.
  • Remember: 'a' and 'b' cannot both be zero.

🎙️ Just like how we have a standard way to write numbers, this is the standard way to write these equations. It helps us easily identify the parts of the equation: the coefficients 'a' and 'b', and the constant 'c'. Always try to convert your equation into this form.

Identifying Coefficients (a, b, c)

  • Example: Express 3x + 4y = 7 in the form ax + by + c = 0.
  • Rewrite as: 3x + 4y - 7 = 0.
  • Here, a = 3, b = 4, and c = -7.

🎙️ This is a simple but crucial skill. Always make sure all terms are on one side of the equation, with zero on the other, before identifying 'a', 'b', and 'c'. Pay close attention to the signs of the numbers!

What are Solutions of a Linear Equation?

  • A solution is a pair of values (x, y) that satisfies the equation.
  • When you substitute these values into the equation, both sides become equal.
  • For example, in x + y = 5, (2, 3) is a solution because 2 + 3 = 5.

🎙️ Think of a solution as a 'correct answer' for both 'x' and 'y' that makes the equation true. It's not just one number, but a pair of numbers, an ordered pair (x, y), that works perfectly for the given equation.

How to Find Solutions?

  • Choose any arbitrary value for one variable (say, x).
  • Substitute that value into the equation.
  • Solve the resulting linear equation in one variable for the other variable (y).

🎙️ Finding solutions is like a puzzle! You pick a number for 'x', put it into the equation, and then figure out what 'y' has to be to make the equation true. You can also start by choosing a value for 'y' and then solving for 'x'.

Worked Example: Finding Solutions for x + y = 5

  • If x = 0, then 0 + y = 5, so y = 5. Solution: (0, 5).
  • If x = 5, then 5 + y = 5, so y = 0. Solution: (5, 0).
  • If x = 2, then 2 + y = 5, so y = 3. Solution: (2, 3).

🎙️ See how we can find different pairs of numbers that add up to 5? Each pair (0,5), (5,0), (2,3) is a valid solution. You can choose any value for x, and you will always find a corresponding y.

A Key Property: Infinite Solutions

  • Unlike linear equations in one variable, which have one solution...
  • A linear equation in two variables has infinitely many solutions.
  • Every point on the line representing the equation is a solution.

🎙️ This is a very important difference! Because there are countless combinations of 'x' and 'y' that can satisfy the equation, there are infinitely many solutions. This means you can keep finding solutions forever!

Visualizing: The Graph of a Linear Equation

  • Every linear equation in two variables can be represented visually.
  • This visual representation is a straight line on a coordinate plane.
  • Each point on this line corresponds to a solution of the equation.

🎙️ The most exciting part is seeing these equations come to life! When we plot the solutions of a linear equation, they don't scatter randomly. They all fall perfectly on a single straight line. This is why they are called 'linear' equations!

Why Do We Graph Equations?

  • Graphs provide a clear visual understanding of the relationship between variables.
  • It helps us see all the infinite solutions at once.
  • We can easily identify points that are or are not solutions.

🎙️ Graphs are powerful tools! Instead of listing solutions one by one, a graph shows us the entire set of solutions as a continuous line. Any point on that line is a solution, and any point not on the line is not a solution.

Steps to Graph a Linear Equation

  • Step 1: Find at least two solutions (x, y) for the equation.
  • Step 2: Plot these ordered pairs on a Cartesian plane.
  • Step 3: Draw a straight line passing through these plotted points.

🎙️ Follow these three simple steps to graph any linear equation. While two points are enough to draw a straight line, finding a third point can act as a check to ensure your calculations are correct and your line is accurate.

Worked Example: Graphing x + y = 5

  • Solutions we found: (0, 5), (5, 0), (2, 3).
  • Plot (0, 5) on the y-axis, (5, 0) on the x-axis, and (2, 3) in the first quadrant.
  • Connect these points with a straight line and extend it in both directions.

🎙️ Let's put our steps into action! We already found three solutions for x + y = 5. Now, imagine a graph paper. We'd mark these three points and then carefully draw a straight line through them. That line is the graph of our equation.

Special Points: Intercepts

  • The point where the line crosses the x-axis is the x-intercept (y=0).
  • The point where the line crosses the y-axis is the y-intercept (x=0).
  • Finding intercepts is often the easiest way to get two points for graphing.

🎙️ Intercepts are like landmarks on your graph. They are very easy to find: just set one variable to zero and solve for the other. These two points are usually distinct and provide a good base for drawing your line.

Lines Parallel to Axes: x = k and y = k

  • An equation of the form x = k (where k is a constant) represents a vertical line.
  • This line is parallel to the y-axis.
  • An equation of the form y = k represents a horizontal line, parallel to the x-axis.

🎙️ These are special cases! When an equation only has 'x' (like x=3), it means 'x' is always 3, no matter what 'y' is. This creates a vertical line. Similarly, y=k creates a horizontal line. These are still linear equations in two variables, just with one coefficient being zero.

Worked Example: Graphing x = 3 and y = -2

  • For x = 3: All points have x-coordinate 3, e.g., (3,0), (3,1), (3,-2).
  • This forms a vertical line passing through (3,0) on the x-axis.
  • For y = -2: All points have y-coordinate -2, e.g., (0,-2), (1,-2), (-3,-2).
  • This forms a horizontal line passing through (0,-2) on the y-axis.

🎙️ These are the easiest lines to graph! For x=3, just draw a straight vertical line through the point 3 on the x-axis. For y=-2, draw a straight horizontal line through the point -2 on the y-axis. Simple and quick!

Chapter Recap: Linear Equations in Two Variables

  • An equation of the form ax + by + c = 0 (a,b not both zero).
  • It has infinitely many solutions, each represented by an ordered pair (x, y).
  • The graph of a linear equation is always a straight line.
  • We can easily find solutions and graph equations by plotting points.

🎙️ We've covered a lot today! Remember the standard form, the concept of infinite solutions, and how to represent these equations graphically as straight lines. Practice finding solutions and drawing graphs, and you'll master this chapter in no time. Keep exploring!

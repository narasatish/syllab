Catenation: Carbon's Self-Linking Ability

  • Forms long chains, branches, rings.
  • Carbon-Carbon bonds are strong.
  • Leads to vast number of compounds.

🎙️ Catenation is a superpower of carbon! It can link with other carbon atoms to form incredibly long chains, branched structures, and even closed rings. This property is why we have so many carbon compounds.

Tetravalency of Carbon

  • Carbon has 4 valence electrons.
  • Forms 4 covalent bonds.
  • Can bond with C, H, O, N, S, etc.

🎙️ Carbon's atomic number is 6, so its electronic configuration is 2, 4. This means it has four valence electrons. To achieve stability, it needs to form four covalent bonds, making it tetravalent. Think of it like having four hands to hold onto other atoms!

Saturated Hydrocarbons (Alkanes)

  • Contain only single C-C bonds.
  • General formula: CnH2n+2.
  • Examples: Methane, Ethane, Propane.

🎙️ Saturated hydrocarbons, also known as alkanes, are like compounds where all carbon atoms are holding hands with single bonds. They are very stable. Methane, the main component of natural gas, is a simple alkane.

Unsaturated Hydrocarbons (Alkenes & Alkynes)

  • Contain double or triple C-C bonds.
  • Alkenes (double bond): CnH2n.
  • Alkynes (triple bond): CnH2n-2.

🎙️ Unsaturated hydrocarbons have at least one double or triple bond between carbon atoms. Alkenes have double bonds, like ethene, used to ripen fruits. Alkynes have triple bonds, like ethyne, used in welding.

Aldehydes and Ketones

  • Contain the carbonyl group (C=O).
  • Aldehydes: -CHO at end of chain.
  • Ketones: C=O in middle of chain.

🎙️ Both aldehydes and ketones contain the carbonyl group. The difference lies in its position: aldehydes have it at the end of the carbon chain, while ketones have it in the middle. Formaldehyde is an aldehyde, and acetone is a ketone.

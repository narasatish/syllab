What are Chemical Reactions?

  • Processes that change substances into new ones
  • New substances have different properties
  • Happens all around us, every day!

🎙️ Think about how food digests or how iron rusts. These are all examples of chemical reactions where original substances transform into completely new ones with different characteristics.

Chemical vs. Physical Change

  • Chemical Change: New substance forms, Irreversible (mostly)
  • Physical Change: No new substance, Reversible (mostly)

🎙️ Remember from previous classes, a physical change only alters appearance, like melting ice. A chemical change, however, creates something entirely new, like burning wood into ash.

  • We look for observable changes

Characteristic 1: Change in State and Color

  • Change in State: Reactants might be liquid, product might be gas
  • Change in Color: Original color disappears, new color appears.

🎙️ When you burn a candle, solid wax melts (physical) but then reacts with oxygen to form carbon dioxide gas and water vapor (chemical). Also, think about how silver tarnishes and turns black – a color change due to a chemical reaction.

  • Change in Temperature: Reaction can release (exothermic) or absorb (endothermic) heat.

🎙️ When you drop an antacid tablet in water, you see bubbles – that's gas evolving. And sometimes, a reaction makes things hot, like burning fuel, or cold, like instant cold packs.

Balancing Equations: Why?

  • Law of Conservation of Mass
  • Mass can neither be created nor destroyed
  • Total mass of reactants = Total mass of products
  • Number of atoms of each element must be same on both sides.

🎙️ This is a fundamental law in chemistry. It means that during a reaction, atoms are only rearranged, not lost or gained. So, we must ensure the number of atoms of each element is the same on both sides of the equation.

⚖️
Mass must be equal!

Balancing Equations: Step 1

  • Write the unbalanced equation
  • List atoms of each element on both sides
  • Start balancing with the most complex compound.

🎙️ We'll use a method called 'Hit and Trial'. First, write down the equation and count how many atoms of each element are present on the reactant and product sides.

Balancing Example: Fe + H₂O → Fe₃O₄ + H₂

  • Reactants: Fe=1, H=2, O=1
  • Products: Fe=3, H=2, O=4
  • Balance Oxygen first: Multiply H₂O by 4.

🎙️ Let's take this example. We see that Oxygen atoms are 1 on the left and 4 on the right. So, we put a '4' in front of H₂O on the reactant side. This changes the H count too.

🧪🧪🧪🧪
Adding coefficients
14 / 25

Balancing Example: Fe + 4H₂O → Fe₃O₄ + H₂

  • Now: Reactants: Fe=1, H=8, O=4
  • Products: Fe=3, H=2, O=4
  • Balance Fe: Multiply Fe by 3. Balance H: Multiply H₂ by 4.

🎙️ After balancing oxygen, we now have 8 Hydrogen atoms on the left and 2 on the right. We also have 1 Iron on the left and 3 on the right. So, we adjust the coefficients for Fe and H₂ to make them equal. The final balanced equation is: 3Fe + 4H₂O → Fe₃O₄ + 4H₂.

Combination Reaction

  • Two or more reactants combine to form a single product
  • A + B → AB
  • Example: Burning of coal (C + O₂ → CO₂).

🎙️ It's like two friends meeting and becoming one team. For instance, when carbon burns in oxygen, they combine to form carbon dioxide gas.

➕➕
Joining together
17 / 25

Decomposition Reaction

  • A single reactant breaks down into two or more simpler products
  • AB → A + B
  • Example: Heating limestone (CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂).

🎙️ This is the opposite of combination. One big molecule breaks into smaller pieces. Heating calcium carbonate (limestone) is a common example, producing calcium oxide and carbon dioxide.

➖➖➖
Breaking apart
18 / 25

Displacement Reaction

  • A more reactive element displaces a less reactive element from its compound
  • A + BC → AC + B
  • Example: Iron nail in copper sulphate solution (Fe + CuSO₄ → FeSO₄ + Cu).

🎙️ Imagine a strong person replacing a weaker person in a game. Here, a more reactive metal like iron pushes out a less reactive metal like copper from its salt solution.

↔️
One takes another's place

Double Displacement Reaction

  • Two compounds exchange ions to form two new compounds
  • AB + CD → AD + CB
  • Example: Barium chloride + Sodium sulphate (BaCl₂ + Na₂SO₄ → BaSO₄ + 2NaCl).

🎙️ In this reaction, it's like two dance partners swapping! The positive ions of two compounds exchange places with each other to form two entirely new compounds.

Oxidation and Reduction

  • Oxidation: Gain of Oxygen OR Loss of Hydrogen OR Loss of Electrons
  • Reduction: Loss of Oxygen OR Gain of Hydrogen OR Gain of Electrons
  • Both happen simultaneously.

🎙️ These are very important concepts. Think of them as opposite processes that always occur together. If one substance gains oxygen, another must lose it, or vice-versa with hydrogen or electrons.

Real-life Impact: Corrosion

  • Degradation of metals due to reaction with air, moisture, or chemicals
  • Example: Rusting of iron (Fe + O₂ + H₂O → hydrated iron(III) oxide)
  • Causes damage to bridges, vehicles, etc.

🎙️ You've seen iron gates turn reddish-brown. That's rusting, a type of corrosion where iron reacts with oxygen and water. It's a huge problem, causing significant economic losses.

⚙️

Real-life Impact: Rancidity

  • Oxidation of fats and oils in food
  • Leads to bad smell and taste
  • Example: Spoiling of chips, butter
  • Prevention: Add antioxidants, store in airtight containers, flush with nitrogen gas.

🎙️ Have you ever noticed how old chips or butter taste and smell bad? That's rancidity! The fats and oils react with oxygen in the air. We use methods like packaging with nitrogen to prevent this.

What is Heredity?

  • Heredity is the passing of traits from parents to their offspring.
  • These traits can be physical, like eye colour or height, or even certain behaviours.
  • It's why children often resemble their mother or father.

🎙️ Heredity is a fundamental biological process. Think of it as a biological blueprint being passed down. For example, if both your parents have curly hair, there's a good chance you might also have curly hair. This passing of characteristics is what we call heredity.

Understanding Variation

  • Variation refers to the differences among individuals of the same species.
  • Even siblings, born to the same parents, show some differences.
  • These differences can be in appearance, behaviour, or other characteristics.

🎙️ While heredity ensures traits are passed on, variation ensures that no two individuals are exactly alike, except identical twins. Look around your classroom; everyone has unique features. This uniqueness is due to variation, which is crucial for the survival and evolution of species.

Why is Variation Important?

  • Variation is essential for the survival of a species.
  • It allows species to adapt to changing environments.
  • It forms the basis for evolution, leading to new species over time.

🎙️ Imagine a sudden change in climate. If all individuals in a species were identical, they might all perish. But with variation, some individuals might possess traits that allow them to survive the change, ensuring the species continues. This adaptability is critical for long-term survival and drives the process of evolution.

Inherited Traits

  • Inherited traits are characteristics passed from parents to offspring through genes.
  • These traits are present from birth and are part of our genetic makeup.
  • Examples include eye colour, hair type, blood group, and natural height.

🎙️ Inherited traits are like the default settings we get from our parents. You didn't choose your eye colour or your blood group; these were determined by the genes you received. For instance, a child often inherits a tendency for a certain height range from their parents.

Acquired Traits

  • Acquired traits are characteristics that an individual develops during their lifetime.
  • These traits are not passed down to offspring.
  • They are influenced by environment, lifestyle, or learning.

🎙️ Acquired traits are things we gain or learn. Learning to ride a bicycle, developing strong muscles from exercise, or getting a scar from an injury are all acquired traits. Your children won't automatically know how to ride a bike just because you do; they'll have to learn it themselves.

Inherited vs. Acquired Traits: A Comparison

  • Inherited: Passed through genes, present from birth, e.g., eye colour.
  • Acquired: Developed during life, not genetic, e.g., learning to read.
  • Only inherited traits contribute to the next generation's genetic pool.

🎙️ It's crucial to distinguish between these two types of traits. Inherited traits are part of our genetic blueprint, while acquired traits are a result of our interactions with the world. Only inherited traits can be passed on to your children, shaping their genetic potential.

Introduction to Genetics

  • Genetics is the branch of biology that studies heredity and variation.
  • It explores how traits are passed from one generation to the next.
  • It also investigates the mechanisms that lead to differences among individuals.

🎙️ Genetics is the scientific field that ties all these concepts together. It delves deep into the molecular basis of heredity, studying genes and their role. Understanding genetics helps us comprehend many biological phenomena, from diseases to evolution.

Gregor Mendel: The Father of Genetics

  • Gregor Mendel was an Austrian monk and scientist.
  • He conducted groundbreaking experiments with pea plants in the 19th century.
  • His work laid the foundation for modern genetics.

🎙️ Before Mendel, people knew traits were inherited, but the mechanisms were a mystery. Mendel, through careful and systematic experiments, uncovered the fundamental laws of heredity. His meticulous approach and quantitative analysis were revolutionary for his time.

Mendel's Pea Plant Experiments

  • Mendel chose garden pea plants (Pisum sativum) for his experiments.
  • Pea plants have several easily observable contrasting traits.
  • They are also easy to grow and can self-pollinate or cross-pollinate.

🎙️ Mendel's choice of pea plants was brilliant. Their distinct traits, short life cycle, and ease of controlled breeding made them an ideal model organism. This allowed him to track specific characteristics through multiple generations accurately.

Mendel's Seven Pairs of Contrasting Traits

  • Mendel studied seven pairs of distinct traits in pea plants.
  • Examples: Tall vs. Dwarf, Round vs. Wrinkled seeds, Yellow vs. Green seeds.
  • These clear differences helped him identify patterns of inheritance.

🎙️ Mendel focused on traits that had only two clear, opposing forms, like a plant being either tall or dwarf, with no in-between. This simplified his analysis greatly. He studied seed shape, seed color, pod shape, pod color, flower color, flower position, and stem height.

Genes and Alleles

  • A gene is a segment of DNA that carries information for a specific trait.
  • Alleles are different forms of the same gene.
  • For example, the gene for height has two alleles: one for tallness and one for dwarfness.

🎙️ Think of a gene as a 'trait instruction manual'. For each instruction, there can be different versions, which are the alleles. You inherit one allele for each gene from your mother and one from your father, determining your specific traits.

Dominant and Recessive Traits

  • A dominant allele expresses its trait even when only one copy is present.
  • A recessive allele expresses its trait only when two copies are present.
  • In pea plants, tallness is dominant over dwarfness.

🎙️ Dominant traits are like the 'bossy' ones; they show up if they are present. Recessive traits are 'shy'; they only appear if there's no dominant allele to overshadow them. This concept is key to understanding how traits appear or disappear across generations.

Genotype and Phenotype

  • Genotype: The genetic makeup of an organism, the set of alleles it possesses.
  • Phenotype: The observable physical characteristics or traits of an organism.
  • Genotype determines phenotype, but environmental factors can also influence phenotype.

🎙️ Genotype is like the hidden code, the letters representing the alleles (e.g., TT, Tt, tt). Phenotype is what you actually see (e.g., Tall plant, Dwarf plant). Two individuals can have the same phenotype (tall) but different genotypes (TT or Tt).

Monohybrid Cross - Part 1: Parental (P) Generation

  • A monohybrid cross involves studying the inheritance of a single trait.
  • Mendel started with true-breeding parent plants (P generation).
  • He crossed a pure tall plant (TT) with a pure dwarf plant (tt).

🎙️ In a monohybrid cross, we focus on just one characteristic, like height. The parental generation consists of two pure-bred individuals that are different for that trait. 'True-breeding' means they produce offspring identical to themselves when self-pollinated.

Monohybrid Cross - Part 2: First Filial (F1) Generation

  • The offspring of the P generation are called the First Filial (F1) generation.
  • Mendel found all F1 plants were tall, even though one parent was dwarf.
  • This showed that tallness was dominant over dwarfness.

🎙️ When Mendel crossed the pure tall and pure dwarf plants, every single plant in the F1 generation was tall. This was surprising because the dwarf trait seemed to disappear. However, the dwarf allele was still present, just hidden by the dominant tall allele.

Monohybrid Cross - Part 3: Second Filial (F2) Generation

  • Mendel then self-pollinated the F1 plants to produce the F2 generation.
  • In F2, he observed both tall and dwarf plants in a 3:1 ratio.
  • This proved that the recessive dwarf trait reappeared.

🎙️ The reappearance of the dwarf trait in the F2 generation was crucial. It confirmed that the dwarf allele wasn't destroyed in F1, but merely suppressed. The 3:1 phenotypic ratio (3 tall: 1 dwarf) is a hallmark of a monohybrid cross involving dominant and recessive alleles.

The Punnett Square

  • A Punnett Square is a diagram used to predict the outcome of a genetic cross.
  • It shows all possible combinations of alleles from the parents.
  • Helps visualize the probabilities of different genotypes and phenotypes.

🎙️ The Punnett square is a simple yet powerful tool. It's like a chessboard where you place the possible gametes (sperm/egg cells) from each parent along the top and side. Filling in the squares then shows all the possible genetic combinations for the offspring.

Example: Monohybrid Cross using Punnett Square

  • Cross: Heterozygous Tall (Tt) x Heterozygous Tall (Tt).
  • Possible gametes from Tt: T, t.
  • Results: 1 TT (Tall), 2 Tt (Tall), 1 tt (Dwarf).

🎙️ Let's apply the Punnett Square to our F1 self-cross. One parent contributes T or t, the other also contributes T or t. Combining them gives us TT, Tt, Tt, and tt. This leads to a genotypic ratio of 1:2:1 and a phenotypic ratio of 3:1 (Tall:Dwarf).

Dihybrid Cross: Inheritance of Two Traits

  • A dihybrid cross studies the inheritance of two different traits simultaneously.
  • Mendel crossed plants with two contrasting pairs, e.g., seed shape and seed colour.
  • This led to his Law of Independent Assortment.

🎙️ While a monohybrid cross looks at one trait, a dihybrid cross considers two at once, like seed shape (round/wrinkled) and seed color (yellow/green). This more complex cross helped Mendel understand that traits are inherited independently of each other, not always as a package.

Sex Determination in Humans

  • In humans, sex is determined by sex chromosomes: XX for female, XY for male.
  • Females produce only X gametes; males produce X and Y gametes.
  • The sperm (X or Y) from the father determines the sex of the child.

🎙️ Human cells have 23 pairs of chromosomes. 22 pairs are autosomes, and one pair is sex chromosomes. Females have two X chromosomes (XX), while males have one X and one Y chromosome (XY). This means the father's sperm is the deciding factor in whether a child is born male or female.

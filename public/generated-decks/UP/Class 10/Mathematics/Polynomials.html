What are Polynomials?

  • An expression consisting of variables (like x) and coefficients (numbers).
  • Involves addition, subtraction, multiplication, and non-negative integer exponents of variables.
  • Example: 3x² + 2x - 5

🎙️ Think of polynomials as special algebraic expressions. They are built using variables, coefficients, and basic operations, but with a strict rule: no negative or fractional powers for the variables.

Understanding Terms

  • Each part of a polynomial separated by a '+' or '-' sign is called a term.
  • Example: In 3x² + 5x - 2, the terms are 3x², 5x, and -2.

🎙️ Just like a puzzle is made of different pieces, a polynomial is made of terms. Each term is a product of a number and one or more variables raised to a non-negative integer power.

Coefficients and Variables

  • Coefficient: The numerical factor multiplying the variable part of a term.
  • Variable: The letter (like x, y, z) representing an unknown value.
  • Example: In 5x², '5' is the coefficient and 'x' is the variable.

🎙️ It's crucial to distinguish between coefficients and variables. Coefficients tell us 'how many' of the variable part we have, while variables represent quantities that can change or are unknown.

What is the Degree?

  • The highest power of the variable in a polynomial is called its degree.
  • It helps us classify polynomials.
  • Example: The degree of 4x³ - 2x + 7 is 3.

🎙️ The degree is like the 'rank' of the polynomial. To find it, look at all the terms and identify the term with the highest exponent on its variable. That exponent is the degree.

What are Zeroes of a Polynomial?

  • A zero of a polynomial P(x) is a value 'k' such that P(k) = 0.
  • It's the value of the variable that makes the entire polynomial equal to zero.
  • Also known as 'roots' of the polynomial equation.

🎙️ Finding the zeroes of a polynomial is a very important skill. It means finding the specific input value that results in an output of zero for that polynomial. We'll learn how to find them.

Finding Zeroes: Linear Polynomial

  • To find the zero of a linear polynomial P(x) = ax + b, set P(x) = 0.
  • Example: For P(x) = 2x - 6, set 2x - 6 = 0.
  • Solving for x: 2x = 6, so x = 3. The zero is 3.

🎙️ Finding zeroes for linear polynomials is straightforward. You simply set the expression equal to zero and solve for the variable using basic algebraic operations. Always remember to check your answer!

1Set P(x) = 0
2Isolate the variable term
3Solve for x

Finding Zeroes: Quadratic (Factorization)

  • For P(x) = x² - 5x + 6, factorize the polynomial.
  • Factorization: (x - 2)(x - 3).
  • Set each factor to zero: x - 2 = 0 (x=2) and x - 3 = 0 (x=3). Zeroes are 2 and 3.

🎙️ Factorization is a powerful method for finding zeroes of quadratic polynomials. Once you factorize it into two linear factors, finding the zeroes becomes as simple as solving two linear equations.

1Factorize the polynomial
2Set each factor to zero
3Solve for x to find zeroes

Geometric Meaning of Zeroes

  • On a graph, the zeroes of a polynomial are the x-coordinates where its graph intersects the x-axis.
  • A linear polynomial (degree 1) has at most one zero.
  • A quadratic polynomial (degree 2) has at most two zeroes.

🎙️ Visualizing zeroes on a graph is very intuitive. Whenever the graph of a polynomial crosses or touches the x-axis, that point's x-coordinate is a zero of the polynomial. This helps in understanding the number of zeroes.

Zeroes & Coefficients: Linear

  • For a linear polynomial P(x) = ax + b (where a ≠ 0).
  • The zero is given by the formula: x = -b/a.
  • Example: For 2x + 6, the zero is -6/2 = -3.

🎙️ There's a direct and simple relationship between the zero of a linear polynomial and its coefficients. This formula allows you to find the zero without explicitly solving the equation.

🔗
Direct relationship

Zeroes & Coefficients: Quadratic

  • For a quadratic polynomial P(x) = ax² + bx + c (where a ≠ 0).
  • Sum of zeroes (α + β) = -b/a.
  • Product of zeroes (α × β) = c/a.

🎙️ This is a very important relationship for quadratic polynomials! It connects the zeroes (alpha and beta) directly to the coefficients of the polynomial. You can find the sum and product of zeroes without even finding the individual zeroes.

🤝
Sum and product

Example: Zeroes & Coefficients (Quadratic)

  • Consider P(x) = x² - 5x + 6. Here a=1, b=-5, c=6.
  • We found zeroes are 2 and 3.
  • Sum of zeroes = 2 + 3 = 5. Using formula: -b/a = -(-5)/1 = 5. (Matches!)
  • Product of zeroes = 2 × 3 = 6. Using formula: c/a = 6/1 = 6. (Matches!)

🎙️ Let's put the relationship to the test with an example. We calculate the sum and product of zeroes both directly and using the formulas, and see that they perfectly match. This confirms the validity of the relationships.

1Identify a, b, c
2Calculate sum/product from zeroes
3Calculate sum/product from formulas
4Verify they match

Division Algorithm for Polynomials

  • Just like numbers, polynomials can also be divided.
  • Dividend = Divisor × Quotient + Remainder.
  • P(x) = G(x) × Q(x) + R(x), where degree of R(x) < degree of G(x).

🎙️ The division algorithm for polynomials is very similar to the one we use for integers. It states that when you divide one polynomial P(x) by another G(x), you get a quotient Q(x) and a remainder R(x).

Real-Life: Sharing Resources

  • Imagine you have P(x) number of candies (a polynomial representing quantity).
  • You want to share them equally among G(x) friends.
  • Q(x) would be the number of candies each friend gets, and R(x) would be the leftover candies.

🎙️ This simple analogy helps understand polynomial division. Even if the quantities are represented by polynomials, the concept of sharing and having a remainder remains the same. The remainder will always be 'smaller' than the divisor.

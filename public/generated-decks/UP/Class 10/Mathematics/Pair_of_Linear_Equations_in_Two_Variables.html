What is a Linear Equation?

  • Variables have power 1.
  • No x², y³, or xy terms.
  • Graph is always a straight line.

🎙️ The word 'linear' comes from 'line'. In a linear equation, the highest power of any variable is always one. This means you won't see terms like x-squared or y-cubed. When you plot a linear equation on a graph, it always forms a straight line.

What is a Pair of Linear Equations?

  • Two linear equations.
  • In the same two variables.
  • Solved together to find common solution.

🎙️ Since a single linear equation in two variables has many solutions, to find a unique solution for both 'x' and 'y', we usually need another piece of information. This extra information comes in the form of a second linear equation. When we have two such equations, we call it a 'pair of linear equations'.

  • Our equation: 2x + 3y = 60.

🎙️ Continuing our stationery shop example: your friend also buys some items. They buy 3 pens and 1 notebook for ₹45. Now we have two equations: 2x + 3y = 60 (your purchase) and 3x + y = 45 (your friend's purchase). This is a pair of linear equations, and we can now find the unique cost of one pen and one notebook.

General Form of a Pair of Linear Equations

  • Equation 1: a₁x + b₁y + c₁ = 0
  • Equation 2: a₂x + b₂y + c₂ = 0
  • a, b, c are real numbers, a, b not both zero.

🎙️ Mathematicians like to write things in a standard way. The general form for a pair of linear equations in two variables (x and y) is shown here. Here, a₁, b₁, c₁, a₂, b₂, c₂ are real numbers, and importantly, 'a' and 'b' cannot both be zero in either equation, otherwise, it wouldn't be a two-variable equation.

What are Solutions to a Linear Equation?

🎙️ A solution to a linear equation means finding values for 'x' and 'y' that make the equation a true statement. Geometrically, each solution (x, y) represents a point that lies on the straight line formed by the equation when plotted on a graph.

Solving Pairs: Graphical Method - Introduction

  • Plot both equations as lines.
  • Look for points of intersection.
  • Visual way to find solutions.

🎙️ One powerful way to understand and solve a pair of linear equations is by graphing them. Each equation represents a straight line. The solution to the pair of equations is the point where these two lines meet or intersect. Let's see the different possibilities.

Graphical Method - Case 1: Intersecting Lines

  • Lines cross at one unique point.
  • This point is the unique solution.
  • Condition: a₁/a₂ ≠ b₁/b₂.

🎙️ If the two lines representing the equations cross each other at exactly one point, then that point (x, y) is the unique solution to the pair of equations. This means there is only one set of values for x and y that satisfies both equations. We can identify this case when the ratio of coefficients a₁/a₂ is not equal to b₁/b₂.

Real-Life Example: Unique Solution (Intersecting)

  • Cost of 2 pens + 3 notebooks = ₹60
  • Cost of 3 pens + 1 notebook = ₹45
  • Solution: (x=₹12, y=₹12) - one pen costs ₹12, one notebook costs ₹12.

🎙️ Let's revisit our stationery example. If we graph 2x + 3y = 60 and 3x + y = 45, they would intersect at one specific point. This point would tell us the exact price of one pen and one notebook. In this case, it turns out one pen costs ₹12 and one notebook costs ₹12, which is a unique solution.

Graphical Method - Case 2: Parallel Lines

  • Lines never meet, run side-by-side.
  • No common point, so no solution.
  • Condition: a₁/a₂ = b₁/b₂ ≠ c₁/c₂.

🎙️ Sometimes, when you graph the two equations, the lines turn out to be parallel. Parallel lines never intersect, no matter how far you extend them. This means there is no common point (x, y) that satisfies both equations, and therefore, the system has no solution. This happens when the ratio of a coefficients equals the ratio of b coefficients, but not the ratio of c coefficients.

Real-Life Example: No Solution (Parallel)

  • Equation 1: x + y = 5 (total items)
  • Equation 2: x + y = 8 (total items)
  • Impossible to satisfy both at once.

🎙️ Consider this: 'The sum of two numbers is 5' (x + y = 5) and 'The sum of the same two numbers is 8' (x + y = 8). Can both statements be true at the same time? No! If you graph these, you'll get two parallel lines. There's no pair of numbers that adds up to both 5 and 8 simultaneously.

Graphical Method - Case 3: Coincident Lines

  • One line lies exactly on top of the other.
  • Infinitely many common points.
  • Condition: a₁/a₂ = b₁/b₂ = c₁/c₂.

🎙️ The third possibility is that both equations represent the exact same line. When you graph them, one line will perfectly overlap the other. In this case, every point on the line is a common solution. This means there are infinitely many solutions. This occurs when all three ratios of coefficients are equal.

Same Line

Real-Life Example: Infinitely Many Solutions (Coincident)

  • Equation 1: x + y = 10 (total items)
  • Equation 2: 2x + 2y = 20 (same total items, just scaled)
  • Any pair (x,y) that sums to 10 is a solution.

🎙️ If one shop says 'The cost of 1 samosa and 1 tea is ₹10' (x + y = 10) and another shop says 'The cost of 2 samosas and 2 teas is ₹20' (2x + 2y = 20). These are essentially the same condition! Any combination of samosa and tea prices that adds up to ₹10 for one set will satisfy both. There are infinitely many possibilities.

Solving Pairs: Algebraic Methods - Introduction

  • Sometimes graphing is not precise.
  • Algebraic methods give exact solutions.
  • Substitution and Elimination are common methods.

🎙️ While graphical methods give us a good visual understanding, drawing accurate graphs can sometimes be time-consuming or imprecise, especially if the solutions are not whole numbers. That's where algebraic methods come in handy. These methods use mathematical operations to find exact solutions without drawing graphs. We'll look at two main methods: Substitution and Elimination.

Algebraic Method: Substitution - Steps

  • Step 1: Express one variable in terms of the other from one equation.
  • Step 2: Substitute this expression into the second equation.
  • Step 3: Solve the resulting single-variable equation.
  • Step 4: Substitute the value back to find the other variable.

🎙️ The substitution method is like replacing one piece of information with another. First, you pick one equation and rewrite it to express one variable, say 'x', in terms of 'y'. Then, you 'substitute' this expression for 'x' into the *other* equation. This leaves you with an equation with only 'y', which you can solve. Finally, use the 'y' value to find 'x'.

Worked Example: Substitution Method

  • Solve: x + y = 7 (Eq 1), x - y = 1 (Eq 2)
  • From Eq 1: x = 7 - y
  • Substitute into Eq 2: (7 - y) - y = 1 => 7 - 2y = 1 => 2y = 6 => y = 3
  • Substitute y=3 into x = 7 - y: x = 7 - 3 => x = 4
  • Solution: x=4, y=3.

🎙️ Let's try an example. We have x + y = 7 and x - y = 1. From the first equation, we can say x = 7 - y. Now, substitute '7 - y' in place of 'x' in the second equation. This simplifies to 7 - 2y = 1, giving us y = 3. Finally, put y = 3 back into x = 7 - y to get x = 4. So, our solution is x=4, y=3.

Algebraic Method: Elimination - Steps

  • Step 1: Make coefficients of one variable equal (by multiplying equations).
  • Step 2: Add or subtract equations to eliminate that variable.
  • Step 3: Solve the resulting single-variable equation.
  • Step 4: Substitute value back to find the other variable.

🎙️ The elimination method aims to 'eliminate' one of the variables. You do this by making the coefficients of either 'x' or 'y' the same in both equations, usually by multiplying one or both equations by a suitable number. Then, you either add or subtract the equations to get rid of one variable, leaving you with a single-variable equation to solve. The rest is similar to substitution.

Recap: Key Takeaways

  • A pair of linear equations has two equations with two variables.
  • Solutions can be unique (intersecting), none (parallel), or infinite (coincident).
  • Graphical and Algebraic methods (Substitution, Elimination) help solve them.

🎙️ To summarize, we learned about pairs of linear equations, their general form, and how they represent lines on a graph. We saw three possibilities for solutions: a unique solution when lines intersect, no solution when lines are parallel, and infinitely many solutions when lines are coincident. We also introduced algebraic methods like substitution and elimination for finding exact solutions. Keep practicing, and you'll master this chapter!

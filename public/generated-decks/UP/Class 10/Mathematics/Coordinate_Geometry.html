The Cartesian System

  • Developed by René Descartes.
  • Uses two perpendicular lines as reference axes.
  • These axes intersect at a point called the Origin.

🎙️ The foundation of coordinate geometry is the Cartesian System. It's like setting up a grid, with a central point from which all measurements begin.

X-axis and Y-axis

🎙️ The X-axis runs left to right, and the Y-axis runs up and down. Together, they create a framework that allows us to precisely pinpoint any location in a 2D plane.

Coordinates of a Point (x, y)

  • A point's location is given by an ordered pair (x, y).
  • 'x' is the distance from the Y-axis.
  • 'y' is the distance from the X-axis.
  • The origin is (0, 0).

🎙️ Every point has a unique address, written as (x, y). Remember, the 'x' value always comes first, telling us how far left or right, and 'y' tells us how far up or down.

Plotting Points on a Graph

  • Start at the origin (0,0).
  • Move 'x' units horizontally (right for +, left for -).
  • Then move 'y' units vertically (up for +, down for -).
  • Mark the final position.

🎙️ Plotting points is like following directions. You always start at the origin, take your 'x' step, and then your 'y' step to find your exact spot on the graph.

The Distance Formula

  • For two points P(x₁, y₁) and Q(x₂, y₂):
  • Distance PQ = √[(x₂ - x₁)² + (y₂ - y₁)²]
  • Based on the Pythagorean theorem.

🎙️ This formula is derived from the Pythagorean theorem, which you've studied before. It allows us to find the straight-line distance, or the shortest path, between any two points in the coordinate plane.

↔️
Distance between P and Q
10 / 26

Example: Calculate Distance

  • Find the distance between A(2, 3) and B(5, 7).
  • x₁=2, y₁=3, x₂=5, y₂=7
  • Distance = √[(5-2)² + (7-3)²]
  • = √[3² + 4²] = √[9 + 16] = √25 = 5 units.

🎙️ Let's apply the formula. Substitute the coordinates carefully into the equation. You'll see how quickly we can find the distance, which in this case is 5 units.

  • What if a point divides a line segment?
  • It could divide it internally or externally.
  • We'll focus on internal division for now.

🎙️ Sometimes, a point doesn't just lie on a line; it divides it into specific parts. The section formula helps us find the coordinates of such a dividing point.

The Section Formula (Internal Division)

  • If P(x, y) divides the line segment joining A(x₁, y₁) and B(x₂, y₂)
  • in the ratio m₁:m₂ internally, then:
  • x = (m₁x₂ + m₂x₁)/(m₁ + m₂)
  • y = (m₁y₂ + m₂y₁)/(m₁ + m₂)

🎙️ This formula helps us locate the exact coordinates of point P when we know the endpoints of the line segment and the ratio in which P divides it. Remember the pattern of m1 with x2 and m2 with x1.

➗
Ratio Division
13 / 26

Example: Find the Division Point

  • Find the point that divides the line segment joining (1, -3) and (4, 6)
  • in the ratio 1:2 internally.
  • x = (1*4 + 2*1)/(1+2) = 6/3 = 2
  • y = (1*6 + 2*(-3))/(1+2) = (6-6)/3 = 0. The point is (2, 0).

🎙️ Here, m1 is 1 and m2 is 2. Plug these values, along with the coordinates of the endpoints, into the section formula. We find the coordinates of the dividing point to be (2, 0).

Midpoint Formula: The Middle Point

  • A special case of the section formula.
  • When a point divides a line segment into two equal halves.
  • The ratio of division is 1:1.

🎙️ The midpoint is simply the point exactly in the middle of a line segment. It's a very common calculation and can be found by simplifying the section formula when the ratio is 1:1.

🎯
Center Point

The Midpoint Formula

  • For a line segment joining A(x₁, y₁) and B(x₂, y₂):
  • Midpoint M(x, y) is given by:
  • x = (x₁ + x₂)/2
  • y = (y₁ + y₂)/2

🎙️ To find the midpoint, you simply average the x-coordinates and average the y-coordinates. It's a straightforward and frequently used formula.

⚖️
Balance point
16 / 26

Example: Finding the Midpoint

  • Find the midpoint of the line segment joining P(-2, 5) and Q(6, 1).
  • x₁=-2, y₁=5, x₂=6, y₂=1
  • x = (-2 + 6)/2 = 4/2 = 2
  • y = (5 + 1)/2 = 6/2 = 3. The midpoint is (2, 3).

🎙️ Let's quickly calculate the midpoint for these two points. Add the x-coordinates and divide by two, then do the same for the y-coordinates. The midpoint is (2, 3).

Formula for Area of Triangle

  • For a triangle with vertices (x₁, y₁), (x₂, y₂), and (x₃, y₃):
  • Area = 1/2 |x₁(y₂ - y₃) + x₂(y₃ - y₁) + x₃(y₁ - y₂)|
  • The absolute value ensures area is always positive.

🎙️ This formula might look a bit long, but it follows a cyclic pattern. Notice how the indices for y change. Practice will make it easier to remember and apply.

1x1(y2-y3)
2+ x2(y3-y1)
3+ x3(y1-y2)
4Multiply by 1/2

Example: Calculate Triangle Area

  • Find the area of a triangle with vertices (1, 2), (-4, -3), and (4, 1).
  • Area = 1/2 |1(-3-1) + (-4)(1-2) + 4(2-(-3))|
  • = 1/2 |1(-4) - 4(-1) + 4(5)|
  • = 1/2 |-4 + 4 + 20| = 1/2 |20| = 10 square units.

🎙️ Let's plug in the coordinates into the area formula. Be careful with the signs during calculations. The area of this triangle comes out to be 10 square units.

Collinearity: Points on a Line

  • Three or more points are collinear if they lie on the same straight line.
  • If three points are collinear, they cannot form a triangle.
  • Therefore, the area of the triangle formed by them will be zero.

🎙️ Collinearity is an important concept. If you try to form a triangle with points that are already on a straight line, it won't have any 'height', meaning its area will be zero.

Key Terms: Data & Frequency

  • Data: A collection of facts, such as numbers, words, measurements, observations.
  • Raw Data: Data collected in its original, unorganized form.
  • Frequency: The number of times a particular value or item appears in a dataset.

🎙️ Before we dive deeper, let's understand some basic words. Data is simply information – it could be anything from your friends' heights to the number of cars passing your school. When we count how often something appears, that's called frequency.

Types of Data: Grouped vs. Ungrouped

  • Ungrouped Data: Data presented individually, without being categorized into groups.
  • Grouped Data: Data organized into classes or intervals.
  • Choosing the right type depends on the dataset size and purpose.

🎙️ Imagine you have test scores for 5 students versus 500 students. For 5, you can list each score. For 500, listing each one is tedious. That's where grouped data comes in handy, organizing large datasets into manageable chunks.

Ungrouped Frequency Distribution Table

  • Organizes raw data by listing each value and its frequency.
  • Easy to create for smaller datasets.
  • Helps in quickly seeing which values are most common.

🎙️ An ungrouped frequency distribution table is like making a simple count. You list all the unique items, then go through your raw data and make a tally mark for each occurrence. Finally, you count the tally marks to get the frequency.

Grouped Frequency Distribution

  • Data is divided into class intervals (groups).

🎙️ When you have a very wide range of data, like heights of 100 students ranging from 140 cm to 180 cm, listing each height isn't practical. Grouped frequency distribution helps by putting similar values into 'boxes' or 'intervals'.

Class Interval, Size & Limits

  • Class Interval: A range of values (e.g., 0-10, 10-20).
  • Class Limits: The smallest and largest values that can belong to a class (e.g., 0 and 10).
  • Class Size/Width: The difference between the upper and lower class boundaries (e.g., 10-0 = 10).

🎙️ These are crucial terms for grouped data. A class interval defines the range for a group. The class limits are the boundaries of that range, and the class size tells you how wide each group is. We need to be careful with overlapping limits, usually using exclusive intervals like 0-10, 10-20.

Measures of Central Tendency

  • Single values that describe the center of a data set.
  • Represent the 'typical' value in a distribution.
  • The three main measures are Mean, Median, and Mode.

🎙️ Imagine a group of students and you want to know their typical performance. Do you want the average, the middle score, or the most common score? These three measures - Mean, Median, and Mode - help us find that 'center' of our data in different ways.

Mean: The Average

  • The most common measure of central tendency.
  • Calculated by summing all values and dividing by the number of values.
  • Sensitive to extreme values (outliers).

🎙️ The Mean is what you usually think of as the 'average'. It's like balancing all the values on a scale to find the central point. If one value is very high or very low, it can pull the mean significantly in that direction.

⚖️
Balancing Act
12 / 25

Mean for Ungrouped Data

  • Formula: Mean (x̄) = Σx / n
  • Σx = sum of all observations
  • n = total number of observations
  • Example: Scores 5, 7, 6, 8, 9. Mean = (5+7+6+8+9) / 5 = 35/5 = 7

🎙️ If you have 5 candies and your friend has 9, and you want to share them equally, you add them up (14) and divide by 2 (7 each). That's exactly how the mean works for ungrouped data – sum everything and divide by how many items there are.

🍬🍬🍬🍬🍬
Sharing Candies Equally

Mean for Grouped Data: Direct Method

  • Formula: Mean (x̄) = Σfᵢxᵢ / Σfᵢ
  • xᵢ = class mark (mid-point) of each class interval
  • fᵢ = frequency of each class interval
  • Steps: Find class mark, multiply by frequency, sum products, divide by total frequency.

🎙️ For grouped data, we can't use individual values. So, we assume that all values within a class interval are represented by its midpoint, called the class mark. We then multiply this midpoint by its frequency and sum these products to find the mean.

1Find Class Marks (xᵢ)
2Calculate fᵢxᵢ
3Sum fᵢxᵢ and fᵢ
4Divide Sums
14 / 25

Worked Example: Mean (Direct Method)

  • Daily wages of 50 workers:
  • Wages (₹) | No. of Workers (fᵢ) | Class Mark (xᵢ) | fᵢxᵢ
  • 100-120 | 12 | 110 | 1320
  • 120-140 | 14 | 130 | 1820
  • 140-160 | 8 | 150 | 1200
  • 160-180 | 6 | 170 | 1020
  • 180-200 | 10 | 190 | 1900
  • Total Σfᵢ = 50, Σfᵢxᵢ = 7260. Mean = 7260/50 = ₹145.20

🎙️ Here, we see the complete calculation. First, find the midpoint for each wage group. Then, multiply it by the number of workers in that group. Sum up these products and divide by the total number of workers to get the average daily wage.

Median: The Middle Value

  • The middle value of a dataset when arranged in order.
  • If there are two middle values (even number of data points), the median is their average.
  • Less affected by extreme values than the mean.

🎙️ The Median is literally the value that sits right in the middle. Imagine lining up all your data points from smallest to largest. The one in the very center is your median. It's a good measure when you have some unusually high or low values that might skew the mean.

↔️

Median for Ungrouped Data

  • Step 1: Arrange data in ascending or descending order.
  • Step 2: If 'n' is odd, Median = ((n+1)/2)th observation.
  • Step 3: If 'n' is even, Median = average of (n/2)th and ((n/2)+1)th observations.
  • Example: 2, 5, 7, 8, 9 (n=5, odd). Median = (5+1)/2 = 3rd obs = 7.
  • Example: 2, 5, 7, 8 (n=4, even). Median = (5+7)/2 = 6.

🎙️ The key here is to always sort your data first. If you forget to sort, your median will be incorrect. Once sorted, finding the middle is straightforward, whether you have an odd or even number of data points.

2Find Middle Position
3Identify Value

Median for Grouped Data

  • Formula: Median = L + [ (n/2 - cf) / f ] * h
  • L = lower limit of median class
  • n = total frequency
  • cf = cumulative frequency of class preceding median class
  • f = frequency of median class
  • h = class size

🎙️ For grouped data, we first find the 'median class' using the cumulative frequency. Then, we use this formula to pinpoint the exact median value within that class. Cumulative frequency helps us understand how many observations fall below a certain point.

📈
Cumulative Count
18 / 25

Worked Example: Median (Grouped Data)

  • Daily income of 100 families:
  • Income (₹) | No. of Families (f) | Cumulative Freq (cf)
  • 0-50 | 10 | 10
  • 50-100 | 25 | 35
  • 100-150 | 30 | 65 (Median Class)
  • 150-200 | 20 | 85
  • 200-250 | 15 | 100
  • n=100, n/2=50. Median Class is 100-150 (cf=65 > 50). L=100, cf=35, f=30, h=50.
  • Median = 100 + [(50-35)/30] * 50 = 100 + (15/30)*50 = 100 + 25 = ₹125

🎙️ In this example, with 100 families, the median position is the 50th family. Looking at the cumulative frequency, the 50th family falls into the 100-150 income group. Then, we plug in the values into the formula to find the exact median income.

Mode: The Most Frequent

  • The value that appears most often in a dataset.
  • A dataset can have one mode (unimodal), multiple modes (multimodal), or no mode.
  • Useful for qualitative data (e.g., favorite color).

🎙️ The mode is simply the 'most popular' item or value. If you ask students their favorite fruit, the fruit chosen by the most students is the mode. It's the easiest to understand and can even be used when dealing with non-numerical data.

Mode for Ungrouped Data

  • Simply identify the value with the highest frequency.
  • Example: Scores 5, 7, 6, 5, 8, 7, 5, 9, 6, 7.
  • Frequency of 5 is 3. Frequency of 6 is 2. Frequency of 7 is 3. Frequency of 8 is 1. Frequency of 9 is 1.
  • Here, both 5 and 7 appear 3 times. So, the modes are 5 and 7 (bimodal).

🎙️ Let's say these emojis represent the colors of T-shirts students prefer. If blue appears the most, then blue is the mode. In our example, both 5 and 7 are modes because they share the highest frequency.

Mode for Grouped Data

  • Formula: Mode = L + [ (f₁ - f₀) / (2f₁ - f₀ - f₂) ] * h
  • L = lower limit of modal class
  • f₁ = frequency of modal class
  • f₀ = frequency of class preceding modal class
  • f₂ = frequency of class succeeding modal class
  • h = class size

🎙️ For grouped data, we first identify the 'modal class', which is the class interval with the highest frequency. Then, we use this formula to calculate the mode, taking into account the frequencies of the classes before and after the modal class.

👑
The Reigning Class

Worked Example: Mode (Grouped Data)

  • Ages of 100 patients in a hospital:
  • Age (Years) | No. of Patients (f)
  • 5-15 | 6
  • 15-25 | 11
  • 25-35 | 21
  • 35-45 | 23 (Modal Class)
  • 45-55 | 14
  • 55-65 | 5
  • Highest frequency (23) is in class 35-45. So, Modal Class = 35-45. L=35, f₁=23, f₀=21, f₂=14, h=10.
  • Mode = 35 + [(23-21) / (2*23 - 21 - 14)] * 10 = 35 + [2 / (46 - 35)] * 10 = 35 + [2/11] * 10 = 35 + 1.82 = 36.82 years (approx).

🎙️ Here, the class 35-45 has the highest frequency (23 patients), making it our modal class. We then use the frequencies of the classes around it, along with the class limits and size, to calculate the precise mode using the formula.

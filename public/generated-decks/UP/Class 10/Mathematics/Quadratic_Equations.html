What is an Equation?

  • A mathematical statement showing two expressions are equal.
  • It contains an equals sign (=).
  • Often involves variables (like x, y) and constants.
  • Helps us find unknown values.

🎙️ Think of an equation like a balanced scale. Whatever is on one side must be equal to what's on the other side. It's a statement that two mathematical expressions have the same value, usually with a variable we need to solve for.

Types of Equations: Linear vs. Quadratic

  • Equations are classified by the highest power of the variable.
  • Linear equations have a degree of 1 (e.g., 2x + 3 = 0).
  • Quadratic equations have a degree of 2 (e.g., ax² + bx + c = 0).

🎙️ We've already learned about linear equations, where the highest power of the variable is one. Today, we're moving on to quadratic equations, which have the highest power of the variable as two. This difference in degree changes how we solve them and how their graphs look.

What is a Quadratic Polynomial?

  • A polynomial with the highest degree of 2.
  • It contains an x² term.
  • General form: ax² + bx + c, where a ≠ 0.
  • Example: 3x² + 5x - 2.

🎙️ A quadratic polynomial is a polynomial where the highest power of the variable is exactly two. This means it must have an x-squared term. The 'a' coefficient in the general form cannot be zero, otherwise, it would no longer be quadratic.

Defining Quadratic Equations

  • When a quadratic polynomial is set equal to zero.
  • Form: ax² + bx + c = 0.
  • Here, 'a', 'b', and 'c' are real numbers.
  • Crucially, 'a' cannot be zero (a ≠ 0).

🎙️ So, a quadratic equation is simply a quadratic polynomial that has been set equal to zero. The 'a', 'b', and 'c' are coefficients, where 'a' is the coefficient of x², 'b' is the coefficient of x, and 'c' is the constant term. Remember, 'a' must not be zero for it to be a quadratic equation.

Standard Form: ax² + bx + c = 0

  • This is the most common and organized way to write a quadratic equation.
  • 'a' is the coefficient of x² (a ≠ 0).
  • 'b' is the coefficient of x.
  • 'c' is the constant term.

🎙️ The standard form helps us easily identify the coefficients 'a', 'b', and 'c', which are essential for solving the equation. Always try to arrange your quadratic equations into this standard form before attempting to solve them. It makes the process much clearer.

Real-Life Example: Area Calculation

  • Finding dimensions of a rectangular plot.
  • If length is 2m more than width, and area is 120m².
  • Let width = x, length = x + 2.
  • Equation: x(x + 2) = 120 => x² + 2x - 120 = 0.

🎙️ Imagine you have a rectangular piece of land, and you know its area. If the length is related to the width, you can set up a quadratic equation to find the exact dimensions. This is a very practical application of quadratic equations in daily life, especially in construction or gardening.

Real-Life Example: Throwing a Ball

  • The path of a ball thrown upwards is a parabola.
  • Height (h) at time (t) can be given by h = at² + bt + c.
  • Here, 'a' is negative due to gravity.
  • Finding when the ball hits the ground (h=0) involves a quadratic equation.

🎙️ When you throw a ball, it doesn't go straight up and down; it follows a curved path. This path, called a parabola, can be described by a quadratic equation. We use quadratic equations to calculate things like the maximum height the ball reaches or when it will hit the ground.

Solving Quadratic Equations: Factorisation Method

  • This method relies on factoring the quadratic polynomial.
  • If a product of two terms is zero, at least one term must be zero.
  • Steps: Factor the quadratic, set each factor to zero, solve for x.
  • It's often the quickest method when applicable.

🎙️ Factorisation is a powerful method for solving quadratic equations. The core idea is that if you can break down the quadratic expression into two simpler factors, then setting each factor to zero will give you the solutions for x. It's like finding the pieces of a puzzle.

Worked Example: Factorisation

  • Solve: x² - 5x + 6 = 0
  • Find two numbers that multiply to 6 and add to -5.
  • These numbers are -2 and -3.
  • So, (x - 2)(x - 3) = 0. Therefore, x = 2 or x = 3.

🎙️ Let's walk through an example. For x² - 5x + 6 = 0, we look for two numbers that multiply to 6 and add up to -5. Once we find these numbers, which are -2 and -3, we can rewrite the equation and factor it. This directly gives us the values of x.

Solving by Completing the Square

  • A method to transform the quadratic expression into a perfect square trinomial.
  • Allows us to take the square root of both sides.
  • Always works, even when factorisation is difficult or not possible.
  • It's the basis for deriving the quadratic formula.

🎙️ Completing the square is another robust method. The goal is to manipulate the equation so that one side becomes a perfect square trinomial, like (x+k)². This makes it easy to solve by taking the square root. This method is incredibly important as it's the foundation for the quadratic formula.

Worked Example: Completing the Square

  • Solve: x² + 6x + 5 = 0
  • Move constant: x² + 6x = -5
  • Add (6/2)² = 9 to both sides: x² + 6x + 9 = -5 + 9
  • (x + 3)² = 4 => x + 3 = ±2 => x = -3 ± 2
  • Roots are x = -1 and x = -5.

🎙️ Let's apply completing the square to x² + 6x + 5 = 0. First, isolate the x² and x terms. Then, add a specific value to both sides to make the left side a perfect square. This value is always (b/2)². After that, you can take the square root and solve for x.

The Quadratic Formula

  • A universal formula to find the roots of any quadratic equation.
  • Derived from the method of completing the square.
  • Formula: x = [-b ± √(b² - 4ac)] / 2a.
  • Simply substitute 'a', 'b', and 'c' from the standard form.

🎙️ The quadratic formula is a lifesaver! It guarantees a solution for any quadratic equation, regardless of whether it can be factored easily or not. You just need to correctly identify the values of 'a', 'b', and 'c' from the standard form of the equation and plug them into the formula.

Worked Example: Using the Formula

  • Solve: 2x² + 5x + 2 = 0
  • Identify a = 2, b = 5, c = 2.
  • Substitute into formula: x = [-5 ± √(5² - 4*2*2)] / (2*2)
  • x = [-5 ± √(25 - 16)] / 4 = [-5 ± √9] / 4
  • x = [-5 ± 3] / 4. Roots are x = -1/2 and x = -2.

🎙️ Let's apply the quadratic formula to 2x² + 5x + 2 = 0. First, clearly write down your 'a', 'b', and 'c' values. Then, carefully substitute these values into the formula and simplify step-by-step. Pay close attention to the signs and calculations, especially under the square root.

Nature of Roots: The Discriminant (D)

  • The term (b² - 4ac) inside the square root is called the Discriminant (D).
  • D = b² - 4ac.
  • Its value tells us about the nature of the roots (solutions).
  • Roots can be real and distinct, real and equal, or not real.

🎙️ The discriminant, denoted by 'D' or a triangle symbol, is a very important part of the quadratic formula. It's the expression b² - 4ac. By just calculating this value, we can predict what kind of solutions our quadratic equation will have, even before fully solving it.

Cases of Discriminant: D > 0, D = 0, D < 0

  • If D > 0: Two distinct real roots.
  • If D = 0: Two equal real roots (or one repeated root).
  • If D < 0: No real roots (roots are complex/imaginary).
  • This helps verify solutions and understand equation behavior.

🎙️ The sign of the discriminant tells us everything. If it's positive, we get two different real numbers as solutions. If it's zero, both solutions are the same real number. If it's negative, we won't find any real number solutions, which means the roots are complex. This is a powerful concept!

What is a Circle?

  • A circle is a collection of all points in a plane.
  • These points are at a fixed distance from a fixed point.
  • The fixed point is the 'centre', and the fixed distance is the 'radius'.

🎙️ So, mathematically, a circle isn't just a round shape, it's defined by these two crucial elements: a center point and a constant radius. All points satisfying this condition form a circle.

Parts of a Circle: Centre & Radius

  • The 'Centre' (O) is the fixed point inside the circle.
  • The 'Radius' (r) is the fixed distance from the centre to any point on the circle.
  • All radii of the same circle are equal in length.

🎙️ The centre is like the heart of the circle, and the radius is like an arm extending from the heart to the edge. Remember, every arm (radius) has the same length!

Parts of a Circle: Diameter & Chord

  • A 'Diameter' is a chord passing through the centre.
  • It is the longest chord of a circle.
  • Diameter = 2 × Radius (d = 2r).
  • A 'Chord' is a line segment joining any two points on the circle.

🎙️ Think of a chord as any straight line connecting two points on the circle. The special chord that passes right through the middle, the diameter, is the longest one and is always double the radius.

Parts of a Circle: Arc & Segment

  • An 'Arc' is a continuous piece of a circle.
  • It's like a curved part of the circumference.
  • A 'Segment' is the region between a chord and its corresponding arc.

🎙️ Imagine cutting a slice off a round pizza. The crust is an arc, and the cheesy part with the crust is a segment. There's a minor segment (smaller) and a major segment (larger).

Parts of a Circle: Sector

  • A 'Sector' is the region between two radii and their corresponding arc.

  • There can be a minor sector and a major sector.

🎙️ Unlike a segment that uses a chord, a sector uses two radii and the arc between them. Think of how you cut a cake – you're making sectors!

Congruent Circles

🎙️ Congruent means 'exactly the same'. So, if two circles have the same radius, they are congruent. You could perfectly overlap them.

Theorem 1: Chords and Distances from Centre

  • Theorem: Equal chords of a circle (or of congruent circles) are equidistant from the centre.
  • If chord AB = chord CD, then their perpendicular distances from the centre are equal.
  • This means OM = ON if AB = CD.

🎙️ This theorem tells us that if two chords are the same length, they will be the same distance away from the centre of the circle. It's like balancing them out.

⚖️
Balance and equality

Worked Example 1: Applying Theorem 1

  • Given: A circle with centre O, chords AB and CD are equal.
  • OM ⊥ AB and ON ⊥ CD. If OM = 3 cm, find ON.
  • Solution: Since AB = CD, by Theorem 1, their distances from O are equal.
  • Therefore, ON = OM = 3 cm.

🎙️ This example shows a direct application of the theorem. If you know one distance and that the chords are equal, you automatically know the other distance. Simple, right?

Theorem 2: Perpendicular from Centre to Chord

  • Theorem: The perpendicular from the centre of a circle to a chord bisects the chord.
  • If OM ⊥ AB, then AM = MB.
  • This means the perpendicular cuts the chord into two equal halves.

🎙️ This is a very useful theorem. If you drop a perpendicular line from the centre onto any chord, it will always divide that chord into two perfectly equal parts. It's like cutting a rope precisely in the middle.

✂️
Cutting in half

Worked Example 2: Applying Theorem 2

  • Given: A chord AB of length 8 cm, perpendicular distance from centre O to AB is 3 cm.
  • Find the radius of the circle.
  • Solution: By Theorem 2, AM = MB = 8/2 = 4 cm.
  • In ΔOMA, using Pythagoras: OA² = OM² + AM² = 3² + 4² = 9 + 16 = 25.
  • So, OA (radius) = √25 = 5 cm.

🎙️ This example combines Theorem 2 with the Pythagoras theorem, which you already know. Many circle problems involve using right-angled triangles to find unknown lengths.

Angles Subtended by a Chord at the Centre

  • A chord AB subtends an angle ∠AOB at the centre O.
  • This angle is formed by connecting the endpoints of the chord to the centre.
  • The size of this angle depends on the length of the chord.

🎙️ Every chord in a circle creates an angle at the centre. Imagine holding two ends of a string (the chord) and pulling them towards the centre. The angle formed at your hand is the angle subtended at the centre.

Theorem 3: Equal Chords Subtend Equal Angles at Centre

  • Theorem: Equal chords of a circle (or of congruent circles) subtend equal angles at the centre.
  • If chord AB = chord CD, then ∠AOB = ∠COD.
  • The converse is also true: If angles subtended by chords at the centre are equal, then the chords are equal.

🎙️ This theorem makes sense intuitively. If two chords are the same length, they should 'open up' the same amount at the centre, creating equal angles.

🤝
Equality

Worked Example 3: Applying Theorem 3

  • Given: Chords PQ and RS are equal in a circle with centre O.
  • If ∠POQ = 70°, find ∠ROS.
  • Solution: Since chords PQ = RS, by Theorem 3, the angles subtended by them at the centre are equal.
  • Therefore, ∠ROS = ∠POQ = 70°.

🎙️ This is a straightforward application. If you know two chords are equal, you immediately know the angles they form at the centre are also equal.

Angles Subtended by an Arc: Centre vs. Circumference

  • An arc also subtends angles.
  • It subtends an angle at the centre (like a chord does).
  • It also subtends an angle at any point on the remaining part of the circle (on the circumference).

🎙️ Imagine you're looking at a curved segment of a road (the arc). If you stand in the middle of the road (the centre), you see a certain angle. If you stand on the side of the road further away (on the circumference), you see a different, smaller angle.

Theorem 4: Central Angle is Double

  • Theorem: The angle subtended by an arc at the centre is double the angle subtended by it at any point on the remaining part of the circle.
  • If arc AB subtends ∠AOB at the centre and ∠APB on the remaining part.
  • Then, ∠AOB = 2 × ∠APB.

🎙️ This is a very important theorem! The angle you see from the centre is always twice as large as the angle you see from any point on the edge of the circle, as long as they are subtended by the same arc.

✌️
Twice the angle

Worked Example 4: Applying Theorem 4

  • Given: In a circle with centre O, arc PQ subtends ∠POQ at the centre and ∠PRQ at a point R on the remaining part.
  • If ∠PRQ = 40°, find ∠POQ.
  • Solution: By Theorem 4, ∠POQ = 2 × ∠PRQ.
  • Therefore, ∠POQ = 2 × 40° = 80°.

🎙️ This example demonstrates how to use the 'central angle is double' theorem directly. It's a fundamental concept for solving angle problems in circles.

Angles in the Same Segment are Equal

  • This is a direct consequence of Theorem 4.
  • Theorem: Angles in the same segment of a circle are equal.
  • If points P and Q are on the same segment, then ∠APB = ∠AQB.
  • Both angles are half of the central angle subtended by arc AB.

🎙️ If multiple points on the circumference see the same arc, they will all see it at the same angle. It's like everyone in the same row at a movie theatre having the same view of the screen.

Angle in a Semicircle is a Right Angle

  • This is another special case of Theorem 4.
  • Theorem: The angle in a semicircle is a right angle (90°).
  • If AB is a diameter, then the arc ACB is a semicircle.
  • Any angle ∠ACB subtended by this semicircle at point C on the circumference will be 90°.

🎙️ This is a very useful property! If you draw a triangle inside a circle such that one side is the diameter, the angle opposite the diameter will always be 90 degrees. This is because the central angle for a semicircle is 180 degrees, and the angle on the circumference is half of that.

Cyclic Quadrilateral

  • A 'cyclic quadrilateral' is a quadrilateral whose all four vertices lie on a circle.
  • The word 'cyclic' means it forms a cycle on the circle.
  • Example: ABCD is a cyclic quadrilateral if A, B, C, D are all on the circle.

🎙️ Just like a triangle can be inscribed in a circle, a quadrilateral can too! The key is that all four corners (vertices) must touch the circle's circumference. If even one point is off, it's not cyclic.

Theorem 5: Opposite Angles of a Cyclic Quadrilateral

  • Theorem: The sum of either pair of opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral is 180°.
  • In cyclic quadrilateral ABCD, ∠A + ∠C = 180°.
  • Also, ∠B + ∠D = 180°.
  • The converse is also true: If the sum of opposite angles is 180°, the quadrilateral is cyclic.

🎙️ This is a powerful property! If you ever see a quadrilateral with all its vertices on a circle, you immediately know that its opposite angles add up to 180 degrees. This is super useful for finding unknown angles.

➕
Sum to 180 degrees

Worked Example 5: Applying Theorem 5

  • Given: ABCD is a cyclic quadrilateral. If ∠A = 80°, find ∠C.
  • Solution: By Theorem 5, the sum of opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral is 180°.
  • So, ∠A + ∠C = 180°.
  • 80° + ∠C = 180°.
  • ∠C = 180° - 80° = 100°.

🎙️ This example shows how to quickly find an unknown angle in a cyclic quadrilateral if you know its opposite angle. It's a direct application of the theorem.

Common Mistakes to Avoid!

  • Confusing radius and diameter (Diameter = 2 × Radius).
  • Mixing up segments and sectors.
  • Incorrectly applying angle theorems (e.g., forgetting 'same arc' or 'opposite angles').
  • Not drawing clear diagrams for problems.

🎙️ It's easy to make small errors in geometry. Always double-check your definitions and theorem conditions. Drawing a clear, labelled diagram is the best way to avoid confusion and visualise the problem correctly.

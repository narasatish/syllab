How Do We Measure Area?

  • Area is measured in square units.
  • Common units: square centimetres (cm²), square metres (m²).
  • Larger areas use hectares or square kilometres (km²).

🎙️ Just like length is measured in metres, area has its own units. Always remember to use the 'square' unit, whether it's square centimetres for small objects or square kilometres for large regions like cities.

Area of a Rectangle

  • A rectangle has length and breadth.
  • Area = Length × Breadth.
  • Example: A rectangle 4 units long and 2 units wide has an area of 8 square units.

🎙️ Let's quickly recall the area of basic shapes. For a rectangle, we simply multiply its two dimensions. Imagine arranging 8 small square tiles to perfectly cover a 4x2 rectangular floor.

Area of a Square

  • A square is a special rectangle where all sides are equal.
  • Area = Side × Side (or Side²).
  • Example: A square with side 3 units has an area of 9 square units.

🎙️ The square is a very common shape. Since its length and breadth are the same, we simply square the length of its side to find its area. This is a fundamental formula.

Congruent Figures Have Equal Areas

  • Congruent figures are identical in shape and size.
  • If two figures are congruent, they will perfectly overlap.
  • Therefore, they must have the same area.

🎙️ This is an important property. If you have two identical cookies, they take up the same space, right? Similarly, in geometry, congruent figures always have equal areas. It's a logical consequence of their identical nature.

Equal Areas Don't Mean Congruent

  • Two figures can have the same area but look different.
  • They might not be congruent.
  • Example: A 4x4 square and an 8x2 rectangle both have an area of 16 sq units.

🎙️ Now, here's a crucial distinction! Just because two shapes have the same area doesn't mean they are identical. They can be very different in appearance, like our square and rectangle example. This concept is key for the theorems we'll learn.

What Does 'Same Base' Mean?

  • Two figures are said to be on the same base if they share a common side.
  • This common side acts as the 'foundation' for both figures.
  • Crucial for comparing areas.

🎙️ When we talk about 'same base', we mean that two or more shapes literally sit on the same line segment. Imagine two houses built side-by-side, sharing a common wall at the ground level. That common wall is their 'same base'.

What Does 'Between Same Parallels' Mean?

  • Two figures are between the same parallels if their bases lie on one parallel line.
  • And the vertices opposite to the base lie on the other parallel line.
  • This ensures a constant 'height' relationship.

🎙️ This condition is about height. If shapes are between the same parallel lines, it means their perpendicular distance (or height) between the base and the opposite vertex is constant. Think of two roads running parallel; anything built between them will have a similar 'spanning' distance.

Theorem 1: Parallelograms of Equal Area

  • Statement: Parallelograms on the same base and between the same parallels are equal in area.
  • This is a fundamental theorem in understanding areas.
  • It implies their 'width' and 'height' relationships are equivalent.

🎙️ This theorem is incredibly useful. It tells us that even if parallelograms look a bit 'slanted' differently, as long as they share a base and are confined by the same parallel lines, their areas will be identical. Think of it as pushing a block sideways; its base and height remain, so its area doesn't change.

📐
Geometry Theorem

Visualizing Theorem 1: Why It's True

  • Imagine two parallelograms, ABCD and ABEF, on base AB.
  • We can 'cut' triangle ADE from the left of ABCD.
  • And 'paste' it as triangle BCF to form ABEF, showing equal areas.

🎙️ The proof of this theorem involves a simple 'cut and paste' method. You can literally see how one parallelogram can be transformed into the other by moving a triangular piece, proving their areas are indeed the same.

Applying Theorem 1: Example

  • Parallelograms PQRS and PQMN are on the same base PQ.
  • They are also between the same parallels PQ and SN.
  • If Area(PQRS) = 25 cm², what is Area(PQMN)?

🎙️ According to Theorem 1, since both parallelograms share the same base PQ and are between the same parallel lines, their areas must be equal. So, if PQRS has an area of 25 cm², then PQMN must also have an area of 25 cm².

Theorem 2: Triangle is Half the Parallelogram

  • Statement: If a triangle and a parallelogram are on the same base and between the same parallels, the area of the triangle is half the area of the parallelogram.
  • This theorem establishes a direct relationship between their areas.
  • The height of both figures is the same.

🎙️ This theorem is a powerful tool. It means that if you have a parallelogram and a triangle that share a base and are 'contained' by the same parallel lines, the triangle will always cover exactly half the area of the parallelogram. This makes calculations much easier!

🔺
Triangle Area

Visualizing Theorem 2: How it Works

  • Draw a parallelogram ABCD and a diagonal AC.
  • Triangle ABC and ADC are congruent, so Area(ABC) = ½ Area(ABCD).
  • Any triangle on base AB with its third vertex on line CD will have the same area as triangle ABC.

🎙️ Think about it: a diagonal divides a parallelogram into two triangles of equal area. If we then consider any triangle with the same base as the parallelogram and its third vertex on the opposite parallel line, its height will be the same as the parallelogram's height, leading to half the area.

✂️

Applying Theorem 2: Example

  • Parallelogram PQRS has an area of 50 cm².
  • Triangle PQT is on the same base PQ and between the same parallels PQ and RS.
  • What is the area of triangle PQT?

🎙️ Using Theorem 2, we know that the area of the triangle is half the area of the parallelogram under these conditions. So, Area(PQT) = ½ * Area(PQRS) = ½ * 50 cm² = 25 cm². Simple, isn't it?

Theorem 3: Triangles of Equal Area

  • Statement: Triangles on the same base and between the same parallels are equal in area.
  • This extends the idea from parallelograms to triangles.
  • Their heights are the same, and they share the same base.

🎙️ This is another very important theorem. It means that you can 'slide' the top vertex of a triangle along a line parallel to its base, and the area of the triangle will not change. This is because both its base and its perpendicular height remain constant.

Visualizing Theorem 3: Connecting the Dots

  • Consider two triangles, ABC and ABD, on the same base AB.
  • Both are between the same parallel lines AB and CD.
  • Each triangle's area is half of a parallelogram formed on the same base and between the same parallels, which are equal in area.

🎙️ We can prove this by using Theorem 2. Each triangle can be seen as half of a parallelogram. Since the parallelograms formed would be on the same base and between the same parallels (and thus have equal areas), it follows that the triangles also have equal areas.

Common Mistakes to Avoid!

  • Confusing congruent figures with figures of equal area.
  • Forgetting the 'between the same parallels' condition.
  • Incorrectly identifying the base or height for a given figure.

🎙️ Students often make these mistakes. Remember, equal area doesn't mean identical shapes. Always check both conditions – same base and same parallels – before applying the theorems. Also, ensure you pick the correct base and corresponding height for any area calculation.

What are Polynomials?

  • A special type of algebraic expression.
  • Variables have only whole number exponents (0, 1, 2, 3...).
  • No negative or fractional exponents for variables.

🎙️ A polynomial is a special type of algebraic expression where the exponents of the variables are always whole numbers. Think of it as a well-behaved algebraic expression compared to others.

Key Components of a Polynomial

  • Terms: Parts separated by + or - signs.
  • Coefficients: The numerical part of a term.
  • Variables: The letters representing unknown values (e.g., x, y).
  • Exponents: The powers of the variables (must be whole numbers).

🎙️ Every polynomial is built from terms, which are separated by plus or minus signs. Each term has a coefficient (a number), a variable (a letter), and an exponent (its power).

Real-Life Polynomials: Area Calculation

  • Area = x² + 5x (a polynomial!).

Degree of a Polynomial

  • The highest exponent of the variable in a polynomial.
  • It determines the 'rank' or 'order' of the polynomial.
  • Example: In 3x⁴ + 2x² - 7, the degree is 4.

🎙️ The degree tells us the highest power of the variable present in any term of the polynomial. For example, in x² + 2x, the highest power is 2, so its degree is 2.

Types by Number of Terms

  • Monomial: A polynomial with only one term (e.g., 5x²).
  • Binomial: A polynomial with two terms (e.g., 2x + 3).
  • Trinomial: A polynomial with three terms (e.g., x² - 4x + 7).

🎙️ We classify polynomials based on how many terms they have. A single term is a monomial, two terms make a binomial, and three terms form a trinomial. This helps us describe them quickly.

Zero of a Polynomial

  • A value 'k' for the variable 'x' such that P(k) = 0.
  • It's the point where the polynomial's graph crosses the x-axis.
  • Also known as a 'root' of the polynomial equation.

🎙️ The zero of a polynomial is a very important concept. It's the specific value of the variable that makes the entire polynomial equal to zero. Think of it as finding the 'root' of the equation.

Introduction to Factorisation

  • The process of breaking down a polynomial into simpler factors.
  • It's the reverse process of multiplying polynomials.
  • Helps in simplifying expressions and solving equations.

🎙️ Just as we can break down a number like 12 into its prime factors 2, 2, and 3, we can do the same for polynomials. Factorisation helps us simplify expressions and solve equations by finding their building blocks.

Factors and Multiples (Recap)

  • For numbers: 6 = 2 × 3. Here, 2 and 3 are factors of 6.
  • For polynomials: If P(x) = G(x) × H(x), then G(x) and H(x) are factors of P(x).
  • Example: x² - 4 = (x-2)(x+2). (x-2) and (x+2) are factors.

🎙️ Recall from numbers that factors multiply to give the original number. Similarly, polynomial factors are expressions that multiply to give the original polynomial. This concept is crucial for factorisation.

Remainder Theorem

  • If a polynomial P(x) is divided by a linear polynomial (x-a), then the remainder is P(a).
  • A shortcut to find the remainder without actual division.
  • Example: If P(x) = x² + 2x + 1, remainder when divided by (x-1) is P(1) = 1²+2(1)+1 = 4.

🎙️ The Remainder Theorem gives us a shortcut. Instead of performing long division, we can simply substitute the value 'a' into the polynomial P(x) to find the remainder when divided by (x-a). It's quite handy!

Worked Example: Remainder Theorem

  • Find the remainder when x³ + 2x² - 5x + 7 is divided by x - 1.

🎙️ Here, our divisor is (x-1), so 'a' is 1. Substituting 1 into the polynomial P(x) gives us 1 + 2 - 5 + 7, which equals 5. So, the remainder is 5.

1Divisor is (x-1), so a = 1
2Substitute x = 1 into P(x)
3P(1) = (1)³ + 2(1)² - 5(1) + 7
4P(1) = 1 + 2 - 5 + 7 = 5
5The remainder is 5

Factor Theorem

  • If P(a) = 0, then (x-a) is a factor of the polynomial P(x).
  • Conversely, if (x-a) is a factor of P(x), then P(a) = 0.
  • A special case of the Remainder Theorem (remainder is zero).

🎙️ The Factor Theorem is a direct consequence of the Remainder Theorem. If the remainder P(a) is zero, it means (x-a) divides P(x) perfectly, making it a factor. This helps us find factors easily.

Worked Example: Factor Theorem

  • Is (x-3) a factor of P(x) = x² - 5x + 6?

🎙️ Here, 'a' is 3. Substituting 3 into P(x) = x² - 5x + 6 gives 9 - 15 + 6, which equals 0. Since P(3) = 0, by the Factor Theorem, (x-3) is indeed a factor.

1Divisor is (x-3), so a = 3
2Substitute x = 3 into P(x)
3P(3) = (3)² - 5(3) + 6
4P(3) = 9 - 15 + 6 = 0
5Since P(3)=0, (x-3) is a factor

Factorisation by Splitting the Middle Term

  • A common method for factorising quadratic polynomials (ax² + bx + c).
  • Find two numbers whose product is 'ac' and sum is 'b'.
  • Rewrite the middle term 'bx' using these two numbers, then factor by grouping.

🎙️ This is a powerful technique for quadratic polynomials. We essentially 'split' the middle term 'bx' into two terms using two numbers that satisfy specific product and sum conditions, then factor by grouping common terms.

✂️
Dividing for Factors
21 / 25

Worked Example: Splitting Middle Term

  • Factorise the quadratic polynomial x² + 7x + 10.

🎙️ For x² + 7x + 10, we need two numbers that multiply to 10 and add to 7. These are 2 and 5. We rewrite 7x as 2x+5x, then group terms and factor out common factors to get (x+2)(x+5).

1Product 'ac' = 1 × 10 = 10
2Sum 'b' = 7
3Numbers are 2 and 5 (2×5=10, 2+5=7)
4Rewrite: x² + 2x + 5x + 10
5Factor by grouping: x(x+2) + 5(x+2)
6Result: (x+2)(x+5)

Algebraic Identities for Factorisation

  • a² + 2ab + b² = (a + b)²
  • a² - 2ab + b² = (a - b)²
  • a² - b² = (a - b)(a + b)
  • These are shortcuts to factorise specific polynomial forms.

🎙️ Algebraic identities are like ready-made formulas that help us factorise certain types of polynomials quickly. Recognising these patterns can save a lot of time and simplify complex expressions.

Worked Example: Using Identities

  • Factorise 9x² - 25y².

🎙️ This polynomial is in the form of a difference of two squares. We can write 9x² as (3x)² and 25y² as (5y)². Applying the identity a² - b² = (a-b)(a+b) directly gives us the factors.

1Recognise as a difference of squares
2Write as (3x)² - (5y)²
3Apply a² - b² = (a - b)(a + b)
4Result: (3x - 5y)(3x + 5y)

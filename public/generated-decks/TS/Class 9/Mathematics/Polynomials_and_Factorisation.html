What is a Polynomial? A Special Kind of Expression

  • A polynomial is an algebraic expression where the powers of the variable are only non-negative integers (0, 1, 2, 3...).
  • No negative powers, no fractional powers, no variables in the denominator!
  • Examples: 3x² + 2x - 1, 5y³, 7.
  • Non-examples: x⁻¹, √x, 1/x.

🎙️ Not all algebraic expressions are polynomials. A key rule for polynomials is that the powers of the variables must always be whole numbers – no fractions, no negative numbers. Think of it as a special club with strict entry rules!

Terms of a Polynomial: The Building Blocks

  • Think of a train: each bogie is a separate part, but together they form the train.
  • In a polynomial, the parts separated by '+' or '-' signs are called 'terms'.
  • Example: In 3x² + 2x - 1, the terms are 3x², 2x, and -1.
  • Each term is a product of a constant and one or more variables with non-negative integer powers.

🎙️ Just like a train is made of several bogies, a polynomial is made of several terms. Each term is a product of numbers and variables, and they are joined together by addition or subtraction signs. For instance, in '3x² + 2x - 1', we have three distinct terms.

Coefficients: The Multipliers of Variables

  • If you have '3 apples', '3' tells you how many apples.
  • In a term like '5x²', '5' tells you how many 'x²'s you have.
  • The numerical factor of a term is called its 'coefficient'.
  • Example: In 3x² + 2x - 1, the coefficient of x² is 3, and the coefficient of x is 2. The constant term -1 also has a coefficient of -1 (or can be seen as the coefficient of x⁰).

🎙️ The coefficient is simply the numerical part that multiplies the variable part of a term. It tells you 'how many' of that particular variable power you have. So, if you see '7y', the number 7 is the coefficient of 'y'.

Degree of a Polynomial: The Highest Power

  • Think of the tallest building in a city – that building defines its skyline.
  • The 'degree' of a polynomial is the highest power of the variable in any of its terms.
  • Example: In 5x³ + 2x² - 7x + 1, the powers are 3, 2, 1, 0. The highest is 3.
  • So, the degree of 5x³ + 2x² - 7x + 1 is 3.

🎙️ The degree of a polynomial is like finding the highest point in a landscape. You look at all the powers of the variable in each term, and the largest one is the degree of the entire polynomial. This is a very important characteristic of a polynomial.

Types of Polynomials: By Number of Terms

  • Polynomials are classified based on how many terms they have.
  • Monomial: One term (e.g., 5x²)
  • Binomial: Two terms (e.g., 2x + 3)
  • Trinomial: Three terms (e.g., x² - 4x + 7)
  • Polynomials with more than three terms are generally just called 'polynomials'.

🎙️ We can categorize polynomials based on the number of terms they have. This helps us to describe them more precisely. Monomials have one term, binomials have two, and trinomials have three. Beyond that, we just use the general term 'polynomial'.

Zero of a Polynomial: The Root of the Problem

  • Think of finding the 'sweet spot' or the exact point where something balances.
  • A 'zero' of a polynomial p(x) is a value of 'x' for which p(x) becomes 0.
  • It's the value that makes the entire polynomial expression equal to zero.
  • If p(x) = 2x - 4, and we find x=2, then p(2) = 2(2) - 4 = 0. So, 2 is a zero.

🎙️ The 'zero' of a polynomial is a very important concept. It's the specific value of the variable that makes the entire polynomial expression evaluate to zero. On a graph, these are the points where the curve crosses the x-axis.

How to Find the Zero of a Linear Polynomial

  • For a linear polynomial p(x) = ax + b (where a ≠ 0):
  • 1. Set the polynomial equal to zero: ax + b = 0.
  • 2. Isolate the variable 'x'.
  • 3. The value you find for 'x' is the zero of the polynomial.
  • Example: Find the zero of p(x) = 3x - 6.

🎙️ Finding the zero of a linear polynomial is straightforward. You simply set the entire expression equal to zero and then solve for the variable. For example, to find the zero of 3x - 6, we set 3x - 6 = 0, which gives 3x = 6, and thus x = 2. So, 2 is the zero.

1Set p(x) = 0
2Isolate x
3Value of x is the zero

Factorisation of Polynomials: Breaking Down

  • Think of breaking down a large number (like 12) into its prime factors (2 × 2 × 3).
  • Factorisation of a polynomial means expressing it as a product of two or more polynomials.
  • These smaller polynomials are called 'factors'.
  • Example: x² - 4 = (x - 2)(x + 2). Here, (x - 2) and (x + 2) are factors.

🎙️ Factorisation is a fundamental skill in algebra. It's like reverse multiplication. Instead of multiplying factors to get a polynomial, we start with a polynomial and break it down into its constituent factors. This helps simplify expressions and solve equations.

Factor Theorem: A Shortcut to Factors

  • If p(x) is a polynomial of degree n ≥ 1 and 'a' is any real number,
  • 1. If p(a) = 0, then (x - a) is a factor of p(x).
  • 2. If (x - a) is a factor of p(x), then p(a) = 0.
  • This theorem provides a quick way to check if a linear expression is a factor of a polynomial.

🎙️ The Factor Theorem is a powerful tool. It directly links the zeros of a polynomial to its factors. If substituting a number 'a' into a polynomial gives you zero, then 'x - a' must be a factor. This saves a lot of work when trying to find factors.

Remainder Theorem: What's Left Over?

  • When you divide 10 by 3, you get a remainder of 1 (10 = 3 × 3 + 1).
  • If p(x) is a polynomial of degree n ≥ 1 is divided by a linear polynomial (x - a),
  • Then the remainder is p(a).
  • This means we don't need to perform long division to find the remainder!

🎙️ The Remainder Theorem is incredibly useful! Instead of doing long and tedious polynomial division, you can simply substitute the value 'a' (from the divisor 'x - a') into the polynomial p(x). The result will be your remainder. It's a fantastic shortcut!

Factorisation by Splitting the Middle Term (for Quadratics)

  • This method is commonly used for quadratic trinomials of the form ax² + bx + c.
  • 1. Find two numbers 'p' and 'q' such that p + q = b and p × q = ac.
  • 2. Rewrite the middle term 'bx' as 'px + qx'.
  • 3. Group the terms and factor out common monomials.
  • 4. Factor out the common binomial factor.

🎙️ Splitting the middle term is a crucial technique for factorising quadratic polynomials. The trick is to find two numbers that add up to the middle coefficient 'b' and multiply to the product of 'a' and 'c'. Once you find these, the rest is grouping and common factoring.

1Find p, q (sum=b, product=ac)
2Rewrite bx as px + qx
3Group terms and factor
4Factor out common binomial

Common Mistakes to Avoid!

  • Confusing 'term' with 'factor'.
  • Incorrectly identifying the degree (e.g., for constant polynomial like 5, degree is 0, not 1).
  • Mistakes in signs while applying Factor/Remainder Theorem (e.g., for x+a, use p(-a)).
  • Errors in calculations when splitting the middle term (p+q=b, p*q=ac).

🎙️ Be careful with these common pitfalls! Remember that terms are added/subtracted parts, while factors are multiplied parts. Always double-check the sign when applying the theorems, and ensure your 'p' and 'q' values satisfy both conditions for splitting the middle term.

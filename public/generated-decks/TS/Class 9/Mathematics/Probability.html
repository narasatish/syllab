Random Experiment: What's the Fuss?

  • An activity with well-defined outcomes.
  • The outcome cannot be predicted with certainty.
  • Can be repeated under similar conditions.

🎙️ Just like a science experiment, a random experiment is an action or process that leads to one of several possible results, but we can't tell which one beforehand. For example, rolling a die is a random experiment because you don't know which number will show up, but you know it'll be between 1 and 6.

Sample Space: All Possible Outcomes

🎙️ The sample space is like a complete list of everything that could possibly happen in an experiment. It's crucial to identify the sample space correctly, as it forms the basis for calculating probabilities. It ensures we consider all potential results.

Empirical Probability: Learning from Experience

  • Also called Experimental Probability.
  • Based on actual observations and experiments.
  • Calculated from the frequency of an event.

🎙️ Empirical probability is all about 'doing and observing'. We perform an experiment multiple times, record the results, and then use those observations to estimate the likelihood of an event. It's practical and relies on real data, unlike theoretical probability which we'll learn later.

Formula for Empirical Probability

  • P(Event E) = (Number of trials where E happened) / (Total number of trials)
  • This ratio gives us the probability value.
  • The value is always between 0 and 1.

🎙️ This formula is the heart of empirical probability. You count how many times your desired event occurred and divide it by the total number of times you performed the experiment. Remember, the result will always be a fraction or decimal between zero and one.

Sum of Probabilities: The Whole Picture

  • The sum of probabilities of all possible outcomes in a sample space is always 1.
  • P(Heads) + P(Tails) = 1 (for a fair coin).
  • It means one of the outcomes is certain to happen.

🎙️ This is a fundamental rule in probability. If you list out all possible, mutually exclusive outcomes of an experiment and add their individual probabilities, the total will always be 1. It represents 100% certainty that one of those outcomes will occur.

Probability Range: Between 0 and 1

  • The probability of any event E, denoted P(E), is always between 0 and 1 (inclusive).
  • 0 ≤ P(E) ≤ 1
  • It can be a fraction, decimal, or percentage.

🎙️ This is a crucial rule for all probabilities. The probability value can never be negative, nor can it be greater than 1. If your calculation gives you a value outside this range, you know there's been a mistake. It's a quick way to check your answers.

Complementary Events: The 'Not' Event

  • If E is an event, then 'not E' (or E') is its complementary event.
  • P(E) + P(not E) = 1
  • Example: P(rain) + P(no rain) = 1.

🎙️ Complementary events cover all possibilities. If an event E happens, its complement 'not E' means E does not happen. Together, they make up the entire sample space, so their probabilities must sum to 1. This relationship is very useful for calculations.

Worked Example 4: Cricket Match

  • In a cricket match, the probability that India wins is 0.7.
  • What is the probability that India does not win?
  • Assume no ties are possible.

🎙️ This is a classic application of complementary events. We know the probability of India winning. The event 'India does not win' is the complement. Using the formula P(E) + P(not E) = 1, we can easily find the answer.

🏏
Cricket Bat & Ball
26 / 36

Solution 4: Cricket Match

  • Let E be the event 'India wins'. P(E) = 0.7
  • Let E' be the event 'India does not win'.
  • P(E') = 1 - P(E) = 1 - 0.7 = 0.3

🎙️ So, the probability that India does not win is 0.3. This means there's a 30% chance they won't win the match, given the probability of winning is 70%. Simple, isn't it? This formula saves a lot of calculation time.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Calculating probability > 1 or < 0.
  • Incorrectly identifying total outcomes.
  • Not simplifying fractions to their lowest terms.
  • Confusing favourable outcomes with total outcomes.

🎙️ It's easy to make small errors in probability. Always double-check that your probability value is between 0 and 1. Make sure you correctly count the total possible outcomes and the specific outcomes for your event. Also, simplify your fractions for the final answer.

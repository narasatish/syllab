What is a Mathematical Proof?

Axioms and Postulates: Self-Evident Truths

  • Statements accepted as true without proof.
  • Axioms are general mathematical truths (e.g., things equal to the same thing are equal to each other).
  • Postulates are specific to geometry (e.g., a straight line can be drawn between any two points).

🎙️ Axioms and postulates are like the basic rules of a game; we agree they are true so we can start playing. They are the foundational statements upon which all other mathematical truths are built.

Direct Proof: Step-by-Step Logic

  • Starts with the given information or known truths.
  • Applies definitions, axioms, and theorems directly.
  • Logically progresses step-by-step to the conclusion.

🎙️ Direct proof is the most common and straightforward method. You simply start with what you're given and, through a series of logical deductions, arrive at the statement you want to prove. It's like walking directly from point A to point B.

Example: Sum of Two Even Numbers

  • Given: Let 'a' and 'b' be any two even integers.
  • To Prove: a + b is an even integer.
  • Proof: Since a and b are even, a = 2k and b = 2m for some integers k, m.
  • Therefore, a + b = 2k + 2m = 2(k + m). Since (k+m) is an integer, 2(k+m) is even. Hence, a+b is even.

🎙️ Let's try a direct proof. We know what an even number is (a multiple of 2). We use this definition to represent our even numbers and then show that their sum also fits the definition of an even number. Simple, right?

Indirect Proof: Proving by Contradiction

  • Assume the opposite of what you want to prove is true.
  • Show that this assumption leads to a contradiction (a statement that is logically impossible).
  • Since the assumption leads to a contradiction, the original statement must be true.

🎙️ Indirect proof, or proof by contradiction, is a clever technique. Instead of directly showing something is true, you assume it's false and then demonstrate that this assumption creates a logical impossibility. If assuming it's false leads to nonsense, then it must be true!

↩️
Reversing an Assumption

Example: If n² is even, then n is even

  • Given: n² is an even integer.
  • To Prove: n is an even integer.
  • Assume for contradiction: n is an odd integer.
  • If n is odd, then n = 2k + 1 for some integer k. Then n² = (2k+1)² = 4k² + 4k + 1 = 2(2k²+2k) + 1. This means n² is odd.
  • This contradicts our given statement that n² is even. Therefore, our assumption that n is odd must be false, meaning n is even.

🎙️ Here, we want to prove 'if n² is even, then n is even'. We start by assuming the opposite: that n is odd. By showing that this leads to n² being odd, which contradicts our initial 'given' information, we prove that n must indeed be even.

Proof by Counterexample: Finding an Exception

  • Used to disprove a universal statement (e.g., 'All X are Y').
  • Find just one specific case where the statement is false.
  • One counterexample is enough to prove a universal statement false.

🎙️ Sometimes, proving something false is easier than proving it true. If someone makes a claim like 'All swans are white', you just need to find one black swan to prove them wrong. That one exception is called a counterexample.

🚫
Disproving

Example: "All Prime Numbers are Odd"

  • Statement: All prime numbers are odd.
  • To Disprove: Find a prime number that is not odd.
  • Counterexample: The number 2 is a prime number.
  • However, 2 is an even number, not odd. Therefore, the statement 'All prime numbers are odd' is false.

🎙️ This is a classic example. Many students might think all prime numbers are odd because most of them are (3, 5, 7, 11...). But the number 2 is a prime number, and it's even. This single counterexample disproves the entire statement.

  • Proof involves drawing a line parallel to one side through the opposite vertex.
  • Uses properties of parallel lines and transversal to show angles add up to 180°.

🎙️ This is a classic geometric proof you might have seen or will see. By adding an auxiliary line, we can use our knowledge of parallel lines to logically demonstrate that the three angles inside any triangle always sum to 180 degrees.

Another Geometric Proof: Pythagoras Theorem

  • Theorem: In a right-angled triangle, the square of the hypotenuse (c) is equal to the sum of the squares of the other two sides (a and b).
  • Formula: a² + b² = c².
  • Many different proofs exist, often using area dissection or similarity.

🎙️ The Pythagoras Theorem is one of the most famous theorems, with a rich history of proofs. While we won't go through a full proof now, understanding that this fundamental relationship has been rigorously proven is key.

Algebraic Proof: Expanding (a+b)²

  • Theorem: For any real numbers a and b, (a+b)² = a² + 2ab + b².
  • Proof: (a+b)² = (a+b) * (a+b).
  • Using distributive property: a(a+b) + b(a+b) = a² + ab + ba + b² = a² + 2ab + b².

🎙️ Algebraic proofs use the rules of algebra to show that an equation or identity is true. Here, we simply expand the expression using the distributive property, which is a fundamental rule of algebra, to arrive at the desired result.

Algebraic Proof: Sum of First 'n' Odd Numbers

  • Conjecture: The sum of the first 'n' odd numbers is n².
  • Example: 1 = 1²; 1+3 = 4 = 2²; 1+3+5 = 9 = 3².
  • Proof involves using arithmetic progression formulas or visual patterns.

🎙️ This is a beautiful pattern! We can see it holds for small numbers. To prove it for any 'n', we can use summation formulas from arithmetic progressions or even a geometric proof by arranging squares.

Beware of Common Proof Errors!

  • Circular Reasoning: Assuming what you are trying to prove.
  • Assuming the Conclusion: Using the conclusion as a step in your proof.
  • Incorrect Logic: Making jumps or using invalid deductions.

🎙️ Just like in any argument, it's easy to make mistakes in proofs. We must be very careful not to assume our conclusion or use faulty logic. A proof is only as strong as its weakest link.

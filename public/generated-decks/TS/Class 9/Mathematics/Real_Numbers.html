  • Starts from 0: {0, 1, 2, 3, ...}

Sharing Equally: Rational Numbers (Q)

  • Numbers written as p/q.
  • p and q are integers, q ≠ 0.
  • Includes fractions and terminating/recurring decimals.

🎙️ Imagine sharing a pizza with friends. You might get half, or one-quarter. These 'parts' are represented by fractions, which are rational numbers. All natural, whole, and integers can also be written in p/q form, making them rational.

Rational Numbers: Examples and Properties

  • Examples: 1/2, -3/4, 5 (as 5/1), 0.75.
  • They are closed under addition, subtraction, multiplication, division (except by zero).
  • They follow commutative and associative properties.

🎙️ Rational numbers are incredibly versatile. We use them for measurements, percentages, and many daily calculations. They behave predictably under basic arithmetic operations, which is why they are so fundamental.

Finding Friends: Rationals Between Two Numbers

  • There are infinitely many rational numbers between any two given rational numbers.
  • Method 1: Find the average (a+b)/2.

🎙️ This is a fascinating property! Unlike integers, where there's a finite number between two integers, rationals are densely packed. The averaging method is a simple way to find one such number, and you can repeat it to find more.

Exact Divisions: Terminating Decimals

  • Decimal expansion that ends after a finite number of digits.
  • Example: 1/2 = 0.5
  • Occurs when the denominator (q) has only 2 or 5 as prime factors.

🎙️ When you divide the numerator by the denominator, and the remainder eventually becomes zero, you get a terminating decimal. This is like a perfect division where nothing is left over. It's a clean and finite representation.

Repeating Patterns: Non-Terminating Recurring Decimals

  • Decimal expansion that never ends, but has a repeating block of digits.
  • Example: 1/3 = 0.333...
  • Also called 'non-terminating repeating' decimals.

🎙️ Sometimes, when you divide, the remainder never becomes zero, but a pattern of digits starts repeating. This is a recurring decimal. It goes on forever, but in a predictable, rhythmic way, like a loop.

The Mystery Numbers: Irrational Numbers (I or Q')

  • Numbers that cannot be written as p/q.
  • Decimal expansion is non-terminating AND non-recurring.
  • Examples: √2, √3, π (pi).

🎙️ Imagine numbers that are truly endless and unpredictable in their decimal form. These are irrational numbers! They cannot be expressed as a simple fraction. Famous examples include the square root of 2 and pi.

♾️

Placing √2: On the Number Line

  • Draw a number line.
  • Construct a right triangle with legs of 1 unit each.
  • The hypotenuse will be √2 units long (by Pythagoras theorem).
  • Draw an arc from 0 with radius √2 to cut the number line.

🎙️ It might seem tricky to place a number like √2 on a number line since it's not a neat fraction. However, using the Pythagorean theorem, we can geometrically construct its exact position. This demonstrates that even 'mystery numbers' have a place!

Mixing Numbers: Operations - Addition and Subtraction

  • Rational + Irrational = Irrational (e.g., 2 + √3).
  • Irrational + Irrational: Can be Rational or Irrational (e.g., √2 + √2 = 2√2, √2 + (-√2) = 0).
  • Simplify by combining like terms (e.g., 3√2 + 5√2 = 8√2).

🎙️ When you mix different types of numbers, the results can be interesting! Generally, adding or subtracting a rational and an irrational number results in an irrational number. However, combining two irrational numbers can sometimes yield a rational result.

Combining Values

Mixing Numbers: Operations - Multiplication and Division

  • Rational × Irrational = Irrational (e.g., 2 × √3 = 2√3).
  • Irrational × Irrational: Can be Rational or Irrational (e.g., √2 × √2 = 2, √2 × √3 = √6).
  • Rational numbers cannot be zero in these operations to maintain irrationality.

🎙️ Similar to addition and subtraction, multiplication and division involving irrational numbers can lead to varied outcomes. A non-zero rational number multiplied by an irrational number will always be irrational. But two irrationals can multiply to give a rational number.

Making it Rational: Rationalising the Denominator

  • Process of converting an irrational denominator to a rational one.
  • Achieved by multiplying the numerator and denominator by a suitable factor.
  • Example: 1/√a becomes √a/a.

🎙️ Why do we rationalise? It makes calculations easier and standardises the form of expressions. When the denominator contains a square root, we multiply by that same square root to remove it, effectively 'cleaning up' the expression.

Power Play: Laws of Exponents for Real Numbers

  • a^m × a^n = a^(m+n)
  • (a^m)^n = a^(mn)
  • a^m / a^n = a^(m-n)
  • a^(1/n) = nth root of a.

🎙️ You've learned about exponents in earlier classes. These laws extend to real numbers, making calculations with powers and roots much simpler. Understanding these rules is crucial for solving many problems in algebra.

Watch Out! Common Mistakes

  • Confusing terminating and recurring decimals.
  • Adding/subtracting unlike irrational terms (e.g., √2 + √3 ≠ √5).
  • Assuming all square roots are irrational (e.g., √4 = 2).
  • Errors in rationalising denominators.

🎙️ It's natural to make mistakes, but being aware of common pitfalls can help you avoid them. Pay close attention to these areas, as they are often sources of errors in exams. Practice makes perfect!

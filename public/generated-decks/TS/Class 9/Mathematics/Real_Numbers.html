  • Natural numbers including zero.
  • Start from 0: {0, 1, 2, 3, ...}

Integers (Z)

  • Positive numbers, negative numbers, and zero.
  • {..., -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, ...}
  • No fractions or decimals.

🎙️ Integers expand our number system to include negative values. Think of a number line extending infinitely in both positive and negative directions, with zero at the center.

Rational Numbers (Q)

  • Can be written as p/q.
  • Where p and q are integers.
  • q cannot be zero (q ≠ 0).

🎙️ Rational numbers are numbers that can be expressed as a fraction, where both the numerator and denominator are integers, and the denominator is not zero. This includes all integers, as they can be written as p/1.

➗
Fraction Form

Properties of Rational Numbers

  • Closed under addition, subtraction, multiplication.
  • Division (except by zero) is also closed.
  • Follow commutative, associative, and distributive laws.

🎙️ Rational numbers behave nicely under basic arithmetic operations. When you add, subtract, or multiply two rational numbers, the result is always another rational number. The same applies to division, as long as you don't divide by zero.

Decimal Representation of Rational Numbers

  • Terminating: Decimals that end (e.g., 0.5).
  • Non-terminating Recurring: Decimals that repeat a pattern (e.g., 0.333...).
  • All rational numbers have one of these forms.

🎙️ When you convert a rational number (a fraction) to a decimal, it will either end after a finite number of digits, or the digits will start repeating in a pattern. These are the only two possibilities for rational numbers.

Irrational Numbers (Q')

  • Cannot be written as p/q form.
  • p and q are integers, q ≠ 0.
  • Non-terminating and non-recurring decimals.

🎙️ Now, let's meet the 'other' kind of numbers – irrational numbers. These are numbers that cannot be expressed as a simple fraction. Their decimal expansions go on forever without any repeating pattern.

🚫
Not a Fraction

Examples of Irrational Numbers

  • Square roots of non-perfect squares (e.g., √2, √3).
  • Pi (π ≈ 3.14159...).
  • Euler's number (e ≈ 2.71828...).

🎙️ The most common examples of irrational numbers are square roots of numbers that are not perfect squares, like √2. Another famous irrational number is Pi, which you use to calculate the circumference of a circle.

Locating Irrational Numbers on the Number Line

  • We can represent irrational numbers geometrically.
  • Using the Pythagorean theorem.
  • Example: √2 on the number line.

🎙️ Even though irrational numbers have infinite non-repeating decimals, we can still accurately locate them on the number line. We often use geometric constructions, like applying the Pythagorean theorem.

📏
Number Line
17 / 25

Worked Example: Locating √2 on Number Line

  • Draw a unit square (sides 1 unit) from 0.
  • The diagonal has length √2 (by Pythagoras).
  • Use a compass to transfer this length to the number line.

🎙️ To locate √2, start at 0, draw a line segment of 1 unit. From 1, draw another segment of 1 unit perpendicular to the first. The hypotenuse of this right triangle is √2. Now use a compass to mark this length on the number line.

2Draw perpendicular 1 unit
3Connect to form √2

Real Numbers (R)

  • The collection of all rational and irrational numbers.
  • Represent every point on the number line.
  • No gaps on the number line.

🎙️ Finally, we come to Real Numbers. This is the big umbrella that covers both rational and irrational numbers. Essentially, any number you can think of that can be placed on a number line is a real number.

ℝ
All Numbers
19 / 25

Properties of Real Numbers

  • Follow all basic arithmetic operations.
  • Are ordered (can be compared).
  • Every real number has a unique point on the number line.

🎙️ Real numbers form a complete and continuous system. They obey all the rules of addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division. You can always compare any two real numbers, knowing which one is greater or smaller.

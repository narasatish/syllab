The Big Reveal: Linear Equation in Two Variables

  • An equation that can be written in the form `ax + by + c = 0`.
  • Here, x and y are the two variables.
  • a, b, and c are real numbers, and 'a' and 'b' are not both zero.

🎙️ This is the formal definition. The 'linear' part means that when you graph this equation, it will form a straight line. The 'two variables' part means we have two unknowns, typically 'x' and 'y', whose highest power is 1.

Standard Form: Our Guiding Structure

  • The general form `ax + by + c = 0` is called the standard form.
  • 'a' is the coefficient of x, 'b' is the coefficient of y.
  • 'c' is the constant term.

🎙️ It's important to always try and write your linear equations in this standard form. This helps us easily identify the coefficients 'a', 'b', and the constant 'c'. Remember, 'a' and 'b' cannot both be zero, otherwise it wouldn't be a linear equation in two variables.

Spot the Coefficients: a, b, and c

  • Rewrite in standard form: `2x + 3y - 5 = 0`

Finding the Perfect Match: Solutions

  • A solution to a linear equation in two variables is a pair of values (x, y).
  • When substituted into the equation, this pair makes the equation true.
  • It's like finding the right key for a lock!

🎙️ For an equation like `x + y = 10`, a solution could be (5, 5) because 5 + 5 = 10. Another solution could be (3, 7). The solution is always an ordered pair (x, y), where the first value is for x and the second for y.

Endless Pairs: Infinite Solutions!

  • Unlike equations in one variable, linear equations in two variables have infinitely many solutions.
  • You can find countless (x, y) pairs that satisfy the equation.
  • Each solution corresponds to a point on the line.

🎙️ This is a key difference! For `x + y = 10`, besides (5,5) and (3,7), we can have (0,10), (10,0), (2,8), (1,9), even fractions like (4.5, 5.5). There are endless possibilities, forming a continuous line when plotted.

Let's Find Solutions: A Step-by-Step Guide

  • Step 1: Choose any value for one variable (e.g., x).
  • Step 2: Substitute this value into the equation.
  • Step 3: Solve the resulting linear equation in one variable for the other variable (y).

🎙️ This systematic approach helps you find solutions easily. You can choose any value for x or y, positive or negative, integers or fractions. The simpler the value you choose, the easier the calculation will be.

1Choose x or y
2Substitute value

Example Time: Finding Solutions for `x + y = 5`

  • If `x = 0`, then `0 + y = 5`, so `y = 5`. Solution: (0, 5)
  • If `x = 5`, then `5 + y = 5`, so `y = 0`. Solution: (5, 0)
  • If `x = 2`, then `2 + y = 5`, so `y = 3`. Solution: (2, 3)

🎙️ See how we picked simple values for x, like 0, 5, and 2? This made it very easy to calculate the corresponding y values. Each (x, y) pair is a valid solution for the equation `x + y = 5`.

Our Canvas: The Cartesian Plane

  • It's a two-dimensional plane defined by two perpendicular lines: x-axis and y-axis.
  • Points are represented by ordered pairs `(x, y)`.
  • The intersection of the axes is the origin `(0, 0)`.

🎙️ Remember the Cartesian plane from previous classes? The horizontal line is the x-axis, and the vertical line is the y-axis. Every point on this plane can be uniquely identified by an ordered pair (x, y). We will use this plane to plot our solutions.

Plotting Our Path: Graphing Steps

  • Step 1: Find at least two solutions (x, y) for the equation.
  • Step 2: Plot these points on the Cartesian plane.
  • Step 3: Draw a straight line passing through these points and extend it.

🎙️ You only need two points to draw a straight line. However, finding a third point is a good way to check your work and ensure accuracy. If all three points lie on the same straight line, your calculations are likely correct.

Special Lines: Parallel to Axes

  • Equations of the form `x = k` (where k is a constant) are lines parallel to the y-axis.
  • Equations of the form `y = k` are lines parallel to the x-axis.
  • These are also linear equations in two variables, just with a zero coefficient for one variable.

🎙️ Consider `x = 3`. This can be written as `1x + 0y - 3 = 0`. No matter what y is, x is always 3. So, it's a vertical line. Similarly, `y = -2` means `0x + 1y + 2 = 0`, a horizontal line. These are important special cases.

Watch Out! Common Mistakes

  • Confusing `a`, `b`, and `c` values, especially signs.
  • Incorrectly substituting values when checking solutions.
  • Plotting points inaccurately on the graph.
  • Not drawing a straight line extending across the plane.

🎙️ It's easy to make small errors, so be vigilant! Always double-check your calculations and plotting. A slight error in a coordinate can lead to a completely different line. Take your time, especially during graphing.

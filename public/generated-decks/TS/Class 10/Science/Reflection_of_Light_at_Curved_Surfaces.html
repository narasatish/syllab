Flat vs. Curved Reflection

  • Plane mirrors: Flat, produce virtual, erect, same-size images.
  • Curved mirrors: Bend light, produce varied images (real/virtual, inverted/erect, magnified/diminished).

🎙️ Remember plane mirrors? They give us a clear, identical reflection. But curved mirrors are different; they can distort, magnify, or shrink reflections because they bend light in more complex ways. This bending property is what makes them so versatile and useful.

Meet Spherical Mirrors

  • These mirrors are a part of a hollow sphere.
  • Their reflecting surface is curved inwards or outwards.

🎙️ Imagine a big, hollow glass ball. If you cut out a piece of this ball and silver one of its surfaces, you get a spherical mirror! Their curve comes directly from being a segment of a sphere, giving them unique reflective properties.

Two Kinds: Concave & Convex

  • Concave mirror: Reflecting surface curves inwards (like the inside of a spoon).
  • Convex mirror: Reflecting surface curves outwards (like the outside of a spoon).

🎙️ Just like our spoon example, there are two main types of spherical mirrors. The inward-curving one is called concave, and the outward-curving one is called convex. They each have distinct ways of reflecting light and forming images.

Concave Mirror: The 'Inward' Curve

🎙️ Think of a cave, it curves inwards. A concave mirror works similarly, reflecting light towards a central point. This focusing ability makes them very useful for applications like flashlights and solar furnaces, where we want to concentrate light.

Convex Mirror: The 'Outward' Curve

🎙️ A convex mirror bulges out, spreading light rays away from each other. This diverging property gives them a wider field of view, making them ideal for applications where you need to see a large area, like in vehicle rear-view mirrors.

Important Terms: Pole (P) & Centre of Curvature (C)

  • Pole (P): The geometric centre of the spherical mirror.
  • Centre of Curvature (C): The centre of the hollow sphere from which the mirror was cut.

🎙️ To understand how mirrors work, we need to define some key reference points. The Pole is the exact middle point on the mirror's surface. The Centre of Curvature is like the imaginary centre of the original sphere that the mirror is a part of.

Important Terms: Radius of Curvature (R) & Principal Axis

  • Radius of Curvature (R): The distance between the Pole (P) and the Centre of Curvature (C).
  • Principal Axis: An imaginary straight line passing through the Pole (P) and the Centre of Curvature (C).

🎙️ The Radius of Curvature is simply the radius of that imaginary sphere. The Principal Axis is a crucial reference line that helps us draw ray diagrams and understand where images will form. It's the backbone of our mirror system.

Important Terms: Principal Focus (F) & Focal Length (f)

  • Principal Focus (F): The point on the principal axis where parallel rays actually meet (concave) or appear to meet (convex) after reflection.
  • Focal Length (f): The distance between the Pole (P) and the Principal Focus (F).

🎙️ The Principal Focus is where the magic happens – where light rays converge or diverge. For a concave mirror, parallel rays literally meet here. For a convex mirror, they spread out but appear to come from this point behind the mirror. The Focal Length is simply the distance to this special point.

The Link: Focal Length (f) & Radius (R)

  • For spherical mirrors, the principal focus (F) lies exactly midway between the Pole (P) and the Centre of Curvature (C).
  • Therefore, the focal length (f) is half of the radius of curvature (R).
  • The relationship is: f = R/2.

🎙️ This is a very important relationship for spherical mirrors. It means if you know the radius of curvature, you can easily find the focal length, and vice-versa. This formula is fundamental for calculations involving mirrors.

Ray Diagram Rules: Parallel Ray

  • Rule 1: A ray parallel to the principal axis, after reflection, passes through the principal focus (F) for a concave mirror.
  • For a convex mirror, it appears to diverge from the principal focus (F).

🎙️ These rules are our tools for drawing ray diagrams, which help us visualize image formation. The first rule tells us what happens to light coming in parallel to the main axis. For concave, it goes directly through F; for convex, it looks like it came from F.

Ray Diagram Rules: Through Focus

  • Rule 2: A ray passing through the principal focus (F) of a concave mirror, after reflection, becomes parallel to the principal axis.
  • For a convex mirror, a ray directed towards the principal focus (F) becomes parallel to the principal axis.

🎙️ This rule is essentially the reverse of the first one! If light hits the mirror aiming at or coming from the focus, it will reflect back parallel to the principal axis. This symmetry is very useful in understanding light paths.

🎯

Ray Diagram Rules: Through Centre

  • Rule 3: A ray passing through the centre of curvature (C) of a concave mirror, after reflection, is reflected back along the same path.
  • For a convex mirror, a ray directed towards the centre of curvature (C) is reflected back along the same path.

🎙️ Think of the centre of curvature as the normal to the mirror at that point. Any light ray passing through C hits the mirror perpendicularly, meaning it bounces straight back along its original path. This is true for both concave and convex mirrors.

Ray Diagram Rules: To the Pole

  • Rule 4: A ray incident obliquely to the principal axis, towards the Pole (P) of the mirror, is reflected obliquely.
  • The incident and reflected rays make equal angles with the principal axis.

🎙️ This rule is based on the law of reflection: angle of incidence equals angle of reflection. When a ray hits the pole, the principal axis acts as the normal, so the ray reflects at an equal angle on the other side. This is also applicable to both types of spherical mirrors.

Concave Mirror: Object at Infinity

  • When an object is very far away (at infinity), rays coming from it are considered parallel.
  • Image is formed at the Principal Focus (F).
  • Image is real, inverted, and highly diminished (point-sized).

🎙️ Imagine the sun's rays hitting a concave mirror. They are practically parallel. All these parallel rays converge at the focus, forming a tiny, sharp image. This principle is used in solar furnaces to concentrate sunlight for heating.

Concave Mirror: Object Beyond C

  • Object placed beyond the Centre of Curvature (C).
  • Image is formed between F and C.
  • Image is real, inverted, and diminished.

🎙️ As we bring the object closer, but still beyond C, the image starts to form between F and C. It's still real and inverted, but it's smaller than the object. This is a common scenario in many optical instruments.

Concave Mirror: Object at C

  • Object placed at the Centre of Curvature (C).
  • Image is formed at C itself.
  • Image is real, inverted, and of the same size as the object.

🎙️ This is a special case! When the object is placed exactly at C, the image also forms at C. It's the only position where the image is the same size as the object, while still being real and inverted. This is a key point to remember for ray diagrams.

Concave Mirror: Object Between C & F

  • Object placed between the Centre of Curvature (C) and the Principal Focus (F).
  • Image is formed beyond C.
  • Image is real, inverted, and magnified.

🎙️ Now, as we move the object even closer, between C and F, the image shifts further away from the mirror, beyond C. Crucially, the image becomes magnified, meaning it's larger than the object. This is useful for projectors.

Concave Mirror: Object at F

  • Object placed at the Principal Focus (F).
  • Reflected rays become parallel to the principal axis.
  • Image is formed at infinity (very far away), real, inverted, and highly magnified.

🎙️ This is another unique case, the inverse of object at infinity. When an object is at the focus, the reflected rays become parallel. This means the image forms extremely far away and is incredibly large. This principle is used in car headlights and searchlights.

Concave Mirror: Object Between F & P

  • Object placed between the Principal Focus (F) and the Pole (P).
  • Reflected rays diverge, appearing to come from behind the mirror.
  • Image is formed behind the mirror, virtual, erect, and magnified.

🎙️ This is the only case where a concave mirror forms a virtual and erect image. Because the reflected rays diverge, our eyes trace them back behind the mirror, seeing a magnified, upright image. This is why concave mirrors are used as shaving mirrors or by dentists.

Convex Mirror: Always the Same Image!

  • No matter where the object is placed (except at infinity), a convex mirror always forms a specific type of image.
  • Image is always formed between P and F, behind the mirror.
  • Image is always virtual, erect, and diminished (smaller than the object).

🎙️ Unlike concave mirrors with their many image types, convex mirrors are much simpler. They consistently produce a virtual, upright, and smaller image, located behind the mirror. This predictable behavior is a key reason for their specific applications.

Where Do We Use Concave Mirrors?

  • Shaving mirrors: To see a magnified, erect image of the face.
  • Dentists' mirrors: To see magnified images of teeth.
  • Headlights of cars, searchlights: To get powerful parallel beams of light.
  • Solar furnaces: To concentrate sunlight at the focus to generate heat.

🎙️ Concave mirrors are incredibly versatile! Their ability to magnify objects when close or produce powerful parallel beams when light is at the focus makes them indispensable. From personal grooming to industrial applications, they play a vital role.

Where Do We Use Convex Mirrors?

  • Rear-view mirrors in vehicles: Provide a wider field of view and show objects smaller but erect.

🎙️ Convex mirrors are all about seeing more! Their diverging nature gives them a wide field of view, which is perfect for safety applications like car mirrors or surveillance. They help us see a lot in a small space.

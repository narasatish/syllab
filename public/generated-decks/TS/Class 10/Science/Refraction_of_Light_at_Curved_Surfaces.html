Recap: Laws of Refraction

  • Incident ray, refracted ray, normal lie in same plane
  • Snell's Law: sin i / sin r = constant.

🎙️ We know two main laws: the first states that all rays and the normal are in the same plane. The second, Snell's Law, describes the relationship between the angles of incidence and refraction using the refractive index.

Recap: Refractive Index

  • Ratio of speed of light in vacuum to medium
  • Denoted by 'n' or 'μ'
  • Determines how much light bends.

🎙️ The refractive index is a crucial property of a medium, telling us how much slower light travels in it compared to a vacuum. A higher refractive index means more bending.

🎙️ Let's define some important points. The Pole (P) is the exact center of the curved surface itself. The Centre of Curvature (C) is the center of the imaginary sphere from which the curved surface was cut.

Refraction at a Single Spherical Surface

  • Light bends as it enters/leaves
  • Follows Snell's law locally
  • Image formation depends on object position.

🎙️ When light rays strike a single spherical surface, they refract according to Snell's law at each point. This refraction leads to the formation of images, just like with flat surfaces, but with more complexity.

The Refraction Formula

  • n2/v - n1/u = (n2 - n1)/R
  • n1: refractive index of medium 1 (object side)
  • n2: refractive index of medium 2 (image side)
  • u: object distance, v: image distance, R: radius of curvature.

🎙️ This is the general formula for refraction at a single spherical surface. It relates the object distance, image distance, refractive indices of the two media, and the radius of curvature. We'll use this formula to solve problems.

Cartesian Sign Convention

  • Pole (P) is the origin (0,0)
  • Incident light travels left to right (positive)
  • Distances measured right of P are positive, left are negative
  • Heights above principal axis are positive, below are negative.

🎙️ To apply the refraction formula correctly, we need a consistent sign convention. We use the Cartesian system, where the pole is the origin, and distances are measured from it, following specific rules for positive and negative values.

1Pole is origin
2Incident light direction
3Distances left/right
4Heights up/down

Focal Point & Focal Length: Convex Lens

  • Principal Focus (F): Point where parallel rays converge
  • Focal Length (f): Distance from optical centre to Principal Focus
  • Has a real principal focus.

🎙️ For a convex lens, parallel rays of light, after refraction, converge at a specific point on the principal axis called the Principal Focus (F). The distance from the optical centre to F is its focal length (f).

🎯
Convergence Point

Focal Point & Focal Length: Concave Lens

  • Principal Focus (F): Point from which diverged rays appear to come
  • Focal Length (f): Distance from optical centre to Principal Focus
  • Has a virtual principal focus.

🎙️ For a concave lens, parallel rays diverge after refraction. If we extend these diverging rays backward, they appear to originate from a point on the principal axis called the Principal Focus (F). This is a virtual focus.

Ray Diagram Rules: Convex Lens

  • Ray parallel to principal axis passes through F
  • Ray passing through F becomes parallel
  • Ray passing through optical centre goes undeviated.

🎙️ To draw ray diagrams for convex lenses, we follow three simple rules. These rules help us locate the image formed by the lens for any object position.

1Parallel -> Focus
2Focus -> Parallel
3Optical Centre -> Undeviated

Ray Diagram Rules: Concave Lens

  • Ray parallel to principal axis appears to diverge from F
  • Ray directed towards F becomes parallel
  • Ray passing through optical centre goes undeviated.

🎙️ Similarly, for concave lenses, there are specific rules for drawing ray diagrams. These rules help us understand how light diverges and where the virtual image is formed.

2Towards F -> Parallel
3Optical Centre -> Undeviated

  • Atomic Number: 6
  • Electronic Configuration: 2, 4
  • Valency: 4

🎙️ Carbon has an atomic number of 6, meaning it has 6 protons. Its electronic configuration shows 4 valence electrons, which makes it very versatile in forming bonds with other atoms.

Covalent Bonding: Carbon's Way

  • Carbon forms bonds by sharing electrons.
  • Neither gains nor loses electrons.
  • Forms strong covalent bonds.

🎙️ Unlike ionic bonds where electrons are transferred, carbon prefers to share its valence electrons with other atoms. This electron sharing leads to the formation of very stable covalent bonds.

Example: Methane (CH₄)

  • Simplest carbon compound.
  • One carbon atom shares electrons with four hydrogen atoms.
  • Each bond is a single covalent bond.

🎙️ Methane, often called marsh gas, is a perfect example of covalent bonding. The carbon atom sits at the center, forming a stable structure by sharing one electron with each of the four hydrogen atoms.

  • Different forms of the same element.
  • They have different physical properties.
  • But the same chemical properties.

🎙️ Allotropes are fascinating! Imagine having different materials made of only one type of atom, but they look and feel completely different. Carbon exhibits this property beautifully with its allotropes.

Diamond: The Hardest Allotrope

  • Each carbon atom bonded to four others.
  • Forms a rigid 3D network structure.
  • Extremely hard, used in cutting tools.

🎙️ Diamond is known for its incredible hardness and brilliance. This is due to its strong, tightly packed tetrahedral structure where every carbon atom is covalently bonded to four others, forming a giant molecule.

✨
Sparkling Diamond
8 / 25

Graphite: The Soft Allotrope

  • Carbon atoms arranged in hexagonal layers.
  • Layers can slide over each other.
  • Soft, slippery, and good conductor of electricity.

🎙️ Graphite is what we find in our pencil leads. Its layered structure allows the layers to slide past each other, making it soft and a good lubricant. It's also unique among non-metals for conducting electricity.

✏️
Pencil Lead

Fullerenes: The Cage-like Allotrope

  • Spherical molecules, e.g., C₆₀ (Buckminsterfullerene).
  • Resemble a soccer ball structure.
  • Used in nanotechnology and medicine.

🎙️ Fullerenes are relatively new discoveries, with C₆₀ being the most famous. Its unique cage-like structure makes it useful in various advanced applications, from drug delivery to superconductors.

Catenation: Carbon's Self-Linking

  • Ability of carbon atoms to form bonds with other carbon atoms.
  • Forms long chains, branched chains, and rings.
  • Leads to large and complex molecules.

🎙️ Catenation is carbon's superpower! It can link up with itself repeatedly, forming incredibly long chains, intricate branched structures, and even stable rings. No other element does this as extensively as carbon.

Tetravalency: Four Bonds

  • Carbon has a valency of four.
  • It can form four covalent bonds.
  • Bonds with H, O, N, S, Cl, etc.

🎙️ With four valence electrons, carbon can form four covalent bonds. This allows it to bond with a wide variety of other elements, not just itself, leading to an immense diversity of organic compounds.

Hydrocarbons: Carbon & Hydrogen

  • Compounds made only of carbon and hydrogen.
  • Building blocks of many organic compounds.
  • Examples: Methane, Ethane, Propane.

🎙️ Hydrocarbons are the simplest organic compounds, forming the backbone for many more complex molecules. They are also important as fuels, like the petrol and LPG we use every day.

⛽
Fuel for Life

Saturated Hydrocarbons (Alkanes)

  • Contain only single bonds between carbon atoms.
  • General formula: CnH2n+2.
  • Relatively unreactive.

🎙️ Saturated hydrocarbons, also known as alkanes, have all their carbon atoms connected by single bonds. This 'saturation' means they have the maximum number of hydrogen atoms possible, making them quite stable.

🔗
Single Bond

Unsaturated Hydrocarbons

  • Contain double or triple bonds between carbon atoms.
  • Alkenes (double bonds): CnH2n.
  • Alkynes (triple bonds): CnH2n-2.

🎙️ Unsaturated hydrocarbons, alkenes and alkynes, are characterized by the presence of double or triple bonds between carbon atoms. These multiple bonds make them more reactive than alkanes.

  • Attached to hydrocarbon chains.
  • Give specific reactivity to the molecule.

🎙️ Functional groups are like special attachments that give a hydrocarbon molecule its unique chemical personality. They determine how a compound will react in chemical processes, even if the carbon chain is different.

Alcohol Functional Group (-OH)

  • Hydroxyl group (-OH) attached to a carbon chain.
  • Example: Ethanol (CH₃CH₂OH).
  • Found in alcoholic beverages and disinfectants.

🎙️ The -OH group is characteristic of alcohols. Ethanol, a common alcohol, is used in many industries and also as a solvent. Its presence greatly changes the properties of the hydrocarbon it's attached to.

Carboxylic Acid Functional Group (-COOH)

  • Carboxyl group (-COOH) attached to a carbon chain.
  • Example: Ethanoic acid (CH₃COOH).
  • Known for their acidic properties.

🎙️ Carboxylic acids, containing the -COOH group, are responsible for the sour taste of many fruits like lemons. Ethanoic acid, or acetic acid, is the main component of vinegar, a common household item.

Chemical Properties: Combustion

  • Carbon compounds burn in oxygen.
  • Produce carbon dioxide, water, and heat.
  • Example: Methane burning in a stove.

🎙️ Combustion is a common chemical property of most carbon compounds. When they burn in sufficient oxygen, they release energy as heat and light, forming carbon dioxide and water. This is why they are excellent fuels.

Substitution Reaction (Alkanes)

  • One atom or group replaces another.
  • Typical for saturated hydrocarbons (alkanes).
  • Example: Methane reacting with chlorine in sunlight.

🎙️ Alkanes are quite stable, so they usually undergo substitution reactions. Under specific conditions, like in the presence of sunlight, a hydrogen atom can be replaced by another atom, such as chlorine.

Addition Reaction (Unsaturated)

  • Atoms are added across a double or triple bond.
  • Typical for unsaturated hydrocarbons (alkenes/alkynes).
  • Example: Hydrogenation of vegetable oils.

🎙️ Unsaturated hydrocarbons are more reactive due to their double or triple bonds. They readily undergo addition reactions where atoms like hydrogen or halogens simply add across the multiple bond, converting it to a single bond.

Soaps and Detergents: Cleaning Action

  • Soaps are sodium/potassium salts of long-chain carboxylic acids.
  • Have a hydrophilic (water-loving) head and a hydrophobic (water-hating) tail.
  • Form micelles to trap dirt and grease.

🎙️ Soaps and detergents are fascinating carbon compounds that help us clean. Their unique structure allows them to interact with both water and oil, forming tiny structures called micelles that lift dirt away.

What is Heredity?

  • Transfer of traits from parents to offspring
  • Through genetic material (DNA/genes)
  • Ensures continuity and similarity within a species

🎙️ Heredity is simply the process where characteristics or traits are passed from one generation to the next. These traits are carried by tiny units called genes, ensuring that offspring generally resemble their parents.

Why are we all Unique? (Variation)

  • Can be subtle (e.g., hair shade) or significant (e.g., height)

🎙️ Even within the same family, or among friends, you notice differences in height, skin colour, or hair type. These differences are called variations. They make each individual unique and are crucial for the long-term survival of a species.

Monohybrid Cross: The Experiment

  • Cross between two parents differing in ONE trait
  • E.g., Tall pea plant x Dwarf pea plant
  • Revealed dominant and recessive patterns

🎙️ In a monohybrid cross, Mendel took a tall pea plant and crossed it with a dwarf pea plant. Surprisingly, all the offspring in the first generation (F1) were tall. But when he self-pollinated the F1 plants, the second generation (F2) showed both tall and dwarf plants in a 3:1 ratio.

1P Generation (Tall x Dwarf)
2F1 Generation (All Tall)
3F2 Generation (Tall & Dwarf in 3:1 ratio)

What You See vs. What's Inside

  • Phenotype: Observable characteristics (e.g., tall)
  • Genotype: Genetic makeup of an organism (e.g., TT or Tt)
  • Two different genotypes can result in the same phenotype

🎙️ Phenotype is what you can physically see – like a plant being tall or having purple flowers. Genotype, on the other hand, refers to the actual genetic code or combination of genes responsible for that trait. A tall plant might have two 'tall' genes (TT) or one 'tall' and one 'dwarf' gene (Tt).

Phenotype (Tall Plant)
Genotype (TT or Tt)

Stronger vs. Hidden Traits

  • Dominant trait: Expressed even with one copy (e.g., Tallness)
  • Recessive trait: Expressed only with two copies (e.g., Dwarfness)
  • Dominant 'masks' the recessive trait

🎙️ In Mendel's experiments, 'tallness' was a dominant trait because it appeared in the F1 generation even when paired with a 'dwarf' gene. The 'dwarf' trait was recessive, meaning it only showed up if an individual inherited two copies of the 'dwarf' gene.

How is Gender Decided?

  • Determined by sex chromosomes (X and Y)
  • Females have XX chromosomes
  • Males have XY chromosomes
  • Father determines the sex of the child

🎙️ In humans, sex is determined by specific chromosomes. Females have two X chromosomes (XX), while males have one X and one Y chromosome (XY). The mother always contributes an X chromosome, so it's the father's sperm (carrying either an X or a Y) that determines if the child will be a girl or a boy.

1Mother (XX) contributes X egg
2Father (XY) contributes X or Y sperm
3XX (Girl) or XY (Boy)

Learning a Skill vs. Having a Birthmark

  • Acquired: Developed during lifetime (e.g., learning to swim)
  • Inherited: Passed from parents (e.g., eye colour)
  • Only inherited traits are relevant for evolution

🎙️ Think about it: you can learn to ride a bicycle or get a tattoo, but you won't pass these 'acquired' traits to your children. However, your eye colour or blood group are 'inherited' traits, passed down through genes, and these are the ones relevant to evolution.

Acquired vs. Inherited: Key Differences

  • Acquired: Not passed to offspring, caused by environment/experience
  • Inherited: Passed to offspring, caused by genes/DNA
  • Acquired traits do not change the genetic makeup for future generations

🎙️ Acquired traits are changes an organism develops during its lifetime due to environmental influences or personal experiences, like building strong muscles or getting a tan. Inherited traits, on the other hand, are coded in our DNA and are passed directly from parents to offspring, like natural hair colour or blood type.

How New Species Emerge (Speciation)

  • Process by which new species arise
  • From existing species, over long periods
  • Due to reproductive isolation and accumulated differences

🎙️ Have you ever wondered how there came to be so many different kinds of birds, animals, and plants? Speciation is the process where one species splits into two or more distinct species that can no longer interbreed.

What Causes Speciation?

  • Genetic drift (random changes in gene frequency)
  • Natural selection (survival of the fittest)
  • Geographical isolation (physical barriers like mountains)
  • Reproductive isolation (inability to interbreed)

🎙️ Speciation can happen due to various factors. Imagine a population of beetles separated by a river. Over long periods, they might evolve differently due to natural selection and random genetic changes, eventually becoming so different they can no longer mate, thus forming two new species.

Homologous & Analogous Organs

  • Homologous: Similar structure, different function (e.g., human arm, bat wing)
  • Analogous: Different structure, similar function (e.g., bird wing, insect wing)
  • Both types provide evidence of evolutionary relationships and adaptations

🎙️ Homologous organs, like the arm of a human, the wing of a bat, and the flipper of a whale, share a similar basic structure despite having different functions, suggesting a common ancestor. Analogous organs, like the wings of a bird and an insect, serve the same function but have different underlying structures, showing similar adaptations in different evolutionary lines.

Homologous (common ancestor)
Analogous (similar function, different origin)

Avoid These Common Mistakes!

  • Confusing acquired and inherited traits (only inherited affects evolution)
  • Misunderstanding dominant vs. recessive (dominant masks recessive)
  • Thinking evolution means individuals change (populations evolve)

🎙️ It's easy to mix up acquired and inherited traits – remember only inherited traits are passed on. Also, remember dominant traits show up even with one copy, while recessive needs two. And importantly, individuals don't evolve; populations do over generations.

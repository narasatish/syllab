What is a Chemical Change?

  • A process where new substances with new properties are formed.
  • Bonds are broken and new bonds are formed.
  • Often irreversible.

🎙️ Remember how we learned about changes around us? A chemical change is when the original substances transform into completely different ones. Think about burning paper – you can't turn the ash back into paper, right? That's a chemical change!

Metals form positively charged ions by losing electrons. Reactive metals react with oxygen

How Do We Know a Chemical Change Happened?

  • Change in state, colour, or temperature.
  • Evolution of a gas (bubbles).
  • Formation of a precipitate (solid).

🎙️ When a chemical change occurs, we often see clear signs. For example, when you mix baking soda and vinegar, you see bubbles – that's a gas being released! Or when iron rusts, its colour changes from silvery grey to reddish-brown.

Physical vs. Chemical Change

  • Physical: No new substances formed (e.g., melting ice).
  • Chemical: New substances formed (e.g., burning wood).

🎙️ It's important to distinguish between these two. Melting an ice cube is physical – it's still water. But burning wood is chemical because you get ash and smoke, which are completely different from wood.

What is a Chemical Equation?

  • A symbolic representation of a chemical reaction.
  • Uses chemical formulae and symbols.
  • Shows reactants and products.

🎙️ Instead of writing long sentences to describe a reaction, chemists use a shorthand. This shorthand is called a chemical equation. It's like a special language that quickly tells us what substances are reacting and what new substances are being formed.

✍️
Reaction in symbols

Reactants and Products: The Key Players

  • Reactants: Substances that undergo chemical change.
  • Products: New substances formed after the reaction.
  • Arrow (→) separates them, pointing towards products.

🎙️ Think of it like cooking: the ingredients you start with are the 'reactants', and the delicious dish you end up with is the 'product'. The arrow in the equation shows the direction of the reaction, from reactants to products.

Writing a Word Equation

  • Step 1: Identify reactants and products.
  • Step 2: Write reactants on the left, products on the right.
  • Step 3: Use a '+' sign between multiple reactants/products.
  • Step 4: Use an arrow (→) in between.

🎙️ Before we jump into complex symbols, let's start with words. A word equation is a simple way to represent a reaction using the names of the substances. It helps us clearly understand who is reacting with whom.

Introduction to Chemical Formulae

  • Shorthand notation for elements and compounds.
  • Represents the type and number of atoms.
  • e.g., O₂ for Oxygen, H₂O for Water.

🎙️ To make our equations even more concise and universally understood, we use chemical formulae. These formulae tell us exactly which elements are present in a substance and in what proportions. It's like the DNA of a molecule!

The Law of Conservation of Mass

  • Mass can neither be created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction.
  • Total mass of reactants = Total mass of products.
  • Number of atoms of each element must be equal on both sides.

🎙️ This is a fundamental law in chemistry, given by Antoine Lavoisier. It means that during a reaction, atoms are simply rearranged, not lost or gained. This law is the core reason why we need to balance chemical equations.

Unbalanced vs. Balanced Equations

  • Unbalanced: Unequal number of atoms of elements on both sides.
  • Balanced: Equal number of atoms of each element on both sides.

🎙️ Look at our magnesium example: Mg + O₂ → MgO. On the left, we have 2 oxygen atoms, but only 1 on the right. This equation is unbalanced. To obey the Law of Conservation of Mass, we must balance it.

Why Do We Balance Chemical Equations?

  • To satisfy the Law of Conservation of Mass.
  • To accurately represent the stoichiometry of a reaction.
  • Essential for quantitative calculations in chemistry.

🎙️ Balancing isn't just a mathematical exercise; it's crucial for understanding the true nature of a reaction. It tells us the exact ratio in which reactants combine and products are formed, which is vital for experiments and industrial processes.

Steps to Balance a Chemical Equation (Hit & Trial)

  • Step 1: Write the skeletal equation.
  • Step 2: List atoms of each element on both sides.
  • Step 3: Start balancing elements that appear only once.
  • Step 4: Balance polyatomic ions as a single unit (if any).
  • Step 5: Balance H and O atoms last.
  • Step 6: Check the balance.

🎙️ The Hit and Trial method involves systematically adjusting the coefficients in front of the formulae until the number of atoms of each element is equal on both sides. It requires careful observation and a bit of practice.

1Skeletal Eq.
2List Atoms
3Balance Unique
4Balance H/O
5Final Check

What Information Does an Equation Give?

  • Names of reactants and products.
  • Formulae of reactants and products.
  • Relative number of moles/molecules involved.

🎙️ A balanced chemical equation is a treasure trove of information. It tells us not just what reacts and what forms, but also in what proportions they do so. This is crucial for predicting reaction outcomes and quantities.

More Informative Equations: State Symbols

  • Represent physical states of reactants and products.
  • (s) for solid, (l) for liquid, (g) for gas, (aq) for aqueous solution.

🎙️ To give even more detail, we add state symbols. For example, if a substance is dissolved in water, we write (aq) for 'aqueous'. This helps us visualize the reaction more accurately, whether it's a solid dissolving or a gas evolving.

More Informative Equations: Reaction Conditions

  • Temperature, pressure, catalyst shown above/below arrow.
  • e.g., Δ (delta) for heat, specific temperature/pressure values.

🎙️ Some reactions only occur under specific conditions. We indicate these conditions, like heating, high pressure, or the presence of a catalyst, above or below the arrow. This makes the equation a complete recipe for the reaction.

Recap: Key Takeaways

  • Chemical equations represent reactions symbolically.
  • Reactants become products (A + B → C + D).
  • Equations must be balanced (Conservation of Mass).
  • State symbols and conditions add more information.

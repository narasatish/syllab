What are Acids?

  • Sour in taste.
  • Turn blue litmus red.
  • Corrosive in nature.
  • Release H⁺ ions in water.

🎙️ Acids are substances we often encounter. Think of the sour taste of a lemon – that's due to acids! They have distinct properties, like changing the color of certain indicators and releasing hydrogen ions when dissolved in water.

Common Acid Examples

  • Hydrochloric Acid (HCl) - in stomach.
  • Sulphuric Acid (H₂SO₄) - car batteries.
  • Nitric Acid (HNO₃) - fertilizers.
  • Acetic Acid (CH₃COOH) - vinegar.

🎙️ Acids are everywhere! From the acid that helps digest our food in the stomach to the vinegar we use in cooking, and even in car batteries, acids play crucial roles. Each has its own specific use and strength.

Strong vs. Weak Acids

  • Strong acids ionize completely in water.
  • Weak acids ionize partially in water.
  • Strong acids are more corrosive.
  • Weak acids are less harmful.

🎙️ Not all acids are created equal. Strong acids, like Hydrochloric acid, completely break down into ions in water, making them very reactive. Weak acids, like the one in vinegar, only partially ionize, making them milder.

What are Bases?

  • Bitter in taste.
  • Soapy to touch.
  • Turn red litmus blue.
  • Release OH⁻ ions in water.

🎙️ Now, let's talk about bases. Unlike acids, bases often have a bitter taste and feel slippery, like soap. They are characterized by their ability to release hydroxide ions when dissolved in water, which gives them their unique properties.

🎙️ Bases are also very common in our daily lives. From cleaning products like soap and window cleaner to substances used in construction like whitewash, bases are essential. They help us clean and neutralize acids effectively.

What are Indicators?

  • Substances that show different colors.
  • Help identify acids or bases.
  • Don't change the chemical nature.
  • Just signal pH changes.

🎙️ How do we tell if something is an acid or a base without tasting it, which can be dangerous? We use indicators! These special substances change color when exposed to acids or bases, giving us a visual clue about their nature.

  • Litmus: most common, from lichens.

🎙️ Nature provides us with many indicators. Litmus paper, derived from lichens, is a classic example. Turmeric, a spice we use every day, also acts as an indicator, turning red in basic solutions. Isn't that fascinating?

Olfactory Indicators

The pH Scale

  • Measures the strength of acids or bases.
  • Ranges from 0 to 14.
  • Lower pH = more acidic.
  • Higher pH = more basic.

🎙️ While indicators tell us if a substance is acidic or basic, the pH scale gives us a precise measure of its strength. It's like a ruler for acidity and basicity, ranging from 0 to 14, where 7 is neutral.

pH scale (0-14) measures acidity and alkalinity. Acids < 7 (H+ ions), bases > 7 (OH− ions)
14 / 25

pH Values: Acidic, Neutral, Basic

  • pH < 7: Acidic solutions (e.g., lemon juice, gastric acid).
  • pH = 7: Neutral solutions (e.g., pure water).
  • pH > 7: Basic solutions (e.g., baking soda, soap).

🎙️ On the pH scale, anything below 7 is acidic, with lower numbers indicating stronger acids. A pH of exactly 7 is neutral, like pure water. And anything above 7 is basic, with higher numbers meaning stronger bases. It's a clear way to classify substances.

Neutral (pH 7)
Basic (pH 8-14)

  • Forms salt and water.
  • Heat is usually released.
  • Example: HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O.

🎙️ When an acid meets a base, a very important reaction occurs: neutralization! They react to form a salt and water, often releasing heat. This reaction is fundamental in chemistry and has many practical applications, like treating indigestion.

What are Salts?

  • Ionic compounds formed from neutralization.
  • Composed of a cation (from base) and an anion (from acid).
  • Often crystalline solids.
  • Many are soluble in water.

🎙️ After neutralization, we get salts! These are ionic compounds, meaning they are made of positively and negatively charged ions. Table salt, or Sodium Chloride, is the most common example, but there are many different types of salts with diverse uses.

Families of Salts

  • Derived from specific acids or bases.
  • Chloride salts (from HCl).
  • Sulphate salts (from H₂SO₄).
  • Carbonate salts (from H₂CO₃).

🎙️ Salts can be grouped into families based on the acid or base they originate from. For instance, salts formed from hydrochloric acid are called chlorides, and those from sulfuric acid are called sulphates. This helps us classify and understand their properties.

Recap: Acids, Bases and Salts

  • Acids are sour, release H⁺, turn blue litmus red.
  • Bases are bitter, release OH⁻, turn red litmus blue.
  • Indicators show pH changes (color/smell).
  • pH scale measures strength (0-14).
  • Neutralization forms salt + water.
  • Salts have diverse uses (NaCl, NaOH, Bleaching Powder, Baking Soda, Washing Soda).

What is Probability?

  • A measure of how likely an event is to occur.
  • Expressed as a number between 0 and 1.
  • 0 means impossible, 1 means certain.

🎙️ Formally, probability is a numerical value that tells us the chance of something happening. If an event has a probability of 0, it will never happen. If it has a probability of 1, it will definitely happen. Most events fall somewhere in between.

Understanding Experiments & Outcomes

  • An 'experiment' is an action with observable results.
  • Example: Rolling a die.
  • An 'outcome' is a result of an experiment.

🎙️ In probability, when we talk about an 'experiment', we mean any process that produces well-defined results. Rolling a die is an experiment. Each number that can come up, like 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, or 6, is an 'outcome' of that experiment.

Sample Space: All Possibilities

  • The set of ALL possible outcomes of an experiment.
  • Represented by 'S'.
  • Example: Rolling a die, S = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}.

🎙️ The sample space is like a complete list of everything that *can* happen in an experiment. If you're tossing a coin, the sample space is {Heads, Tails}. If you're drawing a card, the sample space is all 52 cards. It's crucial to identify the sample space correctly.

Event: A Specific Outcome

  • One or more outcomes of an experiment.
  • A subset of the Sample Space.
  • Example: Getting an even number when rolling a die.

🎙️ An event is what we are interested in. It's a specific result or a group of results from our experiment. In our die roll example, 'getting an even number' is an event. The outcomes that satisfy this event would be {2, 4, 6}.

Favourable Outcomes: What We Want

  • Outcomes that satisfy a specific event.
  • Count of these outcomes is 'n(E)'.
  • Example: Event 'getting an even number' on a die roll.

🎙️ From the sample space, the outcomes that match our event are called 'favourable outcomes'. For the event 'getting an even number' when rolling a die, the favourable outcomes are {2, 4, 6}. So, the number of favourable outcomes, n(E), is 3.

The Probability Formula

  • P(E) = (Number of Favourable Outcomes) / (Total Number of Outcomes)
  • P(E) = n(E) / n(S)
  • This is the core formula for calculating probability!

🎙️ This is the most important formula in basic probability. To find the probability of any event, you simply divide the number of ways that event can happen by the total number of possible outcomes. Remember this formula well!

Worked Example 1: Coin Toss

  • Question: What is the probability of getting a Head when tossing a coin?
  • Step 1: Identify Sample Space (S)
  • Step 2: Identify Event (E) and Favourable Outcomes (n(E))
  • Step 3: Apply the Formula

🎙️ Let's apply the formula. For a coin toss, the total outcomes are Heads and Tails, so n(S)=2. If we want a Head, there's only one way, so n(E)=1. Thus, the probability is 1/2.

1S = {H, T}, n(S) = 2
2E = {H}, n(E) = 1
3P(Head) = n(E) / n(S) = 1/2

Worked Example 2: Rolling a Die

  • Step 1: Sample Space (S) & n(S)
  • Step 2: Event (E) 'getting an even number' & n(E)
  • Step 3: Calculate P(E)

🎙️ Here, our total outcomes are 6. The even numbers are 2, 4, and 6, so there are 3 favourable outcomes. Dividing 3 by 6 gives us a probability of 1/2. Simple, right?

1S = {1,2,3,4,5,6}, n(S) = 6
2E = {2,4,6}, n(E) = 3
3P(Even) = n(E) / n(S) = 3/6 = 1/2

Range of Probability: 0 to 1

  • The probability of any event (E) is always between 0 and 1, inclusive.
  • 0 ≤ P(E) ≤ 1
  • It can never be negative or greater than 1.

🎙️ This is a fundamental property of probability. A probability of 0 means an event will never happen, and 1 means it will always happen. Any other probability value, like 0.5 or 0.75, indicates a chance of happening.

Impossible Event: Probability = 0

  • An event that cannot happen.
  • The number of favourable outcomes is 0.
  • Example: Getting a 7 when rolling a standard die.

🎙️ If an event is impossible, its probability is 0. For instance, you can never roll a 7 with a single standard die, so the probability of that event is 0/6, which is 0.

Sure/Certain Event: Probability = 1

  • An event that is certain to happen.
  • The number of favourable outcomes equals the total number of outcomes.
  • Example: Getting a number less than 7 when rolling a die.

🎙️ On the other hand, if an event is sure to happen, its probability is 1. When you roll a die, you are guaranteed to get a number less than 7. All 6 outcomes are favourable, so the probability is 6/6, which is 1.

Complementary Events: E and Not E

  • If E is an event, then 'not E' (or E') is its complementary event.
  • P(E) + P(not E) = 1
  • This means P(not E) = 1 - P(E).

🎙️ This is a very useful concept. If you know the probability of an event happening, you can easily find the probability of it *not* happening by subtracting from 1. For example, if the probability of rain is 0.3, then the probability of no rain is 1 - 0.3 = 0.7.

➕
Event + Not Event

Worked Example 3: Using Complementary Events

  • Question: What is the probability of NOT getting a 6 when rolling a die?
  • Step 1: Find P(E) for 'getting a 6'.
  • Step 2: Use the complementary event formula.
  • P(not 6) = 1 - P(6)

🎙️ Here, the event E is 'getting a 6', and its probability is 1/6. The complementary event is 'not getting a 6'. Using the formula, we get 1 minus 1/6, which is 5/6. This is much faster than counting all outcomes that are not 6!

1P(6) = 1/6
2P(not 6) = 1 - P(6)
3P(not 6) = 1 - 1/6 = 5/6

Types of Events: Elementary Event

  • An event having only ONE outcome from the sample space.
  • Example: Getting a 'Head' in a coin toss.
  • Example: Getting '3' when rolling a die.

🎙️ An elementary event is the simplest kind of event. It's just one specific result from your experiment. For example, if you roll a die, getting a '1' is an elementary event. Getting a '2' is another elementary event, and so on.

Types of Events: Compound Event

🎙️ A compound event, as the name suggests, is made up of multiple elementary events. When we asked for the probability of getting an even number on a die, that was a compound event because it included outcomes 2, 4, and 6.

Worked Example 4: Drawing a King

  • Question: What is the probability of drawing a King from a well-shuffled deck of 52 cards?
  • Step 1: Total outcomes n(S).
  • Step 2: Favourable outcomes n(E) 'drawing a King'.
  • Step 3: Calculate P(E).

🎙️ For this problem, our sample space is 52 cards. There are 4 Kings in the deck (King of Hearts, Diamonds, Clubs, Spades). So, the probability is 4 divided by 52, which simplifies to 1/13.

1n(S) = 52
2There are 4 Kings (one in each suit), so n(E) = 4
3P(King) = 4/52 = 1/13

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Incorrectly identifying the Sample Space (n(S)).
  • Miscounting Favourable Outcomes (n(E)).
  • Forgetting to simplify the final fraction.
  • Stating probability as a value outside [0, 1].

🎙️ Students often make mistakes by not correctly listing all possible outcomes or by miscounting the specific outcomes for the event. Always double-check your sample space and favourable outcomes. Also, remember to simplify your fractions and ensure your answer is between 0 and 1.

Exam Tips for Probability

  • Read the question carefully to identify the experiment and event.
  • Clearly write down n(S) and n(E).
  • Show your formula P(E) = n(E)/n(S).

🎙️ In exams, showing your steps is crucial. Always define your sample space and favourable outcomes. Write down the formula, substitute the values, and then calculate the final answer. Simplification is often required for full marks.

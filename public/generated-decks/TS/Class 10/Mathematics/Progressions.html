What is a Sequence?

  • An ordered list of numbers.
  • Each number is called a 'term'.
  • Follows a specific rule or pattern.

🎙️ A sequence is simply a list of numbers arranged in a particular order. Think of it like items in a line, where each item has its own position. This order is not random; it follows a definite rule.

What is a Series?

  • The sum of the terms of a sequence.
  • Indicated by '+' signs between terms.
  • Represents the total value.

🎙️ While a sequence is just a list, a series is what you get when you add up all the numbers in that sequence. For example, if your sequence is 1, 2, 3, then the series would be 1 + 2 + 3, which equals 6.

Introduction to Arithmetic Progression (AP)

  • A special type of sequence.
  • Each term differs from its preceding term by a constant value.
  • Think of a constant 'jump' or 'fall'.

🎙️ An Arithmetic Progression, or AP, is like taking steps of the same size. If you walk forward, each step covers the same distance. If you walk backward, it's the same. The difference between any two consecutive terms is always the same.

The Common Difference (d)

  • The constant difference between consecutive terms.
  • Found by subtracting any term from its succeeding term.
  • Can be positive, negative, or zero.

🎙️ This constant value is called the 'common difference', denoted by 'd'. To find 'd', you simply take any term and subtract the term right before it. For example, if the sequence is 2, 5, 8, 11, then d = 5-2 = 3, or 8-5 = 3.

2a₁ + d
3a₁ + 2d
4a₁ + 3d

Identifying an AP: Examples

  • Is 5, 10, 15, 20, ... an AP? Yes, d = 5.
  • Is 10, 7, 4, 1, ... an AP? Yes, d = -3.
  • Is 2, 4, 8, 16, ... an AP? No, differences vary.

🎙️ Let's practice identifying APs. For the first example, the difference is always 5. For the second, it's always -3. But for the third example, the differences are 2, 4, 8, which are not constant. So it's not an AP.

General Form of an AP

  • If 'a' is the first term and 'd' is the common difference:
  • The AP is: a, a+d, a+2d, a+3d, ...
  • Each term is generated by adding 'd' to the previous one.

🎙️ This is the blueprint for any Arithmetic Progression. 'a' represents the very first number in our sequence. Then, we keep adding 'd' repeatedly to get the subsequent terms. This structure makes APs very predictable.

Finding the nth Term of an AP (a_n)

  • The formula to find any term in an AP without listing them all.
  • a_n = a + (n - 1)d
  • Where 'a' is the first term, 'n' is the term number, 'd' is the common difference.

🎙️ Imagine you need to find the 100th term of an AP. You wouldn't want to list all 99 terms! This formula is a shortcut. It tells you exactly what the 'n'th term will be, given the first term and the common difference.

🔍
Locating a specific term

Worked Example: Finding the 5th Term

  • AP: 2, 5, 8, 11, ...
  • Here, a = 2, d = 3.
  • To find a_5: a_5 = a + (5-1)d = 2 + (4)3 = 2 + 12 = 14.

🎙️ Let's apply the formula. We have an AP starting with 2, and a common difference of 3. We want the 5th term. Plugging these values into our formula, we find that the 5th term is 14. You can verify this by manually extending the sequence.

Worked Example: Which Term is 78?

  • AP: 3, 8, 13, 18, ...
  • Here, a = 3, d = 5. We want to find 'n' for a_n = 78.
  • 78 = 3 + (n - 1)5 => 75 = (n - 1)5 => 15 = n - 1 => n = 16.

🎙️ This is a slightly different type of problem. We're given the value of a term, and we need to find its position 'n'. We use the same formula, but this time we solve for 'n'. After rearranging the equation, we find that 78 is the 16th term.

Sum of First n Terms of an AP (S_n)

  • Find the total sum of a certain number of terms in an AP.
  • Legendary story of young Gauss summing numbers from 1 to 100.
  • S_n = n/2 [2a + (n - 1)d]

🎙️ Sometimes we need to find the sum of many terms in an AP, not just a single term. This formula helps us do that quickly. It's said that the mathematician Gauss discovered a trick to sum numbers from 1 to 100 very fast, which is related to this formula.

Another Sum Formula (using last term)

  • If the last term (l or a_n) is known:
  • S_n = n/2 [a + l]
  • This formula is often simpler when 'l' is given.

🎙️ This is an alternative, often more convenient, formula for the sum of an AP. If you already know the first term 'a' and the last term 'l' (which is the 'n'th term), you can use this simpler version. It works because the sum of pairs from opposite ends of an AP is always constant.

Worked Example: Sum of First 10 Terms

  • AP: 1, 3, 5, 7, ...
  • Here, a = 1, d = 2, n = 10.
  • S_10 = 10/2 [2(1) + (10 - 1)2] = 5 [2 + 9(2)] = 5 [2 + 18] = 5 [20] = 100.

🎙️ Let's calculate the sum of the first 10 terms of this AP. We identify 'a', 'd', and 'n'. Then, we substitute these values into the sum formula and perform the calculations carefully. The sum comes out to be 100.

Introduction to Geometric Progression (GP)

  • Another special type of sequence.
  • Each term is obtained by multiplying the previous term by a constant value.
  • Think of growth by multiplication.

🎙️ Geometric Progression, or GP, is different from AP. Instead of adding a constant difference, we multiply by a constant value. Imagine a population of bacteria doubling every hour – that's a GP!

📈

The Common Ratio (r)

  • The constant factor by which terms are multiplied.
  • Found by dividing any term by its preceding term.
  • Can be positive or negative, but not zero.

🎙️ This constant multiplier is called the 'common ratio', denoted by 'r'. To find 'r', you divide any term by the term immediately before it. For example, if the sequence is 2, 6, 18, 54, then r = 6/2 = 3, or 18/6 = 3.

Identifying a GP: Examples

  • Is 3, 6, 12, 24, ... a GP? Yes, r = 2.
  • Is 100, 50, 25, 12.5, ... a GP? Yes, r = 1/2.
  • Is 1, 2, 3, 4, ... a GP? No, ratios vary.

🎙️ Let's practice identifying GPs. In the first example, each term is twice the previous. In the second, each term is half the previous. But the third example, which is an AP, does not have a constant ratio, so it's not a GP.

General Form of a GP

  • If 'a' is the first term and 'r' is the common ratio:
  • The GP is: a, ar, ar², ar³, ...
  • Each term is generated by multiplying the previous one by 'r'.

🎙️ Similar to AP, GP also has a general form. 'a' is the first term, and 'r' is the common ratio. We keep multiplying by 'r' to get the next term. This multiplicative pattern is what defines a Geometric Progression.

Finding the nth Term of a GP (a_n)

  • The formula to find any term in a GP.
  • a_n = ar^(n-1)
  • Where 'a' is the first term, 'n' is the term number, 'r' is the common ratio.

🎙️ Just like AP, we have a shortcut formula for GP to find any term without listing them all. Notice the exponent 'n-1'. This is because the first term 'a' has 'r' raised to the power of 0, the second term has 'r' to the power of 1, and so on.

Worked Example: Finding the 4th Term of a GP

  • GP: 2, 6, 18, ...
  • Here, a = 2, r = 3 (since 6/2 = 3). We want to find a_4.
  • a_4 = ar^(4-1) = 2 * 3^3 = 2 * 27 = 54.

🎙️ Let's apply the GP nth term formula. For the given GP, the first term 'a' is 2, and the common ratio 'r' is 3. We want the 4th term. Plugging these into the formula, we find the 4th term is 54. You can check this by multiplying 18 by 3.

Common Mistakes in Progressions

  • Confusing AP and GP formulas.
  • Calculation errors, especially with negative numbers or fractions.
  • Incorrectly identifying 'a', 'd', or 'r'.
  • Forgetting (n-1) in formulas.

🎙️ Students often mix up the formulas for AP and GP, or make small calculation errors. Always double-check if you're dealing with an AP or GP first, and then carefully identify your 'a', 'd', or 'r' values. Pay close attention to the exponent 'n-1'.

Recap: Key Takeaways

  • Sequence: Ordered list of numbers.
  • Series: Sum of terms in a sequence.
  • AP: Constant common difference (d). a_n = a + (n-1)d, S_n = n/2[2a + (n-1)d].
  • GP: Constant common ratio (r). a_n = ar^(n-1).

🎙️ We've covered a lot today! Remember, sequences are lists, series are sums. APs have a constant difference, and GPs have a constant ratio. Keep these core definitions and formulas in mind, and you'll master progressions!

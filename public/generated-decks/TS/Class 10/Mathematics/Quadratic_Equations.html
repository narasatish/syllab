Defining a Quadratic Equation

  • An equation of the form ax² + bx + c = 0.
  • Where 'a', 'b', 'c' are real numbers.
  • And 'a' cannot be zero (a ≠ 0).

🎙️ This is the standard form of a quadratic equation. The condition 'a ≠ 0' is crucial because if 'a' were zero, the x² term would vanish, and it would become a linear equation.

Method 1: Solving by Factorisation (Splitting the Middle Term)

  • Breaking down the quadratic polynomial into linear factors.
  • Uses the zero product rule: if A × B = 0, then A=0 or B=0.
  • A common and often quick method.

🎙️ Factorisation is like taking a complex puzzle and breaking it into simpler, manageable pieces. We'll convert our quadratic into two linear expressions whose product is zero.

🧩
Putting Pieces Together
12 / 30

Steps for Factorisation Method

🎙️ These are the systematic steps to solve any quadratic equation by factorisation. Pay close attention to finding those two crucial numbers 'p' and 'q'.

1Write in standard form: ax²+bx+c=0
2Find two numbers p, q such that p+q=b and p×q=ac
3Split the middle term: ax²+px+qx+c=0
4Factor by grouping
5Set each factor to zero and solve for x
13 / 30

Worked Example 2: Factorisation (x² - 5x + 6 = 0)

  • Here, a=1, b=-5, c=6.
  • We need p+q=-5 and p×q=6.
  • Numbers are -2 and -3.

🎙️ So, we split -5x into -2x and -3x. The equation becomes x² - 2x - 3x + 6 = 0. Then, (x(x-2) - 3(x-2)) = 0, which means (x-2)(x-3) = 0. Thus, x=2 or x=3.

Worked Example 3: Factorisation (2x² + x - 6 = 0)

  • Here, a=2, b=1, c=-6.
  • We need p+q=1 and p×q=2×(-6)=-12.
  • Numbers are 4 and -3.

🎙️ Splitting 'x' as '4x - 3x', we get 2x² + 4x - 3x - 6 = 0. Factor by grouping: 2x(x+2) - 3(x+2) = 0. This gives (2x-3)(x+2) = 0. So, x = 3/2 or x = -2.

Method 2: Completing the Square (Making a Perfect Shape)

  • Transforming the equation into a perfect square trinomial.
  • A powerful method to derive the quadratic formula.
  • Can solve equations that are not easily factorised.

🎙️ Imagine you have an incomplete square. Completing the square is like adding just the right pieces to make it a perfect square. This method guarantees a solution for any quadratic equation.

📐
Perfect Square Geometry
17 / 30

Steps for Completing the Square

🎙️ Follow these steps carefully. The key is to manipulate the equation such that one side becomes a perfect square, allowing us to easily take the square root.

1Move constant term 'c' to RHS
2Divide by 'a' to make x² coefficient 1
3Add (b/2a)² to both sides
4Write LHS as (x + b/2a)²
5Take square root of both sides (±)
6Solve for x
18 / 30

Worked Example 4: Completing the Square (x² + 6x + 5 = 0)

  • x² + 6x = -5
  • Add (6/2)² = 3² = 9 to both sides.
  • x² + 6x + 9 = -5 + 9
  • (x + 3)² = 4

🎙️ Taking the square root of both sides, we get x + 3 = ±2. This gives us two possibilities: x + 3 = 2 (so x = -1) or x + 3 = -2 (so x = -5). These are our roots.

⏹️
Making a Square

Method 3: The Quadratic Formula (A Universal Tool)

  • Derived from the completing the square method.
  • Always works, even when factorisation is difficult.
  • x = [-b ± √(b² - 4ac)] / 2a

🎙️ This formula is your best friend for quadratic equations! It's a direct way to find the roots without going through the steps of factorisation or completing the square every time.

🛠️
The All-Purpose Tool

Steps for using the Quadratic Formula

🎙️ Carefully identify 'a', 'b', and 'c' including their signs. Then, substitute them into the formula and perform the calculations step-by-step to avoid errors.

1Identify a, b, c from ax²+bx+c=0
2Substitute values into the formula
3Calculate the term inside the square root (discriminant)
4Simplify the square root
5Calculate the two possible values for x
21 / 30

Worked Example 5: Quadratic Formula (2x² - 7x + 3 = 0)

  • Here, a=2, b=-7, c=3.
  • x = [-(-7) ± √((-7)² - 4×2×3)] / (2×2)
  • x = [7 ± √(49 - 24)] / 4
  • x = [7 ± √25] / 4

🎙️ Continuing the calculation: x = [7 ± 5] / 4. This gives two roots: x = (7+5)/4 = 12/4 = 3, and x = (7-5)/4 = 2/4 = 1/2. Simple and effective!

Nature of Roots: Predicting the Outcome

  • What kind of roots will the equation have?
  • Real and distinct? Real and equal? No real roots?
  • Determined by the 'discriminant' (D).

🎙️ Before even solving, we can predict the type of roots a quadratic equation will have. This is done by looking at a special part of the quadratic formula called the discriminant.

🔮
Predicting the Future
24 / 30

The Discriminant (D = b² - 4ac)

  • If D > 0: Two distinct real roots.
  • If D = 0: Two equal real roots.
  • If D < 0: No real roots (roots are complex).

🎙️ The discriminant, D, is the expression under the square root in the quadratic formula. Its value tells us everything about the nature of the roots without actually calculating them.

D > 0 (Distinct Real)
D = 0 (Equal Real)
D < 0 (No Real Roots)
25 / 30

Worked Example 6: Nature of Roots

  • 1. x² - 4x + 4 = 0 (D = (-4)² - 4(1)(4) = 16 - 16 = 0) -> Equal Real Roots
  • 2. x² - 5x + 6 = 0 (D = (-5)² - 4(1)(6) = 25 - 24 = 1) -> Distinct Real Roots
  • 3. x² + x + 1 = 0 (D = (1)² - 4(1)(1) = 1 - 4 = -3) -> No Real Roots

🎙️ As you can see, by simply calculating the discriminant, D, we can quickly determine the nature of the roots for each quadratic equation. This is a very useful property.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Sign errors in the quadratic formula or discriminant.
  • Incorrectly splitting the middle term in factorisation.
  • Forgetting 'a' in the denominator (2a) of the formula.
  • Not considering both ± in square root operations.

🎙️ These are some of the most frequent errors students make. Always double-check your calculations, especially with negative signs, and ensure you're applying the formulas correctly.

Standard Form of a Quadratic Equation

  • The general form is `ax^2 + bx + c = 0`.
  • `a`, `b`, `c` are real numbers.
  • `a` must not be zero (`a ≠ 0`).

🎙️ This is the standard way we write quadratic equations. The 'a' coefficient is with `x^2`, 'b' with `x`, and 'c' is the constant term. Remember, if 'a' were zero, it wouldn't be a quadratic equation anymore, but a linear one.

Method 1: Factorization (Splitting the Middle Term)

  • Express the quadratic polynomial as a product of two linear factors.
  • Set each factor to zero to find the roots.
  • Works well when factors are easy to find.

🎙️ Our first method to find roots is factorization, often called 'splitting the middle term'. The idea is to break down the quadratic expression into simpler linear expressions, which then makes finding 'x' much easier.

✂️
Break Apart

Steps for Factorization Method

  • 1. Write in standard form `ax^2 + bx + c = 0`.
  • 2. Find two numbers whose product is `ac` and sum is `b`.
  • 3. Split the middle term (`bx`) using these numbers.
  • 4. Factor by grouping.

🎙️ Let's outline the steps clearly. The key is finding those two special numbers that help us split the middle term. Once you do that, the rest is just careful algebraic manipulation.

1Standard Form
2Find P, S
3Split bx
4Factor by Grouping

Worked Example: Factorization

  • Solve `x^2 - 5x + 6 = 0`.

🎙️ Let's apply the factorization method to solve `x^2 - 5x + 6 = 0`. Here, `a=1`, `b=-5`, `c=6`. We need two numbers whose product is `1*6 = 6` and sum is `-5`. These numbers are `-2` and `-3`.

Method 2: Completing the Square

  • Transform the equation into a perfect square trinomial.
  • This allows us to take the square root of both sides.
  • Always works, even when factorization is difficult.

🎙️ Our second method, 'Completing the Square', is a powerful technique. It's about manipulating the equation so that one side becomes a perfect square, making it easy to isolate 'x' by taking the square root. This method is universal.

Perfect Shape

Steps for Completing the Square Method

  • 1. Make `a=1` (divide by `a` if needed).
  • 2. Move constant `c` to the RHS.
  • 3. Add `(b/2)^2` to both sides.
  • 4. Factor LHS as `(x + b/2)^2`, simplify RHS.
  • 5. Take square root of both sides.

🎙️ These are the steps to complete the square. The crucial step is adding `(b/2)^2` to both sides, which mathematically creates a perfect square on the left-hand side. Remember to handle the square root carefully, considering both positive and negative roots.

1Make a=1
2Move c
3Add (b/2)²
4Factor LHS
5Square Root

Worked Example: Completing the Square

  • Solve `x^2 + 4x - 5 = 0`.

🎙️ Let's solve `x^2 + 4x - 5 = 0` using completing the square. Here `a=1`, `b=4`, `c=-5`. First, move `c` to the RHS: `x^2 + 4x = 5`. Then, add `(4/2)^2 = 4` to both sides: `x^2 + 4x + 4 = 5 + 4`. This simplifies to `(x+2)^2 = 9`. Taking the square root gives `x+2 = ±3`. So, `x = 1` or `x = -5`.

Method 3: The Quadratic Formula

  • A direct formula to find the roots.
  • Derived by completing the square on the general form.
  • Works for ALL quadratic equations.

🎙️ The quadratic formula is a fantastic shortcut! It's derived from the completing the square method applied to the general form `ax^2 + bx + c = 0`. This means it will always give you the roots, no matter how complex the equation is.

➗
Direct Solution

The Quadratic Formula

  • For `ax^2 + bx + c = 0`:
  • `x = [-b ± √(b^2 - 4ac)] / 2a`.

🎙️ This is the quadratic formula. Memorize it well! You just need to identify `a`, `b`, and `c` from your equation, substitute them into the formula, and calculate to find the two roots of 'x'.

✨
Magic Formula
20 / 25

Worked Example: Quadratic Formula

  • Solve `2x^2 + 3x - 2 = 0`.

🎙️ Let's use the quadratic formula for `2x^2 + 3x - 2 = 0`. Here, `a=2`, `b=3`, `c=-2`. Substitute these values into the formula: `x = [-3 ± √(3^2 - 4*2*(-2))] / (2*2)`. Simplify to get `x = [-3 ± √(9 + 16)] / 4` which is `x = [-3 ± √25] / 4`. So `x = [-3 ± 5] / 4`. This gives `x = 2/4 = 1/2` or `x = -8/4 = -2`.

Nature of Roots: The Discriminant

  • The expression `b^2 - 4ac` is called the 'discriminant'.
  • It determines the nature of the roots.
  • Represented by `Δ` (Delta).

🎙️ Inside the square root of the quadratic formula, we have `b^2 - 4ac`. This special part is called the 'discriminant'. It tells us a lot about the roots without even fully calculating them. We use the symbol Delta for it.

🧐
Insight

Types of Roots based on Discriminant (Δ)

  • 1. If `Δ > 0`: Two distinct real roots.
  • 2. If `Δ = 0`: Two equal real roots (one repeated root).
  • 3. If `Δ < 0`: No real roots (complex roots).

🎙️ The value of the discriminant is key. If it's positive, you get two different real numbers as roots. If it's zero, you get two identical real roots. And if it's negative, you won't find any real numbers that satisfy the equation.

Δ > 0 (Distinct)
Δ = 0 (Equal)
Δ < 0 (No Real)

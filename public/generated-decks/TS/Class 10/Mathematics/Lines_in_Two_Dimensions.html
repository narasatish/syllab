Plotting Points: Finding Your Spot

  • A point is represented by an ordered pair (x, y).
  • First value is the x-coordinate (horizontal movement).
  • Second value is the y-coordinate (vertical movement).

🎙️ To plot a point like (3, 2), you start at the origin. Move 3 units along the X-axis, then 2 units up parallel to the Y-axis. This ordered pair (x,y) is the unique address of that point on our math map.

1Start at Origin (0,0)
2Move horizontally (x)
3Move vertically (y)
4Mark the point

Deriving the Distance Formula

  • We can form a right-angled triangle.
  • The line segment is the hypotenuse.
  • Apply the Pythagorean theorem!

🎙️ The beauty of the Cartesian plane is that we can use basic geometry. If we have two points, we can draw a horizontal line and a vertical line to form a right-angled triangle. The distance between our two points then becomes the hypotenuse, and we can use Pythagoras' theorem to find its length!

The Distance Formula

  • For two points P(x₁, y₁) and Q(x₂, y₂).
  • Distance PQ = √[(x₂ - x₁)² + (y₂ - y₁)²].
  • Remember to always take the positive square root.

🎙️ Here it is, the powerful Distance Formula! It looks a bit complex, but it's simply the Pythagorean theorem in action. Just plug in the coordinates of your two points, calculate the differences, square them, add them, and finally take the square root. Always remember, distance is always a positive value.

➕➖
Formula for Distance

Worked Example: Distance Calculation

  • Find the distance between A(2, 3) and B(5, 7).
  • Step 1: Identify (x₁, y₁) and (x₂, y₂).
  • Step 2: Substitute values into the formula.
  • Step 3: Calculate and simplify.

🎙️ Let's work through an example. For A(2,3) and B(5,7), x₁=2, y₁=3, x₂=5, y₂=7. Substitute these into the formula. We get the square root of (3 squared plus 4 squared), which simplifies to the square root of 25, giving us a distance of 5 units. Simple, right?

1A(2,3), B(5,7)
2√[(5-2)² + (7-3)²]
3√[3² + 4²] = √[9+16]
4√25 = 5 units

The Section Formula does precisely this for a line segment in the coordinate plane. It helps us locate a point that divides a line segment in a given ratio.

Internal Division Formula

  • Point P(x, y) divides the line segment joining A(x₁, y₁) and B(x₂, y₂).
  • The ratio is m₁ : m₂.
  • P(x, y) = [ (m₁x₂ + m₂x₁)/(m₁ + m₂) , (m₁y₂ + m₂y₁)/(m₁ + m₂) ].

🎙️ This formula helps us find the coordinates of a point that lies *between* two other points and divides the line connecting them in a specific ratio. The 'm' values represent the ratio. Notice how the 'm₁' multiplies with the 'x₂' and 'y₂', and 'm₂' with 'x₁' and 'y₁'. It's a cross-multiplication pattern.

Midpoint Formula: The Middle Ground

  • A special case of the Section Formula.
  • The point divides the segment into two equal halves.
  • The ratio is 1:1 (m₁ = 1, m₂ = 1).

🎙️ When the ratio of division is 1:1, it means the point is exactly in the middle of the line segment. This is so common that it has its own simplified formula! You just average the x-coordinates and average the y-coordinates. It's a very handy formula.

Worked Example: Section Formula

  • Find the point that divides the segment joining A(1, 7) and B(4, -3).
  • Ratio is 2:3 (m₁=2, m₂=3).
  • Step 1: Write down coordinates and ratio.
  • Step 2: Apply the Section Formula.

🎙️ Let's apply the Section Formula. Substitute x₁, y₁, x₂, y₂, m₁, and m₂ carefully. For the x-coordinate, it's (2 times 4 plus 3 times 1) divided by (2 plus 3). For the y-coordinate, it's (2 times -3 plus 3 times 7) divided by (2 plus 3). This gives us the point (11/5, 3).

1A(1,7), B(4,-3), m₁=2, m₂=3
2x = (2*4 + 3*1)/(2+3)
3y = (2*-3 + 3*7)/(2+3)
4Point = (11/5, 15/5) = (11/5, 3)

Worked Example: Midpoint Formula

  • Find the midpoint of the segment joining P(-2, 8) and Q(6, -4).
  • Step 1: Identify (x₁, y₁) and (x₂, y₂).
  • Step 2: Apply the Midpoint Formula.

🎙️ Using the Midpoint Formula is even simpler. For P(-2,8) and Q(6,-4), the x-coordinate of the midpoint is (-2 plus 6) divided by 2, which is 4/2 or 2. The y-coordinate is (8 plus -4) divided by 2, which is 4/2 or 2. So the midpoint is (2, 2).

1P(-2,8), Q(6,-4)
2x = (-2 + 6)/2
3y = (8 + (-4))/2
4Midpoint = (4/2, 4/2) = (2, 2)

Area of a Triangle Formula (using coordinates)

  • For a triangle with vertices A(x₁, y₁), B(x₂, y₂), C(x₃, y₃).
  • Area = ½ | x₁(y₂ - y₃) + x₂(y₃ - y₁) + x₃(y₁ - y₂) |.
  • The absolute value ensures the area is always positive.

🎙️ This formula might look daunting, but it follows a cyclic pattern. Start with x₁ and multiply it by (y₂ - y₃), then x₂ by (y₃ - y₁), and finally x₃ by (y₁ - y₂). Sum these products, take half, and then the absolute value. The absolute value is crucial because area cannot be negative.

Collinearity: Points on a Straight Line

  • If three points lie on the same straight line.
  • They cannot form a triangle.
  • Therefore, the area of the 'triangle' formed by them is zero.

🎙️ This is a very important application of the Area of a Triangle formula. If three points are collinear, meaning they all fall on the same straight line, they cannot possibly form a triangle. In such a case, if you use the area formula, the result will be zero. This is a great way to check if points are collinear.

Worked Example: Area Calculation

  • Find the area of the triangle with vertices (1, -1), (-4, 6), and (-3, -5).
  • Step 1: Identify (x₁, y₁), (x₂, y₂), (x₃, y₃).
  • Step 2: Substitute values into the formula.
  • Step 3: Calculate and simplify.

🎙️ Let's calculate the area. Substitute the coordinates carefully. x₁=1, y₁=-1; x₂=-4, y₂=6; x₃=-3, y₃=-5. Follow the formula: 1 times (6 minus -5), plus -4 times (-5 minus -1), plus -3 times (-1 minus 6). Simplify each term and add them up. You'll get 48, and half of that is 24 square units.

1A(1,-1), B(-4,6), C(-3,-5)
2½ |1(6 - (-5)) + (-4)(-5 - (-1)) + (-3)(-1 - 6)|
3½ |1(11) - 4(-4) - 3(-7)|
4½ |11 + 16 + 21| = ½ |48| = 24 sq. units

Slope of a Line: How Steep is it?

  • Imagine climbing a hill or a ramp.
  • Some are gentle, some are very steep.
  • Slope tells us this 'steepness' or 'gradient'.

🎙️ What makes one road easier to cycle up than another? It's the slope! In mathematics, the slope of a line quantifies how steep it is. It tells us how much the line rises or falls vertically for a given horizontal change. This concept is vital for understanding the direction and inclination of lines.

Slope Formula: Rise Over Run

  • Slope (m) = Change in y / Change in x.
  • m = (y₂ - y₁) / (x₂ - x₁).
  • A positive slope means the line goes up to the right, negative goes down.

🎙️ The slope, denoted by 'm', is simply the ratio of the vertical change (rise) to the horizontal change (run) between any two points on the line. If the line rises as you move right, the slope is positive. If it falls, the slope is negative. A horizontal line has a slope of 0, and a vertical line has an undefined slope.

Parallel and Perpendicular Lines

  • Parallel lines: Never meet, have the same slope (m₁ = m₂).
  • Perpendicular lines: Meet at 90°, product of slopes is -1 (m₁ * m₂ = -1).

🎙️ The slopes of lines give us powerful information about their relationship. If two lines are parallel, like railway tracks, they have the exact same steepness. If they are perpendicular, like the arms of a cross, their slopes have a special relationship: when multiplied, they always result in -1. This is a very important property.

Parallel (m1=m2)
Perpendicular (m1*m2=-1)

Common Mistakes to Avoid!

  • Mixing up (x₁, y₁) and (x₂, y₂) in formulas.
  • Sign errors, especially with negative coordinates.
  • Forgetting the absolute value in Area of Triangle.
  • Incorrectly applying ratio in Section Formula (m₁x₁ vs m₁x₂).

🎙️ It's easy to make small errors in Coordinate Geometry. Always double-check your coordinates, be extra careful with negative signs, and remember that distance and area are always positive. In the Section Formula, remember the cross-multiplication pattern: m₁ with x₂ and y₂, and m₂ with x₁ and y₁.

What is Mathematical Modelling?

  • Using mathematics to describe real-world situations.
  • Translating a problem into mathematical terms.
  • Finding solutions using mathematical techniques.

🎙️ Mathematical modelling is essentially like building a simplified mathematical version of a real-world problem. We take a complex situation, identify the key elements, and express them using equations, graphs, or other mathematical structures. This allows us to analyze the problem in a structured way.

🎙️ Mathematical modelling isn't a random process; it follows a clear, structured path. There are five main steps that we'll explore today. These steps guide us from understanding the initial problem all the way to verifying our solution.

Step 1: Understand the Problem

  • Identify the goal of the problem.
  • Collect relevant information and data.
  • Define variables and assumptions clearly.
  • Simplify if necessary, without losing core meaning.

🎙️ The first and crucial step is to fully understand what the problem is asking. We need to clearly define what we want to find out, gather all available information, and make any necessary assumptions to simplify the problem into a manageable form. This foundation is key to building an effective model.

🎙️ Once we understand the problem, the next step is to convert it into the language of mathematics. Example: Ball Flight - Formulating the Model

  • Use projectile motion equations.
  • Variables: distance (x), height (y), time (t).
  • Equations involve initial velocity, angle, and 'g' for gravity.
  • Ignoring air resistance for simplicity (initial assumption).

🎙️ For our cricket ball example, we'd use the equations of projectile motion. These equations relate the ball's horizontal distance and vertical height to its initial speed, launch angle, and the constant acceleration due to gravity. For a first model, we might simplify by ignoring air resistance to make calculations easier.

Example: Ball Flight - Solving the Model

  • Substitute initial values (speed, angle) into equations.
  • Calculate the time the ball is in the air.
  • Determine the horizontal distance covered at that time.
  • Find the point where height (y) becomes zero.

🎙️ For our cricket ball, we would plug in the actual initial speed and angle into our projectile motion equations. We would then solve for the time it takes for the ball to hit the ground (where its height is zero) and then use that time to calculate the total horizontal distance it travelled. This gives us the predicted landing spot.

Step 5: Validate the Model

  • Compare the model's prediction with actual observations.
  • Check if the assumptions made were reasonable.
  • Refine the model if predictions are inaccurate.
  • Ensure the model is reliable for future use.

🎙️ The final step is crucial: validation. We must compare our model's predictions with what actually happens in the real world. If the predictions don't match reality, it means our model needs refinement. We might need to adjust our assumptions, include more factors, or even choose a different mathematical approach until the model is accurate and reliable.

Example: Ball Flight - Validating the Model

  • Observe where the ball actually lands in a game.
  • Compare this actual distance to our predicted 75 meters.
  • If it's close, the model is good.
  • If far off, re-evaluate assumptions (e.g., add air resistance).

🎙️ For our cricket ball, validation means watching where the ball actually lands after being hit and comparing that to our calculated 75 meters. If the ball lands at 74 or 76 meters, our model is pretty good. Types of Mathematical Models: Linear

  • Describes a straight-line relationship.
  • Change in one variable causes a proportional change in another.
  • Represented by equations like y = mx + c.
  • Useful for constant rates of change.

🎙️ One common type of model is a linear model. This is used when there's a direct, straight-line relationship between two variables. For example, if you increase one quantity, the other increases or decreases at a constant rate. Think of it like a graph that forms a straight line.

📏
Straight Line

Real-Life Example: Cab Fare Calculation

  • Cab fare often has a fixed charge plus a charge per kilometer.
  • This is a classic linear relationship.
  • Fare = (Rate per km × Distance) + Fixed Charge.

🎙️ A perfect example of a linear model is calculating a cab fare. There's usually a basic fixed charge just for starting the ride, and then an additional charge that depends directly on how many kilometers you travel. So, the total fare increases steadily with distance, forming a linear relationship.

Worked Example: Calculating Cab Fare

  • Fixed charge = ₹50.
  • Charge per km = ₹15.
  • Distance travelled = 10 km.
  • Total Fare = 50 + (15 × 10) = ₹200.

🎙️ Let's put this into practice. If a cab has a fixed charge of ₹50 and charges ₹15 per kilometer, and you travel 10 kilometers, how much will you pay? We use our linear model: ₹50 (fixed) + (₹15/km * 10 km) = ₹50 + ₹150 = ₹200. This simple calculation gives us the total fare.

Types of Mathematical Models: Quadratic

  • Describes a parabolic or 'U' shaped relationship.
  • Involves a variable raised to the power of two (x²).
  • Represented by equations like y = ax² + bx + c.
  • Useful for areas, projectile motion, optimization.

🎙️ Another important type of model is a quadratic model. Unlike linear models, these describe relationships that are curved, often forming a 'U' shape called a parabola. They involve variables squared, and are very useful for problems involving areas, the path of thrown objects, or finding maximum/minimum values.

Real-Life Example: Area of a Garden

  • The area of a square garden is side × side.
  • If side length is 'x', Area = x².
  • This is a quadratic relationship.
  • Doubling the side quadruples the area.

🎙️ Consider the area of a square garden. If the side length is 'x', the area is 'x multiplied by x', or x squared. This is a quadratic relationship. Notice how if you double the side length, the area doesn't just double, it actually becomes four times larger! This shows the non-linear nature of quadratic models.

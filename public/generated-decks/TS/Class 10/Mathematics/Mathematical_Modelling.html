What is Mathematical Modelling?

  • It's like building a mini-version of a real situation using math.
  • We use equations, graphs, and numbers to represent the problem.
  • Helps us understand, predict, and make decisions.

🎙️ Think of it like creating a map. A map isn't the actual place, but it helps you understand and navigate it. Mathematical modelling is similar – we create a mathematical 'map' or 'model' of a real-world problem using mathematical tools to explore and solve it.

Why Do We Model?

  • To understand complex systems better.
  • To predict future outcomes (e.g., weather).

Modelling Example: Area of a Room

  • Problem: Find the area of a rectangular classroom floor.
  • Why: To calculate the number of tiles needed.
  • Variables: Length (L), Width (W), Area (A).

🎙️ Let's take a simple example. Imagine you need to tile your classroom. The first step is to know the area of the floor. This is a classic real-world problem that can be easily modelled mathematically.

🏫
Classroom Floor
13 / 30

Steps: Area of a Room Model

  • Formulate: A = L × W
  • Solve: If L=10m, W=8m, then A = 10 × 8 = 80 sq m.
  • Interpret: The classroom floor is 80 square meters.
  • Validate: Measure the room again to confirm L and W.

🎙️ Here, our mathematical model is the simple formula for area. We plug in the measurements, get the answer, and then understand what 80 square meters means for our tiling project. We can double-check our measurements to validate the model.

Types of Models: Linear Models

  • Represent relationships with a straight line.
  • Output changes proportionally with input.
  • Equation form: y = mx + c.

🎙️ Just like in algebra, we have different types of mathematical models. Linear models are the simplest, where the relationship between two quantities can be shown with a straight line graph. Think of how distance increases steadily with constant speed.

Modelling Example: Cab Fare

  • Problem: Calculate total cab fare for a trip.
  • Base fare: ₹50, plus ₹10 per km.
  • Variables: Distance (d), Total Fare (F).

🎙️ Let's say you take a cab. There's a fixed starting charge and then a charge per kilometer. This is a perfect scenario for a linear model, as the total cost increases steadily with the distance travelled.

🚕
Cab Ride
16 / 30

Steps: Cab Fare Model

  • Formulate: F = 10d + 50
  • Solve: For 15 km, F = 10(15) + 50 = 150 + 50 = ₹200.
  • Interpret: A 15 km ride costs ₹200.
  • Validate: Check with a taxi app or driver for similar trips.

🎙️ Here, we formulate a linear equation. Then we solve it for a given distance. The interpretation is straightforward: the final cost. We can validate by comparing with actual cab fares or apps to see if our model is accurate.

Types of Models: Quadratic Models

  • Represent relationships with a parabolic curve.
  • Involve a variable raised to the power of 2.
  • Equation form: y = ax² + bx + c.

🎙️ Quadratic models are used when the relationship isn't a straight line but forms a curve, like a parabola. Think about throwing a ball – its path isn't straight, it curves downwards. These models involve a squared term.

Modelling Example: Throwing a Ball

  • Problem: Describe the path of a cricket ball thrown upwards.
  • Why: To predict its height at any given time.
  • Variables: Time (t), Height (h), Gravity (g).

🎙️ Imagine a cricketer hitting a six! The ball goes up and then comes down. This curved path can be described using a quadratic model, considering factors like initial speed and gravity. We can predict its height at any moment.

🏏
Cricket Ball
19 / 30

Steps: Ball Trajectory Model

  • Formulate: h = ut - (1/2)gt² (where u=initial velocity, g=gravity)
  • Solve: For u=20m/s, g=9.8m/s², at t=1s, h = 20(1) - 0.5(9.8)(1)² = 20 - 4.9 = 15.1 m.
  • Interpret: After 1 second, the ball is 15.1 meters high.
  • Validate: Compare with video analysis of actual throws.

🎙️ Here, we use the physics formula involving gravity, which is a quadratic equation. We solve for height at a specific time. The interpretation tells us the ball's position. Validating this might involve complex equipment or comparing with known physics principles.

Modelling Example: Water Tank Capacity

  • Problem: Calculate the volume of a cylindrical water tank.
  • Why: To know how much water it can hold.
  • Variables: Radius (r), Height (h), Volume (V).

🎙️ Many homes and apartments have water tanks. How do we know how much water a tank can store? We use geometric modelling! For a cylindrical tank, we need its radius and height.

💧
Water Tank
23 / 30

Steps: Water Tank Model

  • Formulate: V = πr²h
  • Solve: If r=1m, h=2m, V = π(1)²(2) = 2π ≈ 6.28 cubic meters.
  • Interpret: The tank holds about 6280 liters of water (1m³ = 1000L).
  • Validate: Compare with manufacturer's specifications or actual filling tests.

🎙️ Our model here is the volume formula for a cylinder. We substitute the dimensions, solve for volume, and then interpret it in a practical unit like liters. Validation involves checking against the tank's official capacity or actual measurements.

Common Mistakes in Modelling

  • Ignoring assumptions or variables.
  • Choosing the wrong type of model.
  • Not validating the model with real data.
  • Incorrectly interpreting mathematical results.

🎙️ Even experienced mathematicians can make mistakes! Common pitfalls include overlooking crucial details, picking a model that doesn't fit the problem, or simply forgetting to check if the solution makes sense in the real world. Always be careful!

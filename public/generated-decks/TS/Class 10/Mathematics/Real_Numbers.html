Euclid's Division Lemma (EDL): The Formal Way

  • Given positive integers 'a' and 'b'.
  • There exist unique integers 'q' and 'r'.
  • Such that a = bq + r, where 0 ≤ r < b.

🎙️ Here, 'a' is the dividend, 'b' is the divisor, 'q' is the quotient, and 'r' is the remainder. The remainder 'r' must always be less than the divisor 'b', and it can be zero. This lemma is fundamental to many number theory concepts.

➗
Division Rule
11 / 30

EDL Example: Dividing 15 by 4

  • Let a = 15 (dividend), b = 4 (divisor).
  • We want to find q (quotient) and r (remainder).
  • 15 = 4 × 3 + 3. Here, q=3, r=3.
  • Notice: 0 ≤ 3 < 4. The remainder is valid!

🎙️ This simple example shows how EDL works. We divide 15 by 4. We can get three groups of 4, which is 12, and then 3 is left over. The remainder 3 is indeed less than the divisor 4.

Euclid's Division Algorithm (EDA): Finding HCF

  • A series of steps to find the HCF of two numbers.
  • Repeatedly apply Euclid's Division Lemma.
  • The process continues until the remainder is zero.

🎙️ An algorithm is simply a set of well-defined steps to solve a problem. Euclid's Division Algorithm uses the lemma repeatedly to find the Highest Common Factor (HCF) of two positive integers. It's a very efficient method.

⚙️
Step-by-step Process
13 / 30

EDA Worked Example: HCF of 42 and 24

  • Step 1: Apply EDL to 42 and 24. 42 = 24 × 1 + 18.
  • Step 2: Remainder is 18 ≠ 0. Apply EDL to 24 and 18. 24 = 18 × 1 + 6.
  • Step 3: Remainder is 6 ≠ 0. Apply EDL to 18 and 6. 18 = 6 × 3 + 0.
  • Step 4: Remainder is 0. The divisor at this stage is 6. So, HCF(42, 24) = 6.

🎙️ See how we keep dividing the previous divisor by the remainder until we hit zero? The divisor just before the remainder becomes zero is our HCF. This method is guaranteed to work for any two positive integers.

EDA Worked Example 2: HCF of 135 and 225

  • Step 1: 225 = 135 × 1 + 90.
  • Step 2: 135 = 90 × 1 + 45.
  • Step 3: 90 = 45 × 2 + 0.
  • The remainder is 0. The divisor at this stage is 45. So, HCF(135, 225) = 45.

🎙️ Here's another example to solidify your understanding. Always remember to make the larger number the dividend and the smaller one the divisor in the first step. Then, the divisor becomes the new dividend and the remainder becomes the new divisor.

Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic: Building Blocks!

  • Every composite number can be broken down.
  • It breaks down into a unique product of prime numbers.
  • Think of prime numbers as the 'atoms' of all numbers.

🎙️ Just like building blocks, every composite number can be uniquely constructed from prime numbers. Prime numbers are those numbers greater than 1 that have only two factors: 1 and themselves. This theorem is incredibly important!

🧱🧱🧱🧱🧱🧱
Prime Building Blocks
17 / 30

FTA: Formal Definition

  • Every composite number can be expressed (factorised) as a product of primes.
  • And this factorisation is unique.
  • Except for the order in which the prime factors occur.

🎙️ This means no matter how you start factoring a number, you'll always end up with the same set of prime factors. For example, 60 can be 2x30 or 3x20, but its prime factors will always be two 2s, one 3, and one 5.

⭐
Unique Primes
18 / 30

FTA Example: Prime Factorization of 60

  • Start with 60.
  • Divide by the smallest prime: 60 ÷ 2 = 30.
  • Continue: 30 ÷ 2 = 15.
  • Next prime: 15 ÷ 3 = 5.
  • Finally: 5 ÷ 5 = 1. So, 60 = 2 × 2 × 3 × 5 = 2² × 3 × 5.

🎙️ Here's how we practically apply the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic. We keep dividing by prime numbers until we can't divide anymore. This process gives us the unique prime factorization of the number.

Applications of FTA: HCF & LCM

  • HCF: Product of the smallest power of each common prime factor.
  • LCM: Product of the greatest power of each prime factor involved.
  • Important relation: HCF(a,b) × LCM(a,b) = a × b.

🎙️ The Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic makes finding HCF and LCM of numbers much easier, especially for larger numbers. We just need to find their prime factorizations and then apply these simple rules.

🧮
Calculations
20 / 30

HCF & LCM Example: 12 and 18

  • Prime factors of 12 = 2² × 3¹
  • Prime factors of 18 = 2¹ × 3²
  • HCF(12, 18) = 2¹ × 3¹ = 6 (smallest powers)
  • LCM(12, 18) = 2² × 3² = 4 × 9 = 36 (greatest powers)

🎙️ Let's apply the rules. For HCF, we look for common prime factors and take the lowest power. For LCM, we take all prime factors involved and use their highest powers. Notice how 6 × 36 = 12 × 18 = 216.

Revisiting Irrational Numbers: Proof by Contradiction

  • How do we 'prove' a number is irrational?
  • We assume the opposite (it's rational).
  • Then we show this assumption leads to a contradiction.
  • Therefore, our initial assumption must be false.

🎙️ Proving a number is irrational can be tricky. We use a powerful logical technique called 'proof by contradiction'. It's like saying, 'If I assume X is true, but it leads to something impossible, then X must be false!'

🤔
Logical Thinking
23 / 30

Proof that √2 is Irrational - Step 1: Assume Rational

  • Assume, for the sake of contradiction, that √2 is rational.
  • This means we can write √2 = a/b.
  • Where 'a' and 'b' are coprime integers (no common factors other than 1), and b ≠ 0.

🎙️ Our first step is to make this crucial assumption. If √2 is rational, it *must* be expressible as a fraction a/b in its simplest form. This means 'a' and 'b' share no common factors.

📝
Initial Assumption
24 / 30

Proof that √2 is Irrational - Step 2: The Contradiction

  • From √2 = a/b, we get 2 = a²/b² => a² = 2b².
  • This means 'a²' is an even number.
  • If 'a²' is even, then 'a' must also be an even number.
  • So, we can write a = 2c for some integer c.

🎙️ Now we're diving into the core logic. If a² is 2 times something, it's even. And if a square is even, the original number must also be even. This allows us to substitute 'a' with '2c'.

1√2 = a/b
2a² = 2b² (a is even)
3a = 2c
4(2c)² = 2b²
25 / 30

Proof that √2 is Irrational - Step 3: Conclusion

  • Substitute a = 2c into a² = 2b²: (2c)² = 2b² => 4c² = 2b² => 2c² = b².
  • This means 'b²' is an even number.
  • If 'b²' is even, then 'b' must also be an even number.
  • So, both 'a' and 'b' are even. This contradicts our initial assumption that 'a' and 'b' are coprime!

🎙️ We've reached the contradiction! We assumed 'a' and 'b' had no common factors, but our derivation shows they both have a common factor of 2. This means our initial assumption that √2 is rational must be false. Therefore, √2 is irrational.

Generalizing Irrationality: Proving (3 + 2√5) is Irrational

  • Assume (3 + 2√5) is rational, so 3 + 2√5 = a/b.
  • Rearrange to isolate √5: 2√5 = a/b - 3 => 2√5 = (a - 3b)/b => √5 = (a - 3b) / 2b.
  • Since a, b are integers, (a - 3b) / 2b is rational.
  • This implies √5 is rational, which we know is false. Contradiction!

🎙️ This shows how we can prove expressions involving irrational numbers are also irrational. The key is to isolate the known irrational part and show that if the whole expression were rational, it would force the irrational part to be rational, which is a contradiction.

Decimal Expansions: Terminating or Non-Terminating?

  • Rational numbers have decimal expansions that are either:
  • Terminating (e.g., 1/4 = 0.25).
  • Non-terminating repeating (e.g., 1/3 = 0.333...).
  • Irrational numbers are always non-terminating and non-repeating.

🎙️ Every rational number has a predictable decimal form. It either stops after a few digits, or it goes on forever with a repeating pattern. This is a key characteristic that distinguishes rational from irrational numbers.

Theorem: When is a Decimal Terminating?

  • A rational number x = p/q (in simplest form).
  • It has a terminating decimal expansion.
  • IF the prime factorization of its denominator 'q' is of the form 2ⁿ5ᵐ.
  • Where n and m are non-negative integers.

🎙️ This is a very useful theorem! Without actually performing long division, you can tell if a rational number's decimal will terminate. Just simplify the fraction and check the prime factors of the denominator. If it only has 2s and 5s, it terminates!

Common Mistakes to Avoid!

  • Confusing Lemma and Algorithm.
  • Assuming 'a' and 'b' are not coprime in irrational proofs.
  • Incorrectly identifying prime factors in FTA.
  • Forgetting 'q ≠ 0' for rational numbers.

🎙️ Be careful with these common pitfalls! A lemma is a proven statement used to prove others, while an algorithm is a procedure. Always start irrational proofs by assuming the simplest form a/b where a and b are coprime.

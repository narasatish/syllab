What is an Equation?

  • A mathematical statement showing two expressions are equal.
  • It always contains an 'equal to' sign (=).
  • Example: 3x + 5 = 11.

🎙️ Think of an equation like a balanced scale. Whatever is on one side must be exactly equal to what's on the other side. If you change one side, you must change the other to maintain balance.

What is a Linear Equation?

  • An equation where the highest power of any variable is 1.
  • When graphed, it always forms a straight line.
  • Example: 2x + 3 = 7.

🎙️ The word 'linear' comes from 'line'. If you were to draw the graph of any linear equation, you would always get a perfectly straight line, never a curve. This is a key characteristic.

📏
Straight Line

Linear Equation in Two Variables

  • Contains two different variables, usually 'x' and 'y'.
  • Highest power of each variable is still 1.
  • General form: ax + by + c = 0 (where a, b, c are real numbers, and a ≠ 0, b ≠ 0).

🎙️ Now, we add another variable! Instead of just 'x', we have 'x' and 'y'. This means we're looking for two unknown values that satisfy the equation. Remember, 'a' and 'b' cannot be zero simultaneously for it to be a two-variable equation.

The 'Pair' Concept

  • We are studying two linear equations together.
  • These two equations relate the same two variables.
  • We're looking for values of x and y that satisfy BOTH equations simultaneously.

🎙️ A 'pair' simply means two. So, instead of solving one equation, we will be working with two linear equations at the same time. The goal is to find a common solution that works for both.

📝📝
Two Equations

General Form of a Pair of Linear Equations

  • Equation 1: a₁x + b₁y + c₁ = 0
  • Equation 2: a₂x + b₂y + c₂ = 0
  • Here, a₁, b₁, c₁, a₂, b₂, c₂ are real numbers.

🎙️ This is the standard way we write down a pair of linear equations. The subscripts '1' and '2' simply help us distinguish the coefficients and constants of the first equation from the second. Remember, a₁, b₁ cannot both be zero for the first equation, and similarly for a₂, b₂.

What is a Solution to a Pair of Equations?

  • A pair of values (x, y) that satisfies both equations.
  • When you substitute these values, both equations become true statements.
  • It represents the common point where the two lines 'meet'.

🎙️ Finding a solution means finding the specific 'x' and 'y' values that work perfectly for both equations simultaneously. If you substitute these values, the left side of each equation will equal its right side. Geometrically, this is where their graphs intersect.

Graphical Representation

  • Each linear equation in two variables represents a straight line on a graph.
  • A pair of linear equations means we draw two lines.
  • The solution (if any) is the point(s) where these lines interact.

🎙️ Plotting these equations on a coordinate plane gives us a visual understanding. Each equation becomes a line. The way these two lines behave relative to each other tells us about the nature of their solutions.

Types of Lines for a Pair of Equations

  • When two lines are drawn on a plane, there are three possibilities.
  • They can intersect at one point.
  • They can be parallel and never intersect.
  • They can be coincident (one line lies exactly on top of the other).

🎙️ Think about any two straight lines you can draw on a piece of paper. These are the only three ways they can relate to each other. Each of these relationships corresponds to a different type of solution for our pair of equations.

Case 1: Intersecting Lines

  • The two lines cross each other at exactly one point.
  • This point of intersection is the unique solution to the pair of equations.
  • There is only one (x, y) pair that satisfies both equations.

🎙️ When lines intersect, it's like two roads crossing. There's only one spot where they meet. This means our pair of equations has a single, unique solution – one specific value for 'x' and one for 'y'.

✖️
Single Intersection

Condition for Intersecting Lines

  • For a₁x + b₁y + c₁ = 0 and a₂x + b₂y + c₂ = 0,
  • The lines are intersecting if: a₁/a₂ ≠ b₁/b₂.
  • This condition helps predict the nature of lines without graphing.

🎙️ You don't always need to graph to know if lines will intersect! By comparing the ratios of their coefficients 'a' and 'b', we can tell immediately. If these ratios are not equal, the lines will definitely intersect.

Case 2: Parallel Lines

  • The two lines never meet, no matter how far they are extended.
  • They maintain a constant distance from each other.
  • Therefore, there is no common point, meaning no solution.

🎙️ Parallel lines are like railway tracks – they run side-by-side forever but never cross. Since there's no point where they meet, there's no (x, y) solution that can satisfy both equations simultaneously.

🛤️
Railway Tracks

Condition for Parallel Lines

  • For a₁x + b₁y + c₁ = 0 and a₂x + b₂y + c₂ = 0,
  • The lines are parallel if: a₁/a₂ = b₁/b₂ ≠ c₁/c₂.
  • The slopes are equal, but y-intercepts are different.

🎙️ Again, we can use ratios! If the ratio of 'a' coefficients is equal to the ratio of 'b' coefficients, but this is not equal to the ratio of 'c' constants, then the lines are parallel. This means they have the same 'steepness' but start at different points.

Case 3: Coincident Lines

  • One line lies exactly on top of the other line.
  • They are essentially the same line.
  • Every point on the line is a common point, leading to infinitely many solutions.

🎙️ Coincident lines are like drawing one line and then drawing another line exactly over it. They share every single point. This means there are an infinite number of (x, y) pairs that satisfy both equations, as they are effectively the same equation.

♾️
Infinite Solutions

Condition for Coincident Lines

  • For a₁x + b₁y + c₁ = 0 and a₂x + b₂y + c₂ = 0,
  • The lines are coincident if: a₁/a₂ = b₁/b₂ = c₁/c₂.
  • All corresponding ratios of coefficients and constants are equal.

🎙️ When all three ratios – a₁, b₁, and c₁ to a₂, b₂, and c₂ respectively – are equal, then the lines are coincident. This means one equation is simply a multiple of the other, making them represent the same line.

✨
Perfect Match

Consistency of a Pair of Equations

  • Consistent: A pair of equations that has at least one solution.
  • Inconsistent: A pair of equations that has no solution.
  • This refers to whether a solution exists or not.

🎙️ This concept classifies pairs of equations based on whether they have solutions. Intersecting and coincident lines are 'consistent' because they have at least one solution. Parallel lines are 'inconsistent' because they have no solution.

Methods to Solve a Pair of Linear Equations

  • Graphical Method: Plotting the lines and finding their intersection.
  • Algebraic Methods: Solving using mathematical operations.
  • Algebraic methods include Substitution, Elimination, and Cross-Multiplication.

🎙️ We have two main approaches to solve these equations. The graphical method gives us a visual answer. Algebraic methods use step-by-step calculations to find the exact numerical solution without drawing graphs.

Recap: Key Takeaways

  • A pair of linear equations involves two equations with two variables.
  • Lines can be Intersecting (unique solution), Parallel (no solution), or Coincident (infinite solutions).
  • Consistency means a solution exists; inconsistency means no solution.
  • We can solve them graphically or algebraically.

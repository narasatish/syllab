Line and a Circle: Three Possibilities

  • A straight line can interact with a circle in three distinct ways.
  • It can miss the circle entirely.
  • It can cut through the circle at two points.
  • It can touch the circle at exactly one point.

🎙️ Just like our cricket ball example, a straight line in geometry can interact with a circle in precisely three ways. It can pass by without touching it at all. It can cross the circle, entering at one point and exiting at another. Or, it can just brush against the circle, touching it at a single, unique point. These three scenarios define the different types of lines we're going to study.

Possibility 1: Non-intersecting Line

  • A line that does not touch the circle at any point.
  • The distance from the centre to the line is greater than the radius.
  • It passes completely outside the circle.

🎙️ Our first possibility is the simplest: a non-intersecting line. This is a line that passes completely outside the circle and has no points in common with it. Think of a car driving past a roundabouts without entering it. The key mathematical property here is that the perpendicular distance from the centre of the circle to this line is always greater than the radius of the circle.

Possibility 2: Secant of a Circle

  • A line that intersects the circle at two distinct points.

🎙️ Now, let's look at the second possibility: a secant. A secant is a line that passes through the circle, intersecting its circumference at two different points. It literally 'cuts' the circle into two parts. In our cricket analogy, this is like the ball crossing the boundary for a 'four' or a 'six'. Mathematically, the perpendicular distance from the centre of the circle to a secant is always less than the radius.

Possibility 3: Tangent to a Circle

  • A line that intersects the circle at exactly one point.
  • This single point is called the 'point of contact' or 'point of tangency'.
  • The distance from the centre to the line is equal to the radius.

🎙️ Finally, we come to the most important concept for this chapter: a tangent. A tangent is a line that touches the circle at one and only one point. This unique point is called the point of contact. Remember our bicycle tyre nail? That's a tangent! The perpendicular distance from the centre of the circle to a tangent is exactly equal to the radius of the circle. This is a crucial property we'll explore further.

Case 1: Point Inside the Circle

Any line you draw through 'P' will always intersect the circle at two distinct points, making it a secant, not a tangent. So, the conclusion is clear: you cannot draw any tangent to a circle from a point that lies inside it.

Tangent-Radius Property: Perpendicularity

  • The tangent at any point of a circle is perpendicular to the radius.
  • This radius passes through the point of contact.
  • This is a fundamental theorem in circle geometry.

🎙️ This is one of the most important properties of tangents, so pay close attention! The theorem states that if you draw a radius to the point where a tangent touches the circle, that radius will always be perpendicular to the tangent line. This means they form a perfect 90-degree angle. This property is incredibly useful for solving many problems involving tangents.

Right Angle

Why is it Perpendicular? (Intuition)

  • Consider all points on the tangent line.
  • The point of contact is the closest point on the line to the centre.
  • The shortest distance from a point to a line is always perpendicular.

🎙️ Let's understand why this property holds true. Imagine the centre of the circle and the tangent line. Among all the points on the tangent line, the point of contact is the only one that lies on the circle. Any other point on the tangent line would be outside the circle, meaning it's further away from the centre than the radius. Since the radius connecting the centre to the point of contact represents the shortest distance from the centre to the tangent line, it must be perpendicular to the tangent. This is a basic geometric principle: the shortest distance from a point to a line is along the perpendicular.

1Tangent touches at P
2Radius OP to P
3OP is shortest distance
4Shortest distance is perpendicular

Length of Tangents from an External Point

  • From an external point, two tangents can be drawn to a circle.
  • The lengths of these two tangents are equal.
  • This is another important theorem for problem-solving.

🎙️ We know that from an external point, two tangents can be drawn to a circle. Now, let's explore a fascinating property related to their lengths. This theorem states that the segments of these two tangents, from the external point to their respective points of contact on the circle, are always equal in length. This is a powerful tool for solving many geometric problems.

Equal Lengths

Why are the Lengths Equal? (Proof Idea)

  • Consider the two tangents PA and PB from external point P.
  • Draw radii OA and OB, and connect OP.
  • We can prove ΔOAP ≅ ΔOBP using RHS congruence criterion.
  • Thus, PA = PB by CPCTC (Corresponding Parts of Congruent Triangles are Congruent).

🎙️ Let's briefly understand the logic behind this theorem. If we have an external point P and two tangents PA and PB to a circle with centre O, we can form two right-angled triangles: ΔOAP and ΔOBP. We know that OA = OB (both are radii), OP is common to both triangles, and angles OAP and OBP are 90 degrees (due to the tangent-radius property). By the RHS (Right-angle, Hypotenuse, Side) congruence criterion, these two triangles are congruent. Therefore, their corresponding parts, including the tangent lengths PA and PB, must be equal.

1Draw OA, OB, OP
2∠OAP = ∠OBP = 90° (Tangent-Radius)
3OA = OB (Radii)
4OP = OP (Common)
5ΔOAP ≅ ΔOBP (RHS)
6PA = PB (CPCTC)

Worked Example: Finding Tangent Length

  • A point P is 13 cm from the centre O of a circle.
  • The radius of the circle is 5 cm.
  • Find the length of the tangent from P to the circle.

🎙️ Let's apply this knowledge to a problem. We have a point P, 13 cm away from the centre O, and the circle's radius is 5 cm. We need to find the length of the tangent from P. First, visualize this as a right-angled triangle OAP, where A is the point of contact. OA is the radius (5 cm), OP is the hypotenuse (13 cm), and AP is the tangent length. Using the Pythagorean theorem, we can easily calculate AP. This shows how the tangent-radius property and Pythagoras theorem work hand-in-hand.

1Draw diagram: ΔOAP
2OA = radius = 5 cm
3OP = distance from centre = 13 cm
4∠OAP = 90° (Tangent-Radius Theorem)
5Apply Pythagoras Theorem: OP² = OA² + AP²
613² = 5² + AP² → 169 = 25 + AP²
7AP² = 169 - 25 = 144 → AP = 12 cm

Imagine drawing a horizontal diameter, then drawing tangents at each end; they will be vertical and parallel. This concept is straightforward but important.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Confusing secant with chord: A chord is a segment, a secant is a line.
  • Assuming a line is a tangent if it 'looks like' it touches at one point; it must be proven.
  • Incorrectly applying Pythagoras theorem (e.g., using tangent as hypotenuse).

🎙️ It's natural to make mistakes, but being aware of common pitfalls can help you avoid them. A frequent error is confusing a secant (an infinite line) with a chord (a line segment inside the circle). Also, never assume a line is a tangent just by looking at a diagram; always verify the single point of contact or the perpendicularity property. Finally, be careful when applying Pythagoras theorem; the hypotenuse is always the longest side, opposite the right angle.

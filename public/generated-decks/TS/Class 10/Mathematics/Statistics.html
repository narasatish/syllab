Primary vs. Secondary Data

  • Primary data: Collected directly by the investigator for the first time.
  • Secondary data: Already exists, collected by someone else.
  • Both types are useful but have different implications.

🎙️ When you conduct a survey yourself to know your classmates' favorite sports, that's primary data. But if you read a report from a sports magazine about popular sports in India, that's secondary data. Primary data is fresh and tailor-made, while secondary data is readily available but might not perfectly fit your exact needs.

Ungrouped Frequency Distribution

  • Used when the number of distinct values is small.
  • Each distinct value is listed with its frequency.
  • Frequency: How many times a value appears.

🎙️ Let's say we asked 10 students how many apples they ate last week. If the answers were 1, 2, 1, 3, 2, 1, 0, 2, 3, 1, we can create a table showing '0 apples - 1 student', '1 apple - 4 students', '2 apples - 3 students', '3 apples - 2 students'. This is an ungrouped frequency distribution.

Grouped Frequency Distribution

  • Used when data has a wide range of values.
  • Data is divided into class intervals or groups.
  • Each interval has a frequency.

🎙️ If we collect the heights of 100 students, it's unlikely many will have the exact same height. Instead of listing every single height, we group them, for example, '150-155 cm', '155-160 cm', and so on. This makes the data manageable and easier to analyze, showing how many students fall into each height range.

Mean: The Arithmetic Average

  • Most common measure of central tendency.
  • Calculated by summing all values and dividing by the count.
  • Sensitive to extreme values (outliers).

🎙️ The mean is what you usually think of as the 'average'. For example, if you want to find your average marks across all subjects, you add up all your marks and divide by the number of subjects. It gives a good overall sense of the data, but remember, a single very high or very low score can pull the mean in that direction.

⚖️

Mean for Ungrouped Data

  • Formula: Sum of all observations / Total number of observations.
  • Symbol: \(\bar{x}\) (read as 'x-bar').
  • Example: Marks of 5 students: 10, 15, 20, 25, 30. Mean = (10+15+20+25+30)/5 = 100/5 = 20.

🎙️ This is the most straightforward way to calculate the mean. You simply add up all the numbers you have and then divide by how many numbers there are. It's like sharing a total amount equally among a group.

1Sum all values
2Count values
3Divide sum by count
13 / 25

Mean for Grouped Data: Direct Method

  • Used when data is in a frequency distribution table.
  • Find the mid-point (class mark, \(x_i\)) for each class interval.
  • Formula: \(\bar{x} = \frac{\sum f_i x_i}{\sum f_i}\)

🎙️ When data is grouped, we don't have individual values. So, we assume that the mid-point of each class interval represents all the values in that interval. We then multiply this mid-point by its frequency and sum these products, finally dividing by the total frequency.

➕
Summing Products
14 / 25

Example: Mean (Direct Method)

  • Class Interval | Frequency (\(f_i\)) | Mid-point (\(x_i\)) | \(f_i x_i\)
  • 0-10 | 2 | 5 | 10
  • 10-20 | 3 | 15 | 45
  • 20-30 | 5 | 25 | 125
  • 30-40 | 4 | 35 | 140
  • Total \(\sum f_i=14\), \(\sum f_i x_i=320\). Mean = 320/14 \(\approx\) 22.86

🎙️ Here's a step-by-step example. First, calculate the midpoint for each interval. Then, multiply each midpoint by its frequency. Sum up the frequencies and the products. Finally, divide the sum of products by the sum of frequencies to get the mean. This gives us an average value for the grouped data.

1Find mid-points (\(x_i\))
2Calculate \(f_i x_i\)
3Sum \(f_i\) and \(f_i x_i\)
4Divide \(\sum f_i x_i\) by \(\sum f_i\)

Median: The Middle Ground

  • The middle value of a dataset when arranged in order.
  • Not affected by extreme values (unlike mean).
  • Gives a sense of the 'typical' value without being skewed.

🎙️ The median is literally the middle number. If you line up all your friends by height, the person exactly in the middle is the median height. It's a very useful measure when your data might have some unusually high or low values that would distort the mean.

Median for Ungrouped Data

  • Step 1: Arrange data in ascending or descending order.
  • Step 2: If 'n' (number of observations) is odd, Median = \(\left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)^{th}\) observation.
  • Step 3: If 'n' is even, Median = Average of \(\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)^{th}\) and \(\left(\frac{n}{2}+1}\right)^{th}\) observations.
  • Example: 2, 5, 7, 8, 10. n=5 (odd). Median = \((5+1)/2 = 3^{rd}\) obs = 7.

🎙️ The first and most crucial step for finding the median is to sort your data. Once sorted, if you have an odd number of items, the median is right in the middle. If you have an even number, there are two 'middle' numbers, so you take their average.

1Order the data
2Find middle position
3Identify middle value(s)

Median for Grouped Data

  • Requires cumulative frequency to find the median class.
  • Median Class: The class interval where \(\frac{N}{2}\) (half of total frequency) lies.
  • Formula: \(Median = L + \left(\frac{\frac{N}{2} - cf}{f}\right) \times h\) (where L=lower limit, N=total freq, cf=cum. freq. of class preceding median class, f=freq of median class, h=class size).

🎙️ For grouped data, we can't find an exact middle value. Instead, we find a 'median class' – the interval where the median is expected to fall. We use cumulative frequency to locate this class and then apply a specific formula to estimate the median more precisely within that class.

Mode: The Most Popular Value

  • The value that appears most frequently in a dataset.
  • A dataset can have one mode (unimodal), multiple modes (multimodal), or no mode.
  • Useful for qualitative data (e.g., favorite color).

🎙️ The mode is simply the value that shows up most often. If you ask your friends what their favorite ice cream flavor is, the flavor chosen by the most people would be the mode. It's great for understanding preferences or the most typical item in a category.

Mode for Ungrouped Data

  • Simply count the frequency of each observation.
  • The observation with the highest frequency is the mode.
  • Example: 2, 3, 5, 2, 7, 2, 8. The value '2' appears 3 times, which is the most. So, Mode = 2.

🎙️ Finding the mode for ungrouped data is quite easy. You just look through your list of numbers and see which one repeats the most. If two numbers repeat the same highest number of times, then both are modes.

Mode for Grouped Data

  • Identify the 'modal class' - the class interval with the highest frequency.
  • Formula: \(Mode = L + \left(\frac{f_1 - f_0}{2f_1 - f_0 - f_2}\right) \times h\) (where L=lower limit of modal class, \(f_1\)=freq of modal class, \(f_0\)=freq of preceding class, \(f_2\)=freq of succeeding class, h=class size).

🎙️ Similar to the median, for grouped data, we first find the 'modal class', which is the class interval with the highest frequency. This class contains the mode. Then, we use a specific formula that considers the frequencies of the modal class and its adjacent classes to calculate a more precise mode.

Relationship: Mean, Median, Mode

  • For moderately skewed distributions, an empirical relationship exists.
  • Formula: Mode \(\approx\) 3 Median - 2 Mean.
  • Helps estimate one measure if the other two are known.

🎙️ While Mean, Median, and Mode are distinct measures, they are often related, especially in distributions that are not perfectly symmetrical. This empirical formula provides a useful approximation, allowing us to estimate one measure if we know the other two. It's a handy shortcut in many statistical analyses.

When to Use Which Measure?

  • Mean: Best for symmetrical data with no outliers (e.g., average test scores).
  • Median: Best for skewed data or data with outliers (e.g., average income, property prices).
  • Mode: Best for categorical data or finding the most frequent item (e.g., popular shoe size, favorite color).

🎙️ It's important to know when to use each measure. The mean is great for balanced data. The median is more robust when extreme values are present. And the mode is perfect for understanding preferences or the most common category. Choosing the right measure gives you the most accurate insight into your data.

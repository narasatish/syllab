Our Main Star: What is a Polynomial?

  • A polynomial is a special type of algebraic expression.
  • The powers (exponents) of the variables must be whole numbers (0, 1, 2, 3...).
  • No negative or fractional powers are allowed.

🎙️ Not all algebraic expressions are polynomials. For an expression to be a polynomial, a key rule is that the powers of its variables must always be whole numbers. This is a fundamental definition to remember.

🌟
Math Star

Polynomial or Not? Let's See!

  • Polynomial: 3x² + 2x - 1 (powers 2, 1, 0 - all whole numbers)
  • NOT Polynomial: √x + 5 (power of x is 1/2 - not a whole number)
  • NOT Polynomial: 1/x + 2 (power of x is -1 - not a whole number)

🎙️ Look at these examples carefully. The crucial difference lies in the exponents of the variables. If you see square roots of variables or variables in the denominator, it's generally not a polynomial.

The Power Level: Degree of a Polynomial

  • The degree of a polynomial is the highest power of the variable in the polynomial.
  • For a polynomial in one variable, find the largest exponent.
  • Example: In 5x³ + 2x² - 7, the highest power is 3, so the degree is 3.

🎙️ The degree tells us the 'power level' of the polynomial. It's simply the largest exponent you find on any variable within the polynomial. This number is very important for classifying and understanding polynomials.

Classifying Polynomials by Degree

  • Linear Polynomial: Degree 1 (e.g., 2x + 3)
  • Quadratic Polynomial: Degree 2 (e.g., x² - 4x + 1)
  • Cubic Polynomial: Degree 3 (e.g., 3x³ + 2x² - 5x + 8)

🎙️ We classify polynomials based on their degree because it helps us predict their behavior and properties. Linear, quadratic, and cubic are the most common types you'll encounter and are named after their highest power.

Classifying Polynomials by Terms

  • Monomial: One term (e.g., 5x, 7y²)
  • Binomial: Two terms (e.g., 2x + 3, x² - 4)
  • Trinomial: Three terms (e.g., x² + 2x - 1)

🎙️ We can also classify polynomials by the number of terms they have. 'Mono' means one, 'Bi' means two, and 'Tri' means three. This classification is simpler and helps describe the polynomial's structure.

What's the Value? Evaluating a Polynomial

  • The value of a polynomial P(x) at a specific value of x (say, x=k) is found by substituting 'k' for 'x'.
  • We write this as P(k).
  • It's like finding the output of a machine when you put in a specific input.

🎙️ Evaluating a polynomial means finding its numerical result when you replace the variable with a given number. This is a fundamental skill and helps us understand what the polynomial 'produces' for different inputs.

➡️
Input-Output

Example: Finding P(2) for P(x) = x² + 2x + 1

  • Step 1: Substitute x=2 into the polynomial.
  • P(2) = (2)² + 2(2) + 1
  • Step 2: Simplify the terms.
  • P(2) = 4 + 4 + 1
  • Step 3: Calculate the final result. P(2) = 9.

🎙️ Let's walk through an example. To find P(2), we replace every 'x' with '2'. Then, we follow the order of operations to simplify and get the final value. It's a straightforward substitution and calculation process.

2Simplify terms

The Special Numbers: Zeroes of a Polynomial

  • A zero of a polynomial P(x) is a value of x for which P(x) becomes 0.
  • It's the specific input that makes the polynomial's output equal to zero.
  • If P(k) = 0, then 'k' is a zero of the polynomial P(x).

🎙️ Zeroes are very special numbers for any polynomial. They are the values that, when plugged into the polynomial, make the entire expression evaluate to zero. Finding zeroes is a crucial part of solving many mathematical problems.

Visualizing Zeroes: Graphs and X-axis

  • At these points, the y-value (which is P(x)) is zero.
  • This gives us a visual way to find zeroes.

🎙️ Graphs provide a powerful visual tool. Whenever a polynomial's graph crosses or touches the x-axis, that x-coordinate is a zero of the polynomial. This geometric interpretation is very important for understanding zeroes.

How Many Zeroes Can There Be?

  • The maximum number of zeroes a polynomial can have is equal to its degree.
  • A linear polynomial (degree 1) has at most one zero.
  • A quadratic polynomial (degree 2) has at most two zeroes.
  • A cubic polynomial (degree 3) has at most three zeroes.

🎙️ This is a key property: the degree of a polynomial tells you the *maximum* number of zeroes it can have. A polynomial of degree 'n' will have at most 'n' zeroes. This helps us know what to expect when solving for zeroes.

Zeroes & Coefficients: Linear Link

  • For a linear polynomial P(x) = ax + b (where a ≠ 0),
  • The zero is given by x = -b/a.
  • This directly relates the zero to the constant term (b) and the coefficient of x (a).

🎙️ For simple linear polynomials, finding the zero is straightforward. You just set P(x) = 0 and solve for x. The formula x = -b/a shows a direct relationship between the zero and the polynomial's coefficients.

🔗
Direct Connection

Zeroes & Coefficients: Quadratic Connection

  • For a quadratic polynomial P(x) = ax² + bx + c (where a ≠ 0), let its zeroes be α and β.
  • Sum of zeroes (α + β) = -b/a
  • Product of zeroes (αβ) = c/a

🎙️ This is a very important relationship for quadratic polynomials! The sum and product of the zeroes can be directly found using the coefficients of the polynomial. You don't even need to find the zeroes first to know their sum and product.

🤝
Relating Parts

Example: Verifying Zeroes-Coefficient Relation

  • Polynomial: x² - 5x + 6. Zeroes are 2 and 3.
  • Step 1: Calculate sum of zeroes: 2 + 3 = 5.
  • Step 2: Calculate -b/a: -(-5)/1 = 5.
  • Step 3: Calculate product of zeroes: 2 × 3 = 6.
  • Step 4: Calculate c/a: 6/1 = 6. (Relations verified!)

🎙️ Let's see this in action. For the polynomial x² - 5x + 6, the zeroes are 2 and 3. We can easily verify that their sum and product match the formulas derived from the coefficients. This confirms the relationship.

2Calculate Sum/Product

Building a Polynomial from its Zeroes

  • If α and β are the zeroes of a quadratic polynomial, then the polynomial can be written as:
  • P(x) = k [x² - (α + β)x + αβ]
  • Where 'k' is any non-zero real number (often k=1 for simplicity).

🎙️ This formula is incredibly useful! If you know the zeroes of a quadratic polynomial, you can reconstruct the polynomial itself. Just find the sum and product of the zeroes, substitute them into the formula, and you're done.

🏗️
Constructing

Sharing The Polynomials: Division Algorithm

  • For any two polynomials P(x) and G(x) (where G(x) ≠ 0),
  • We can find polynomials Q(x) (quotient) and R(x) (remainder) such that:
  • P(x) = G(x) × Q(x) + R(x)
  • Where R(x) = 0 or degree of R(x) < degree of G(x).

🎙️ This is just like the division algorithm you learned for numbers, but applied to polynomials. When you divide one polynomial by another, you get a quotient and a remainder, just like with integers. This is fundamental for finding factors and zeroes.

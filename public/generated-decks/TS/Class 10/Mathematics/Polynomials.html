What is an Algebraic Expression?

  • A combination of constants, variables, and mathematical operations.
  • Operations include addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division.
  • Example: 2x + 5, 3y - 7z

🎙️ An algebraic expression is like a recipe in math. It combines numbers, letters, and operations. The letters are called variables, and they can represent different values. It's the building block for what we'll learn today.

Understanding "Terms" in an Expression

  • Parts of an expression separated by '+' or '-' signs.
  • Each term can be a constant, a variable, or a product/quotient of both.
  • Example: In 3x² + 2x - 5, the terms are 3x², 2x, and -5.

🎙️ Think of terms as individual ingredients in our mathematical recipe. They are distinct parts of an expression. For instance, in '3x² + 2x - 5', '3x²' is one term, '2x' is another, and '-5' is the third term.

Constants vs. Variables

  • Constants: Fixed numerical values (e.g., 5, -2, 1/2).
  • Variables: Symbols (letters) that can take different values (e.g., x, y, a).
  • Example: In 3x + 7, '3' and '7' are constants, 'x' is a variable.

🎙️ Constants are like fixed prices – they don't change. Variables are like quantities you buy – they can change depending on what you need. Understanding this difference is crucial for algebraic thinking.

What is a Coefficient?

  • The numerical factor multiplying a variable part in a term.
  • It tells us 'how many' of that variable part we have.
  • Example: In 5x², '5' is the coefficient of x². In -y, '-1' is the coefficient of y.

🎙️ The coefficient is the number that stands in front of a variable. It directly multiplies the variable. For example, if you have '3 apples', '3' is the coefficient telling you how many apples you have.

Defining a Polynomial

  • An algebraic expression where the powers of the variables are ONLY non-negative integers (0, 1, 2, 3...).
  • No negative powers, no fractional powers, no variables in the denominator.
  • Example: 3x² + 2x - 5 is a polynomial. 1/x or √x are NOT allowed.

🎙️ Now we combine our understanding of terms, constants, and variables. A polynomial is a special type of algebraic expression. The key rule is that the powers of the variables must be whole numbers, never negative or fractions.

Polynomial or Not? Let's Check!

  • Polynomial: 4x³ - 2x + 1 (Powers are 3, 1, 0 - all non-negative integers).
  • Not a Polynomial: 3√x + 5 (√x means x^(1/2), a fractional power).
  • Not a Polynomial: 2/x - 7 (1/x means x^(-1), a negative power).

🎙️ It's important to be able to identify what makes an expression a polynomial and what doesn't. Remember, if you see a variable under a square root or in the denominator, it's generally not a polynomial.

What is the Degree of a Polynomial?

  • The highest power (exponent) of the variable in a polynomial.
  • For a constant polynomial (e.g., 5), the degree is 0 (since 5 = 5x⁰).
  • Example: In 7x³ - 2x⁵ + x, the highest power is 5, so the degree is 5.

🎙️ The degree tells us the 'rank' or 'order' of a polynomial. It's simply the largest exponent you find on any variable in the polynomial. This is a very important characteristic for classifying polynomials.

Linear Polynomials: Degree 1

  • A polynomial with the highest degree of 1.
  • General form: ax + b, where 'a' is not zero.
  • Example: 2x + 3, y - 7. Its graph is a straight line.

🎙️ Linear polynomials are the simplest type, where the variable has a power of 1. When you graph them, they always form a straight line. Think of equations like distance = speed * time, where distance is a linear function of time.

📏
Straight Line

Quadratic Polynomials: Degree 2

  • A polynomial with the highest degree of 2.
  • General form: ax² + bx + c, where 'a' is not zero.
  • Example: x² - 5x + 6, 3y² + 1. Its graph is a parabola.

🎙️ Quadratic polynomials are very common in physics and engineering. They describe curved paths, like the trajectory of a ball thrown in the air. Their graphs are U-shaped curves called parabolas.

📐
Curved Shape

Cubic Polynomials: Degree 3

  • A polynomial with the highest degree of 3.
  • General form: ax³ + bx² + cx + d, where 'a' is not zero.
  • Example: 2x³ + 4x² - x + 1. Its graph has more turns.

🎙️ Cubic polynomials are the next step up. They can have more complex curves than quadratics. Think of them when calculating volumes, where dimensions are often multiplied three times, leading to a degree of 3.

Volume Shape

Monomials: One Term

  • A polynomial consisting of only one term.
  • Can be a constant, a variable, or a product of constants and variables.
  • Example: 5, x, 3y², -7abc.

🎙️ Monomials are the simplest polynomials in terms of structure – they have just one part. For example, '5' is a monomial, 'x' is a monomial, and '3y²' is also a monomial.

Binomials: Two Terms

  • A polynomial consisting of exactly two terms.
  • The two terms are separated by a '+' or '-' sign.
  • Example: x + 5, 2y - z, a² + b².

🎙️ Binomials are polynomials with two distinct terms. Think of 'bi' meaning two, like a bicycle has two wheels. So, an expression like 'x + 5' has two terms: 'x' and '5'.

Trinomials: Three Terms

  • A polynomial consisting of exactly three terms.
  • The three terms are separated by '+' or '-' signs.
  • Example: x² + 2x + 1, a - b + c, 3y³ - 2y + 5.

🎙️ Following the pattern, trinomials have three terms. 'Tri' means three, like a tricycle has three wheels. A classic example is the quadratic expression 'ax² + bx + c', which usually has three terms.

Real-Life Polynomial: Area of a Garden

  • Imagine a rectangular garden. Its length is (x + 3) meters and width is (x) meters.
  • Area = Length × Width = (x + 3) × x = x² + 3x.
  • This is a quadratic polynomial representing the garden's area!

🎙️ Polynomials are everywhere! If you design a rectangular garden, its area can be described by a polynomial. Here, the area 'x² + 3x' is a quadratic polynomial, which helps us calculate the space for different 'x' values.

Zeroes of a Polynomial

  • A 'zero' of a polynomial P(x) is a value of 'x' for which P(x) = 0.
  • It's also called a 'root' of the polynomial equation P(x) = 0.
  • Essentially, it's where the polynomial's graph crosses the x-axis.

🎙️ The zeroes of a polynomial are incredibly important. They are the specific values of the variable that make the entire polynomial equal to zero. Think of it as finding the 'solution' or the 'answer' to the polynomial equation.

🎯

Finding Zeroes: Linear Polynomial Example

  • Consider the linear polynomial P(x) = 2x - 6.
  • To find the zero, set P(x) = 0: 2x - 6 = 0.
  • Solve for x: 2x = 6, so x = 3.
  • Thus, '3' is the zero of the polynomial 2x - 6.

🎙️ Let's find the zero for a linear polynomial. It's a straightforward process: set the polynomial equal to zero and then solve for 'x'. In this example, when x is 3, the polynomial 2x - 6 becomes 0.

Geometrical Meaning: Linear Zeroes

  • For a linear polynomial ax + b, its graph is a straight line.
  • The zero of the polynomial is the x-coordinate where the line intersects the x-axis.
  • Example: For P(x) = 2x - 6, the line y = 2x - 6 crosses the x-axis at x = 3.

🎙️ Visually, the zero of a linear polynomial is where its graph 'hits' the x-axis. This point tells us the x-value where the output of the polynomial (y-value) is exactly zero. A linear polynomial has at most one zero.

Geometrical Meaning: Quadratic Zeroes

  • For a quadratic polynomial ax² + bx + c, its graph is a parabola.
  • The zeroes are the x-coordinates where the parabola intersects the x-axis.
  • A quadratic polynomial can have 0, 1, or 2 distinct zeroes (real roots).

🎙️ For quadratic polynomials, the graph is a parabola. The zeroes are where this U-shaped curve crosses the x-axis. It can cross once (touching), twice (intersecting at two points), or not at all (no real zeroes).

Zeroes & Coefficients: Linear Polynomials

  • For a linear polynomial P(x) = ax + b (where a ≠ 0).
  • The zero is given by x = -b/a.
  • This shows a direct relationship between the zero and the coefficients 'a' and 'b'.

🎙️ There's a beautiful relationship between the zeroes and the coefficients of a polynomial. For a linear polynomial, the single zero can be directly found by using the formula -b/a. This is a powerful shortcut!

Summary: Key Takeaways from Polynomials

  • Polynomials are algebraic expressions with non-negative integer powers.
  • Classified by Degree (Linear, Quadratic, Cubic) and Terms (Monomial, Binomial, Trinomial).
  • Zeroes are x-values where P(x)=0, corresponding to x-intercepts on the graph.
  • There's a direct relationship between zeroes and coefficients.

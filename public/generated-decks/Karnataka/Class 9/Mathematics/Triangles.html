What is a Triangle?

  • A polygon with three sides.
  • Has three vertices and three angles.
  • Sum of its interior angles is always 180°.

🎙️ Imagine connecting three dots that are not in a straight line. The shape you get is a triangle! It's the simplest polygon and forms the basis for many complex shapes and structures.

Parts of a Triangle

  • Vertices: The three corner points (A, B, C).
  • Sides: The three line segments connecting vertices (AB, BC, CA).
  • Angles: The three angles formed at the vertices (∠A, ∠B, ∠C).

🎙️ Every triangle has these three essential components. Understanding them is crucial for describing and comparing different triangles. We often name triangles using their vertices, like Triangle ABC.

Congruent Figures: Identical Twins

  • Congruent means 'exactly the same shape and size'.
  • Two figures are congruent if one can be perfectly superimposed on the other.
  • Think of two identical ₹10 coins.

🎙️ When we say two figures are congruent, we mean they are perfect copies of each other. If you place one on top of the other, they would match perfectly, with no part sticking out. It's like having two identical biscuits from the same mould!

Congruent Triangles: Perfect Matches

  • Two triangles are congruent if all their corresponding sides and angles are equal.
  • We use the symbol '≅' to denote congruence (e.g., ΔABC ≅ ΔPQR).
  • Corresponding Parts of Congruent Triangles are Congruent (CPCTC).

🎙️ For triangles, congruence means not just looking similar, but being exactly the same in every measurement. This concept is fundamental because if we prove two triangles are congruent, we automatically know all their corresponding parts are equal.

SSS Congruence Criterion: Side-Side-Side

  • If three sides of one triangle are equal to the three corresponding sides of another triangle, then the triangles are congruent.
  • Analogy: If you fix three sticks of specific lengths, you can only form one unique triangle.

🎙️ The SSS criterion is very intuitive. If you have three specific lengths, there's only one way to connect them to form a triangle. This means the shape and size are uniquely determined, leading to congruence.

Example: SSS Congruence

  • Given: In ΔABC and ΔPQR,
  • AB = PQ, BC = QR, CA = RP.
  • Conclusion: ΔABC ≅ ΔPQR (by SSS criterion).

SAS Congruence Criterion: Side-Angle-Side

  • If two sides and the *included* angle of one triangle are equal to the corresponding two sides and the included angle of another triangle, then the triangles are congruent.
  • The angle *must* be between the two sides.

🎙️ Think of two arms (sides) connected at a shoulder joint (angle). If you fix the length of the arms and the angle between them, the position of your hands is fixed. This is similar to how SAS works for triangles.

Example: SAS Congruence

  • Given: In ΔXYZ and ΔLMN,
  • XY = LM, ∠Y = ∠M, YZ = MN.
  • Conclusion: ΔXYZ ≅ ΔLMN (by SAS criterion).

🎙️ It's vital to ensure the angle is *included* between the two sides. If the angle is not the included angle, then the SAS criterion cannot be applied. Pay close attention to the order of sides and angles.

ASA Congruence Criterion: Angle-Side-Angle

  • If two angles and the *included* side of one triangle are equal to the corresponding two angles and the included side of another triangle, then the triangles are congruent.
  • The side *must* be between the two angles.

🎙️ Imagine drawing a line segment (side) and then drawing two rays from its endpoints at specific angles. There's only one point where these rays will meet, forming a unique triangle. This illustrates the ASA criterion.

Example: ASA Congruence

  • Given: In ΔPQR and ΔSTU,
  • ∠Q = ∠T, QR = TU, ∠R = ∠U.
  • Conclusion: ΔPQR ≅ ΔSTU (by ASA criterion).

🎙️ Similar to SAS, the side must be *included* between the two angles. Always mark your diagrams to clearly show which parts are given as equal, it makes applying the criteria much easier.

AAS Congruence Criterion: Angle-Angle-Side

  • If two angles and a *non-included* side of one triangle are equal to the corresponding two angles and the non-included side of another triangle, then the triangles are congruent.
  • This is often a consequence of ASA, as the third angle is determined.

🎙️ AAS is a powerful rule. Since the sum of angles in a triangle is 180°, if two angles are known, the third angle is automatically determined. This means AAS can often be converted to ASA.

Example: AAS Congruence

  • Given: In ΔDEF and ΔGHI,
  • ∠D = ∠G, ∠E = ∠H, EF = HI.
  • Conclusion: ΔDEF ≅ ΔGHI (by AAS criterion).

🎙️ Notice that the side EF is not between angles D and E. This is why it's a non-included side. AAS is particularly useful when the included side is not given directly.

RHS Congruence Criterion: Right Angle-Hypotenuse-Side

  • If in two right-angled triangles, the hypotenuse and one side of one triangle are equal to the hypotenuse and one side of the other triangle, then the triangles are congruent.
  • Applicable *only* for right-angled triangles.

🎙️ RHS is a special case criterion exclusively for right-angled triangles. The hypotenuse is always the side opposite the right angle. This rule is a direct consequence of the Pythagorean theorem.

Example: RHS Congruence

  • Given: In right-angled ΔABC and ΔDEF (at B and E),
  • AC = DF (hypotenuses), AB = DE (sides).
  • Conclusion: ΔABC ≅ ΔDEF (by RHS criterion).

🎙️ Remember, for RHS, you *must* have a right angle, and the hypotenuse must be one of the equal sides. This criterion is very powerful for proving congruence in right triangles.

Properties of Isosceles Triangles

  • An isosceles triangle has two equal sides.
  • The angles opposite to the equal sides are also equal.
  • The bisector of the vertex angle is perpendicular to the base.

🎙️ Isosceles triangles have a beautiful symmetry. If two sides are equal, the angles 'looking' at those sides must also be equal. This property is very useful in solving geometric problems.

Theorem: Angles Opposite Equal Sides

  • Statement: Angles opposite to equal sides of an isosceles triangle are equal.

Triangle Inequalities: The Path Matters!

  • The sum of the lengths of any two sides of a triangle is greater than the length of the third side.
  • Analogy: The shortest path between two points is a straight line.
  • Example: If sides are 3, 4, 5, then 3+4 > 5, 4+5 > 3, 3+5 > 4.

🎙️ This inequality ensures that a triangle can actually be formed. If the sum of two sides wasn't greater than the third, the two shorter sides wouldn't 'meet' to form a vertex, or they would just lie on the third side.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Confusing SAS with SSA (Side-Side-Angle is NOT a congruence rule).
  • Not identifying the *included* angle/side correctly.
  • Incorrectly matching corresponding vertices/sides/angles.
  • Forgetting the 'right angle' condition for RHS.

🎙️ Many students make these errors, especially when applying congruence rules. Always double-check if the angle is truly *between* the two sides for SAS, or if the side is *between* the two angles for ASA. Careful labelling helps immensely.

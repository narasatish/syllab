What is a Circle?

  • Set of all points in a plane.
  • Equidistant from a fixed point.
  • The fixed point is the 'Centre'.

🎙️ Imagine a point in the middle, and then all the other points that are exactly the same distance away from it. If you connect all those points, you get a circle! The fixed point is called the centre.

Key Terms: Centre and Radius

  • Centre: The fixed point inside the circle.
  • Radius: Distance from the centre to any point on the circle.
  • All radii of a circle are equal in length.

🎙️ The Centre is the heart of the circle. The Radius is like an arm stretching from the centre to the edge. Think of it as the 'reach' of the circle. Every point on the circle is one radius-length away from the centre.

Key Terms: Diameter and Chord

  • Diameter: A chord passing through the centre.
  • It's the longest chord in a circle.
  • Chord: A line segment joining any two points on the circle.

🎙️ A chord connects any two points on the circle. If that chord happens to pass right through the centre, it's called a diameter. The diameter is always twice the length of the radius.

Key Terms: Arc and Segment

  • Arc: A continuous piece of a circle.
  • Segment: Region between a chord and its corresponding arc.
  • Can be major (larger) or minor (smaller).

🎙️ An arc is just a curved piece of the circle's boundary. When you draw a chord, it cuts the circle into two regions. Each region, bounded by the chord and an arc, is called a segment.

Key Terms: Sector

  • Sector: Region between two radii and their corresponding arc.
  • Looks like a 'slice of pizza' or a 'slice of cake'.
  • Can be major (larger) or minor (smaller).

🎙️ Unlike a segment which uses a chord, a sector uses two radii and the arc between them. Think of cutting a pizza or a cake from the centre outwards – that's a sector!

Angle Subtended by a Chord at the Centre

  • A chord AB forms triangle OAB with the centre O.
  • The angle ∠AOB is the angle subtended by chord AB at the centre.
  • Different chords can subtend different angles.

🎙️ When you connect the endpoints of a chord to the centre of the circle, you form an angle at the centre. This angle tells us how 'wide' the chord appears from the centre.

Angle Subtended by a Chord at a Point on the Circle

  • A chord AB forms triangle APB with a point P on the circle.
  • The angle ∠APB is the angle subtended by chord AB at point P.
  • This angle is often half the angle at the centre.

🎙️ Instead of the centre, if we pick any other point on the circle and connect the chord's endpoints to it, we get another angle. This angle has a special relationship with the angle at the centre, which we'll see later.

Theorem 1: Perpendicular from the Centre to a Chord

  • The perpendicular from the centre of a circle to a chord bisects the chord.
  • If OM ⊥ AB, then AM = MB.
  • This theorem is very useful for calculations.

🎙️ This is a fundamental theorem. It means if you drop a line straight down from the centre, meeting a chord at a 90-degree angle, that line will cut the chord into two exactly equal halves. It's like cutting a rope precisely in the middle.

Worked Example 1

  • In a circle with radius 5 cm, a chord is 8 cm long.
  • Find the distance of the chord from the centre.
  • Solution: Use Pythagoras theorem in ΔOMA (where M is midpoint).

🎙️ Let's apply the theorem. If the radius is 5 cm and the chord is 8 cm, then half the chord is 4 cm. Now, we have a right-angled triangle with hypotenuse 5 (radius) and one side 4 (half chord). We can find the other side (distance from centre) using Pythagoras. (5^2 = 4^2 + d^2 => 25 = 16 + d^2 => d^2 = 9 => d = 3 cm)

Converse of Theorem 1

  • The line drawn through the centre of a circle to bisect a chord is perpendicular to the chord.
  • If AM = MB, then OM ⊥ AB.
  • This is the reverse of the previous theorem.

🎙️ The converse theorem states the opposite: if a line from the centre cuts a chord into two equal halves, then that line *must* be perpendicular to the chord. Both theorems are crucial for solving problems.

Circle Through Three Non-Collinear Points

  • There is one and only one circle passing through three given non-collinear points.
  • Non-collinear means they don't lie on the same straight line.
  • This means you can always draw a unique circle through any three points not in a line.

🎙️ Imagine three dots on a paper, not in a straight line. You can always draw a perfect circle that passes through all three of them, and only one such circle exists. This is why tripods are stable – three points define a plane, and here, a circle.

Equal Chords and their Distances from the Centre

  • Equal chords of a circle (or of congruent circles) are equidistant from the centre.
  • If AB = CD, then distance from O to AB = distance from O to CD.
  • This means parallel equal chords are always the same distance away.

🎙️ If two chords in a circle have the exact same length, then their perpendicular distance from the centre of the circle will also be the same. This is a powerful property for comparing chords.

Converse of Theorem 2

  • Chords equidistant from the centre of a circle are equal in length.
  • If distance from O to AB = distance from O to CD, then AB = CD.
  • Another important inverse relationship.

🎙️ Just like before, the converse holds true. If you find two chords that are the same distance away from the centre, you can confidently say that those two chords must have the same length. It's a two-way street!

Angle Subtended by an Arc at the Centre and at any point on the Remaining Part of the Circle

  • The angle subtended by an arc at the centre is double the angle subtended by it at any point on the remaining part of the circle.
  • ∠AOB = 2 * ∠APB (where P is on the remaining arc).
  • This is a crucial relationship for many problems.

🎙️ This theorem is a cornerstone! If an arc creates an angle at the centre, and also an angle at any point on the circumference *not* on that arc, the angle at the centre will always be exactly twice as big. It's like looking at something from close up versus far away.

🎙️ If the angle at the centre is 70 degrees, then the angle at any point on the remaining part of the circumference will be half of that. So, it would be 35 degrees. Simple application of the theorem!

Angles in the Same Segment

  • Angles in the same segment of a circle are equal.
  • If points P and Q are in the same segment formed by chord AB, then ∠APB = ∠AQB.
  • This is a direct consequence of the previous theorem.

🎙️ Imagine two different people looking at the same chord from different points on the same arc. The angle their eyes make with the chord will be identical. This is because both angles are half of the same central angle.

What is a Cyclic Quadrilateral?

  • A quadrilateral whose all four vertices lie on a circle.
  • The vertices are 'concyclic'.
  • It's a special type of quadrilateral with unique properties.

🎙️ A cyclic quadrilateral is a four-sided shape where every corner touches the circle's edge. Not all quadrilaterals are cyclic, but those that are have some very interesting properties that make them special.

Theorem 3: Opposite Angles of a Cyclic Quadrilateral

  • The sum of either pair of opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral is 180°.
  • ∠A + ∠C = 180° and ∠B + ∠D = 180°.
  • This is a powerful tool for solving problems involving cyclic quadrilaterals.

🎙️ This is the most important property of a cyclic quadrilateral. If you add up the angles opposite each other, they will always sum up to 180 degrees. This makes them behave much like angles on a straight line, but within a circular context.

Worked Example 4

  • If ∠B = 100°, find ∠D.
  • Solution: Apply the cyclic quadrilateral theorem.

🎙️ Using the theorem, if angle A is 70 degrees, then angle C must be 180 - 70 = 110 degrees. Similarly, if angle B is 100 degrees, then angle D must be 180 - 100 = 80 degrees. It's quite straightforward!

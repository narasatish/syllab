What is a Circle?

  • Imagine a fixed point (the center).
  • Now, imagine drawing all points that are exactly the same distance from it.
  • That collection of points forms a circle!

🎙️ Think of a goat tied to a pole with a rope. As the goat walks around, keeping the rope taut, it traces a perfect circle on the ground. The pole is the center, and the rope's length is the fixed distance.

The Heart and Arm: Center and Radius

  • The **Center** is the fixed point inside the circle.
  • The **Radius** is the fixed distance from the center to any point on the circle.
  • All radii of the same circle are equal in length.

🎙️ Just like your heart is at the center of your body, the center is the core of the circle. The radius is like an arm extending from the center to touch the boundary. Remember, every arm in the same circle will be the same length!

Across the Circle: Diameter

  • A **Diameter** is a chord that passes through the center of the circle.
  • It's the longest chord possible in a circle.
  • The diameter is always twice the length of the radius (D = 2r).

🎙️ Imagine cutting a perfectly round cake exactly in half, passing your knife through the very center. The cut you make is the diameter. It's the widest 'straight path' you can take across the circle.

Connecting Points: Chord

  • A **Chord** is a line segment joining any two points on the circle.
  • Think of it as a 'bridge' connecting two points on the circular boundary.
  • The diameter is a special type of chord.

🎙️ If you pick any two points on the edge of a bangle and draw a straight line between them, that line is a chord. It doesn't have to pass through the center, but it can. The diameter is the longest possible chord.

Comparing Lengths: Radius, Diameter, Chord

  • Radius (r): Distance from center to circle.
  • Diameter (d): Longest chord, d = 2r.
  • Chord: Connects any two points on the circle.

🎙️ Let's visualize how these lengths compare. If the radius is '1 unit', the diameter will always be '2 units'. A chord can be any length up to the diameter. This helps us understand their relationships.

A Piece of the Boundary: Arc

  • An **Arc** is a continuous piece of a circle.
  • Like the curved edge of a pizza slice.
  • Arcs can be Minor (smaller) or Major (larger).

🎙️ When you cut a slice of pizza, the crust part is an arc. It's a curved line along the circle's edge. If the arc is less than half the circle, it's a minor arc; if it's more than half, it's a major arc.

The Full Journey: Circumference

  • The **Circumference** is the total length of the boundary of a circle.
  • It's like the perimeter of a polygon, but for a circle.
  • Calculated using the formula C = 2πr or C = πd.

🎙️ Imagine walking once all the way around a circular park. The total distance you cover is the circumference of the park. It's the 'length' of the circle itself. We'll explore its calculation in more detail later.

A Slice of Pizza: Sector

  • A **Sector** is the region enclosed by two radii and the arc between them.
  • It looks exactly like a slice of pizza or cake.
  • Can be a Minor Sector (small slice) or a Major Sector (large slice).

🎙️ When you cut a piece of pizza, you make two straight cuts from the center to the crust, and the crust itself forms an arc. The area enclosed by these two radii and the arc is called a sector. It's a region *within* the circle.

A Segment of Cookie: Segment

  • A **Segment** is the region enclosed by a chord and the arc between its endpoints.
  • Imagine cutting a round cookie with a straight knife.
  • Can be a Minor Segment (smaller part) or a Major Segment (larger part).

🎙️ Now, imagine you have a round cookie and you make one straight cut across it. The two pieces you get are segments. Each piece is bounded by the straight cut (the chord) and the curved edge (the arc).

Sector vs. Segment: What's the Difference?

  • Sector: formed by two radii and an arc (pizza slice).
  • Segment: formed by a chord and an arc (cookie piece).
  • One uses the center (radii), the other doesn't necessarily.

🎙️ It's easy to confuse sectors and segments, but remember the key difference: sectors always involve the center of the circle through their radii, while segments are defined by a chord and the arc it cuts off. Think pizza slice versus cookie piece!

Theorem 1: Perpendicular from Center to Chord

  • **Theorem:** The perpendicular from the center of a circle to a chord bisects the chord.
  • If you draw a line from the center that hits a chord at a 90-degree angle...
  • ...it will cut that chord into two exactly equal halves.

🎙️ Imagine a seesaw. If you place a support exactly in the middle (perpendicularly), it balances perfectly. Similarly, a line from the center that is perpendicular to a chord acts like a balance point, dividing the chord into two equal parts.

📐
Right angle

Worked Example 1: Using Theorem 1

  • A chord of length 16 cm is drawn in a circle of radius 10 cm.
  • Find the distance of the chord from the center.
  • We'll use the Pythagorean theorem here.

🎙️ Let's apply our first theorem. If we have a chord and its length, and the circle's radius, we can find how far the chord is from the center. By drawing a perpendicular from the center to the chord, we form a right-angled triangle, allowing us to use Pythagoras.

1Draw diagram, label O, M, A
2AM = MB = 16/2 = 8 cm
3In ΔOMA, OA² = OM² + AM²
410² = OM² + 8² → OM = 6 cm

Theorem 2: Line Bisecting Chord to Center

  • **Theorem:** The line drawn through the center of a circle to bisect a chord is perpendicular to the chord.
  • This is the converse of our previous theorem.
  • If a line from the center cuts a chord into two equal parts, it must be at a 90-degree angle.

🎙️ This theorem is the 'reverse' of the first one. If you know a line from the center cuts a chord exactly in half, then you automatically know that line forms a right angle with the chord. It's a powerful tool for proving perpendicularity.

Theorem 3: Equal Chords, Equal Distances

  • **Theorem:** Equal chords of a circle (or of congruent circles) are equidistant from the center.
  • If two chords have the same length, they will be the same distance away from the center.
  • Think of them as 'mirror images' in terms of distance.

🎙️ Imagine two identical boats floating on a circular pond. If they are the same length, they will naturally maintain the same distance from the exact center of the pond. This theorem establishes a beautiful symmetry within the circle.

Worked Example 2: Using Theorem 3

  • In a circle, two chords AB and CD are equal.
  • If AB is 5 cm from the center O, what is the distance of CD from O?
  • This is a direct application of the theorem.

🎙️ This example shows how straightforward this theorem is. If you know two chords are equal, you immediately know they are the same distance from the center. No complex calculations needed, just a clear understanding of the property.

1Given: Chord AB = Chord CD
2Distance of AB from O = 5 cm
3By Theorem 3: Equal chords are equidistant.
4Therefore, distance of CD from O = 5 cm.

Theorem 4: Chords Equidistant from Center

  • **Theorem:** Chords equidistant from the center of a circle are equal in length.
  • This is the converse of Theorem 3.
  • If two chords are the same distance from the center, then they must be equal in length.

🎙️ Just as with the perpendicular and bisection theorems, this is the flip side of the previous one. If you measure the distance of two chords from the center and find them to be equal, you can confidently say the chords themselves are equal in length. This is very useful in proofs.

Theorem 5: Angle Subtended by an Arc at the Center

  • **Theorem:** The angle subtended by an arc at the center is double the angle subtended by it at any point on the remaining part of the circle.
  • The angle 'looking' at the arc from the center is twice as big...
  • ...as the angle 'looking' at the same arc from the circumference.

🎙️ This is a very important theorem! Imagine an arc. If you stand at the center and look at it, the angle you form is twice as large as the angle you form if you stand anywhere else on the circle's edge and look at the same arc. It's like a magnifying effect at the center.

Worked Example 3: Using Theorem 5

  • If ∠AOB = 100° (angle at center), what is ∠ACB?
  • A and B are points on the circle, C is another point on the remaining arc.
  • We'll use the relationship between center and circumference angles.

🎙️ This example demonstrates the power of Theorem 5. If you know the angle formed at the center by an arc, you can immediately find the angle formed by the same arc at any point on the rest of the circle, simply by halving it.

1Given: ∠AOB = 100°
2Arc AB subtends ∠AOB at center O.
3Arc AB subtends ∠ACB at point C on remaining arc.
4By Theorem 5: ∠AOB = 2 × ∠ACB
5100° = 2 × ∠ACB → ∠ACB = 50°

Theorem 6: Angles in the Same Segment

  • **Theorem:** Angles in the same segment of a circle are equal.
  • If two or more angles are subtended by the same arc in the same segment...
  • ...then all those angles will have the exact same measure.

🎙️ Imagine multiple friends standing on the same side of a circular stadium, all looking at the same goalpost. No matter where they stand on that side, the angle their eyes make with the goalpost will be the same. This theorem is incredibly useful for finding unknown angles.

Common Mistakes to Avoid!

  • Confusing Sector and Segment definitions.
  • Assuming a chord is a diameter without proof.
  • Incorrectly applying angle theorems (e.g., not checking if angles are in the same segment).
  • Calculation errors in Pythagorean theorem.

🎙️ Even experts make mistakes! Be careful not to mix up sectors and segments. Always verify conditions for theorems before applying them, especially for angles. And double-check your arithmetic, especially when using the Pythagorean theorem.

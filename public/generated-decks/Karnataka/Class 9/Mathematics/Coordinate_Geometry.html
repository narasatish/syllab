The Cartesian Plane: Axes

  • The horizontal line is called the X-axis.
  • The vertical line is called the Y-axis.
  • Together, they form the Coordinate Axes, creating a grid.

🎙️ These two perpendicular lines create a grid-like system called the Cartesian plane, named after the French mathematician René Descartes, who introduced this concept.

The Origin: Our Starting Point

  • The point where the X-axis and Y-axis intersect is called the Origin.
  • Its coordinates are always (0, 0).
  • It's our central reference for all measurements.

🎙️ Just like the 'zero' on a measuring tape, the origin is the central point from which all distances and directions are measured in our coordinate system.

Starting Point

Dividing the Plane: Quadrants

  • The coordinate axes divide the plane into four distinct regions.
  • These regions are called Quadrants, numbered I, II, III, and IV counter-clockwise.

🎙️ The intersection of the X and Y axes creates four distinct sections, much like dividing a cake into four slices. We number them counter-clockwise starting from the top-right.

Coordinates of a Point: (x, y)

  • Every point in the plane has a unique pair of numbers (x, y) as its address.
  • The 'x' is the x-coordinate (Abscissa), representing horizontal distance.
  • The 'y' is the y-coordinate (Ordinate), representing vertical distance.

🎙️ This pair of numbers, always written as (x, y), acts like the unique address of a point, telling us exactly where it is located on the Cartesian plane.

Analogy: The 'Walk then Climb' Rule

  • Think of the 'x' coordinate as walking horizontally (left/right) from the origin.
  • Think of the 'y' coordinate as climbing/descending vertically (up/down) from that horizontal position.
  • Always move along the X-axis first, then along the Y-axis.

🎙️ This simple rule helps to correctly plot points. First, find your horizontal position according to the x-coordinate, and then your vertical position according to the y-coordinate.

Worked Example: Plotting (3, 2) - Step 2

Special Case: Points on the X-axis

  • Any point that lies on the X-axis has its y-coordinate as 0.
  • It will always be in the form (x, 0).
  • Example: (5, 0), (-3, 0).

🎙️ If you're only walking left or right and not climbing up or down, your vertical position (y-coordinate) will always be zero. These points lie directly on the X-axis.

↔️

Special Case: Points on the Y-axis

  • Any point that lies on the Y-axis has its x-coordinate as 0.
  • It will always be in the form (0, y).
  • Example: (0, 7), (0, -2).

🎙️ Similarly, if you're only moving up or down and not walking left or right, your horizontal position (x-coordinate) will be zero. These points lie directly on the Y-axis.

Concept Extension: Reflection (Mirror Image)

  • Just like a mirror, points can be 'reflected' across an axis.
  • If reflecting across the X-axis, the y-coordinate changes sign.
  • If reflecting across the Y-axis, the x-coordinate changes sign.

🎙️ This is a fun extension! Imagine the X-axis as a mirror. A point above it will have its reflection below it, and vice-versa, maintaining the same horizontal distance from the axis.

Worked Example: Reflection of (2, 3)

  • Reflect (2, 3) across the X-axis: The new point is (2, -3).
  • Reflect (2, 3) across the Y-axis: The new point is (-2, 3).

🎙️ Notice how only one coordinate changes its sign depending on which axis you are reflecting across. The absolute distance from the axis remains the same.

The Special Point: (0,0)

  • The origin (0,0) is the only point that lies on BOTH the X-axis and the Y-axis.
  • Its abscissa (x-coordinate) and ordinate (y-coordinate) are both zero.

🎙️ The origin is a unique and very important point in the coordinate system, acting as the central reference for all other points and measurements.

Common Mistakes: Watch Out!

  • Confusing (x, y) with (y, x) when plotting points.
  • Incorrectly assigning positive/negative signs in different quadrants.
  • Forgetting that points on axes have one coordinate as zero.

🎙️ Many students make these small errors. Always remember the 'walk then climb' rule and double-check your signs for coordinates in different quadrants to avoid them!

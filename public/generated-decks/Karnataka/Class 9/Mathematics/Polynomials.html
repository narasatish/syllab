What is a Polynomial?

  • A special type of algebraic expression.
  • Variables must have only whole number powers.
  • No variables under square roots or in the denominator.
  • Example: 3x² + 2x - 1 is a polynomial.

🎙️ Now, let's introduce polynomials! They are a specific kind of algebraic expression with a very important rule about the powers of their variables. This rule makes them well-behaved and predictable.

💡

The Special Condition: Whole Number Exponents

  • Powers of variables must be 0, 1, 2, 3, ... (non-negative integers).
  • Expressions like x^(1/2) or x^(-1) are NOT polynomials.
  • This is the defining characteristic of a polynomial.

🎙️ This is the most critical condition for an expression to be a polynomial. If you see fractional or negative powers on a variable, it's not a polynomial. Simple as that!

Degree of a Polynomial

  • The highest power of the variable in a polynomial.
  • For a constant polynomial (e.g., 5), the degree is 0.
  • Helps classify polynomials.

🎙️ The degree of a polynomial is like its 'rank'. It tells us the highest power to which the variable is raised. This is a very important characteristic for classifying and understanding polynomials.

Linear Polynomials (Degree 1)

  • Polynomials with the highest power of 1.
  • General form: ax + b, where a ≠ 0.
  • Example: 2x + 3, y - 5.

🎙️ Linear polynomials are the simplest type, where the variable has a power of 1. When you graph them, they always form a straight line, hence the name 'linear'.

Quadratic Polynomials (Degree 2)

  • Polynomials with the highest power of 2.
  • General form: ax² + bx + c, where a ≠ 0.
  • Example: x² - 4x + 4, 3y² + 2.

🎙️ Quadratic polynomials are very important and describe many phenomena, like the path of a thrown ball. Their graphs are always curves called parabolas.

Cubic Polynomials (Degree 3)

By Number of Terms: Monomial, Binomial, Trinomial

  • Monomial: One term (e.g., 5x, 7).

  • Trinomial: Three terms (e.g., x² + 2x + 1).

🎙️ Besides degree, we can also classify polynomials by the number of terms they contain. 'Mono' means one, 'Bi' means two, and 'Tri' means three.

The Special Zero Polynomial

  • The constant polynomial '0' is called the zero polynomial.
  • Its degree is undefined.
  • It's unique because it doesn't have a highest power for a variable.

🎙️ The number zero itself is a polynomial, known as the zero polynomial. Interestingly, its degree is considered undefined, as you can write it as 0x², 0x⁵, and so on.

Finding the Value of P(x)

  • P(x) denotes a polynomial in variable x.
  • To find P(a), substitute 'a' for 'x' in the polynomial.
  • Result is a numerical value.

🎙️ Sometimes we need to know the value of a polynomial for a specific input. We do this by simply replacing the variable 'x' with the given number and performing the calculation.

Calculation

Example: Evaluate P(x) at x=2

  • Given P(x) = x² + 3x - 4
  • Substitute x = 2 into the polynomial
  • P(2) = (2)² + 3(2) - 4
  • P(2) = 4 + 6 - 4 = 6

What are Zeroes of a Polynomial?

  • A real number 'a' is a zero of a polynomial P(x) if P(a) = 0.
  • Also called 'roots' of the polynomial equation P(x) = 0.
  • These are the x-values where the polynomial's graph crosses the x-axis.

🎙️ The zeroes of a polynomial are incredibly important. They are the specific values of 'x' that make the entire polynomial expression equal to zero. Think of them as the 'solution' to P(x)=0.

The Remainder Theorem

  • If P(x) is divided by a linear polynomial (x-a), the remainder is P(a).
  • Provides a shortcut to find remainders without long division.
  • Works for any polynomial P(x) and linear divisor.

🎙️ The Remainder Theorem is a powerful tool! It saves us from doing lengthy polynomial division. Just substitute the zero of the linear divisor into the polynomial, and you get the remainder directly.

➗
Division shortcut

Example: Using the Remainder Theorem

  • Find the remainder when P(x) = x³ + x² + x + 1 is divided by (x+1).
  • Zero of (x+1) is x = -1.
  • Remainder = P(-1) = (-1)³ + (-1)² + (-1) + 1
  • Remainder = -1 + 1 - 1 + 1 = 0.

🎙️ Let's apply the Remainder Theorem. For the divisor (x+1), its zero is -1. We substitute -1 into P(x) and calculate. In this case, the remainder is 0, which tells us something special!

The Factor Theorem

  • A special case of the Remainder Theorem.
  • If P(a) = 0, then (x-a) is a factor of P(x).
  • Conversely, if (x-a) is a factor of P(x), then P(a) = 0.

🎙️ The Factor Theorem is directly linked to the Remainder Theorem. If the remainder is zero, it means the divisor completely divides the polynomial, making it a factor. This helps us factorize polynomials.

✖️
Multiplication parts
22 / 25

Example: Using the Factor Theorem

  • Is (x-2) a factor of P(x) = x² - 4?
  • Zero of (x-2) is x = 2.
  • P(2) = (2)² - 4 = 4 - 4 = 0.
  • Since P(2) = 0, (x-2) is a factor of x² - 4.

🎙️ Let's use the Factor Theorem. To check if (x-2) is a factor of x² - 4, we find the zero of (x-2), which is 2. Then, we calculate P(2). If it's zero, then yes, it's a factor!

Recalling Algebraic Identities

  • Equations that are true for all values of the variables.
  • Useful for simplifying, expanding, and factorizing polynomials.
  • Examples: (a+b)² = a² + 2ab + b², (a-b)² = a² - 2ab + b², a² - b² = (a-b)(a+b).

🎙️ Remember those special algebraic identities you learned earlier? They are incredibly powerful tools when working with polynomials. They help us quickly expand, simplify, and factorize complex expressions.

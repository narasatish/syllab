What is Probability?

  • It's the measure of how likely an event is to occur.
  • Expressed as a number between 0 and 1.
  • Helps us predict future outcomes based on data.

🎙️ Simply put, probability tells us how probable, or likely, something is to happen. Think of it like a scale, where we quantify uncertainty with a number.

Experiment and Outcome

  • An `Experiment` is an action or process that leads to well-defined results.
  • An `Outcome` is a possible result of an experiment.

🎙️ In probability, we start with an 'experiment,' which is any activity we perform. Each possible result of that experiment is called an 'outcome'.

Random Experiment

  • An experiment where all possible outcomes are known.
  • But the exact outcome cannot be predicted beforehand.
  • Example: Tossing a coin, rolling a die.

🎙️ A special type of experiment is a 'random experiment.' You know all the things that *could* happen, but you can't say for sure *what* will happen until it's done.

What is a Trial?

  • A `Trial` is a single performance of an experiment.
  • Each trial produces an outcome.

🎙️ When we perform a random experiment once, that single performance is called a 'trial.' So, one coin toss is one trial, one die roll is one trial.

What is an Event?

  • An `Event` is a collection of one or more outcomes of an experiment.
  • It's what we are interested in happening.

🎙️ An 'event' is the specific thing we're looking for. It could be getting a 'head' when tossing a coin, or rolling an 'even number' when rolling a die.

Calculating Probability: The Formula

  • P(E) = (Number of Favorable Outcomes) / (Total Number of Outcomes)
  • P(E) is read as 'Probability of Event E'.

🎙️ This is the core formula for calculating probability. You count how many ways your specific event can happen and divide that by the total number of things that could possibly happen.

1Identify Event E
2Count Favorable Outcomes
3Count Total Outcomes
4Divide!

Worked Example: Coin Toss

  • What is the probability of getting a Head?
  • Total Outcomes = {H, T} = 2
  • Favorable Outcome (Head) = 1
  • P(Head) = 1/2

🎙️ For a single coin toss, there are two total outcomes. If we want a Head, there's only one way for that to happen. So, the probability is one divided by two, or 0.5.

Worked Example: Dice Roll

  • What is the probability of rolling an even number?
  • Total Outcomes = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6} = 6
  • Favorable Outcomes (Even) = {2, 4, 6} = 3
  • P(Even Number) = 3/6 = 1/2

🎙️ When rolling a die, there are six total outcomes. The even numbers are 2, 4, and 6, which are three favorable outcomes. So, the probability is three divided by six, which simplifies to one-half.

The Probability Scale

  • Probability values always range from 0 to 1.
  • 0 means an event is impossible.
  • 1 means an event is certain to happen.
  • 0.5 means an event is equally likely to happen or not happen.

🎙️ Remember, a probability can never be less than 0 or greater than 1. It's a fraction or a decimal that fits within this scale, indicating the degree of certainty.

Impossible Event

  • An event that cannot happen.
  • The number of favorable outcomes is 0.
  • Its probability is 0.

🎙️ If an event simply cannot occur, like rolling a 7 on a standard six-sided die, then it's an impossible event. The probability of such an event is always zero.

Sure Event (Certain Event)

  • An event that is certain to happen.
  • The number of favorable outcomes equals the total number of outcomes.
  • Its probability is 1.

🎙️ On the other hand, if an event is guaranteed to happen, like getting a number less than 7 when rolling a die, then it's a sure event. Its probability is always one.

Relative Frequency Approach to Probability

  • Based on actual observations from experiments.
  • Probability = (Number of times event occurred) / (Total number of trials)
  • Used when theoretical probability is hard to determine.

🎙️ Sometimes, we can't just count possibilities. We have to perform the experiment many times and see what happens. This observed frequency helps us estimate probability.

Worked Example: Drawing Cards

  • A standard deck has 52 cards.
  • What is the probability of drawing a King?
  • Total Outcomes = 52
  • Favorable Outcomes (Kings) = 4
  • P(King) = 4/52 = 1/13

🎙️ In a deck of 52 cards, there are four kings. So, the probability of drawing a king is 4 out of 52, which simplifies to 1 out of 13.

Worked Example: Survey Data

  • A survey of 100 students: 60 like Math, 40 like Science.
  • What is the probability a randomly chosen student likes Math?
  • Total Students = 100
  • Students liking Math = 60
  • P(Liking Math) = 60/100 = 3/5

🎙️ Here, we use the relative frequency approach. Out of 100 students surveyed, 60 liked Math. So, the probability of picking a Math-liking student is 60 out of 100.

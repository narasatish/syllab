Understanding Probability: The Science of Chance

  • Probability measures how likely an event is to occur.
  • It's a number between 0 and 1.
  • 0 means impossible, 1 means certain.

🎙️ Probability is simply a way to quantify how likely something is to happen. We express it as a number from 0 to 1. If an event has a probability of 0, it will never happen. If it has a probability of 1, it will definitely happen. Most events fall somewhere in between.

Important Terms: Experiment, Trial, Event

  • Experiment: An action with observable outcomes (e.g., tossing a coin).
  • Trial: A single performance of an experiment (e.g., one coin toss).
  • Event: A specific outcome or collection of outcomes (e.g., getting 'Heads').

🎙️ To talk about probability precisely, we use specific terms. An 'experiment' is any activity where we can observe results, like rolling a die. Each time we perform that activity, it's a 'trial'. And an 'event' is what we are interested in, such as rolling an even number.

Calculating Experimental Probability

  • Probability of an Event = (Number of Trials where the Event occurred) / (Total Number of Trials)
  • This is also called Empirical Probability.
  • It's based on actual observations.

🎙️ The formula for experimental probability is quite straightforward. You simply count how many times your desired event happened and divide it by the total number of times you performed the experiment. Remember, this probability can change if you do more trials.

➗
Formula for Probability

Example 1: Coin Toss Experiment

  • A coin is tossed 100 times.
  • Heads appeared 45 times, Tails appeared 55 times.
  • What is the experimental probability of getting Heads?

🎙️ Let's apply the formula. Here, the event we are interested in is 'getting Heads'. We saw it 45 times. The total number of trials was 100. So, the probability is 45 divided by 100, which simplifies to 0.45. Simple, isn't it?

1Event: Getting Heads
2Favourable Trials: 45
3Total Trials: 100
4P(Heads) = 45/100 = 0.45

Example 2: Dice Roll Experiment

  • A die is rolled 50 times.
  • The number '3' appeared 8 times.
  • What is the experimental probability of getting a '3'?

🎙️ In this example, our experiment is rolling a die, and our event is getting the number '3'. We performed 50 trials, and '3' showed up 8 times. So, the experimental probability is 8 divided by 50, which can be simplified to 4/25 or expressed as a decimal, 0.16.

1Event: Getting '3'
2Favourable Trials: 8
3Total Trials: 50
4P('3') = 8/50 = 4/25 = 0.16

Outcomes and Sample Space

  • Outcome: A possible result of an experiment (e.g., 'Heads' when tossing a coin).
  • Sample Space: The set of all possible outcomes of an experiment.
  • Represented by 'S'.

🎙️ An 'outcome' is simply any result that can happen in an experiment. The 'sample space' is the complete list of ALL such possible outcomes. For example, when you roll a single die, the sample space is {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}. It covers every single possibility.

Example: Coin and Die Together

  • Experiment: Tossing a coin AND rolling a die.
  • What are all the possible outcomes?
  • Let's list them systematically.

🎙️ When you combine two experiments, like a coin toss and a die roll, the sample space gets larger. For each outcome of the coin (Heads or Tails), there are 6 outcomes for the die. So, we multiply them to find the total number of outcomes: 2 x 6 = 12. Listing them helps visualize the sample space.

1Coin: H, T
2Die: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6
3Sample Space: (H,1), (H,2), ..., (T,6)
4Total Outcomes: 12

What is an Event?

  • An event is a subset of the sample space.
  • It's a specific outcome or a collection of outcomes.
  • Example: Getting an even number when rolling a die.

🎙️ An event is simply what you are looking for within the sample space. If the sample space is all possible results, an event is a specific group of those results that you are interested in. For example, if you roll a die, 'getting a number greater than 4' is an event, which includes outcomes {5, 6}.

The Probability Formula (Theoretical Probability)

  • P(Event) = (Number of Favourable Outcomes) / (Total Number of Possible Outcomes)
  • This is the classical or theoretical probability.
  • It assumes all outcomes are equally likely.

🎙️ This is the most fundamental formula in probability. It's used when you can list all possible outcomes and assume each one has an equal chance of happening. You just need to count the outcomes that satisfy your event and divide by the total number of outcomes in the sample space.

📊
Probability Ratio

Key Properties of Probability

  • The probability of any event E is always between 0 and 1 (inclusive).
  • 0 ≤ P(E) ≤ 1
  • P(E) = 0 for an Impossible Event, P(E) = 1 for a Sure Event.

🎙️ Remember, you can never have a probability less than 0 or greater than 1. A probability of 0 means the event absolutely cannot happen, while a probability of 1 means it absolutely must happen. All other probabilities fall somewhere within this range, often expressed as fractions or decimals.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Not simplifying fractions (e.g., 4/52 instead of 1/13).
  • Incorrectly identifying total outcomes or favourable outcomes.
  • Writing probability as a number greater than 1 or less than 0.
  • Confusing experimental and theoretical probability.

🎙️ Students often make a few common errors. Always simplify your probability fractions to their lowest terms. Double-check your counts for total and favourable outcomes. And critically, remember that probability must always be between 0 and 1. Also, understand when to use experimental data versus theoretical calculations.

Exam Tips for Probability

  • Clearly list your sample space (S) first.
  • Identify the event (E) and count its favourable outcomes.
  • Write the formula: P(E) = Favourable / Total.
  • Simplify your answer and ensure it's between 0 and 1.

🎙️ When tackling probability questions in exams, a systematic approach is key. Always start by defining your sample space. Then, clearly state the event you're calculating probability for. Write down the formula, substitute your values, and simplify. This structured method helps avoid errors and ensures you get full marks.

Rational Numbers: The p/q Form

  • A number 'r' is called a rational number if it can be written in the form p/q.
  • Here, 'p' and 'q' are integers, and 'q' is not equal to zero.
  • Examples: 1/2, 3/4, -5/7, 2 (as 2/1), 0 (as 0/1).

🎙️ This is the formal definition. Remember, the denominator 'q' can never be zero, as division by zero is undefined. All integers are rational numbers because they can be written as p/1.

Decimal Expansions: Terminating

  • When you divide 'p' by 'q' for a rational number, the decimal expansion can end.
  • The remainder becomes zero after a finite number of steps.
  • Examples: 1/2 = 0.5, 7/8 = 0.875, 1/4 = 0.25.

🎙️ Think of 0.50 rupees - it's a definite, fixed value. These are called terminating decimals.

Decimal Expansions: Non-Terminating Repeating

  • Sometimes, the decimal expansion doesn't end, but a block of digits repeats.
  • Examples: 1/3 = 0.333..., 1/7 = 0.142857142857...
  • These are also rational numbers!

🎙️ If you divide 1 by 3, you'll keep getting 3s. This repeating pattern is a key characteristic of another type of rational number decimal expansion.

Converting Repeating Decimals to p/q

  • Let x = the decimal. Multiply x by 10, 100, etc., to shift the repeating block.
  • Subtract the original equation to eliminate the repeating part.
  • Solve for x to get the p/q form.

🎙️ This is a common and important skill. For example, to convert 0.333... to a fraction: Let x = 0.333... Then 10x = 3.333... Subtracting, 9x = 3, so x = 3/9 = 1/3.

Irrational Numbers: Examples & Definition

  • A number 's' is called irrational if it cannot be written in the form p/q.
  • Famous examples: √2, √3, π (pi).
  • Their decimal expansions are non-terminating and non-repeating.

🎙️ You might have heard of pi (π) from circles. Its decimal expansion goes on forever without repeating. The square root of 2 is another classic example.

Locating √2 on the Number Line

  • We can use the Pythagorean theorem to locate √2.
  • Draw a unit square with vertices at (0,0), (1,0), (1,1), (0,1).
  • The diagonal from (0,0) to (1,1) has length √2.

🎙️ By constructing a right-angled triangle with sides of length 1 unit, the hypotenuse will be √2 units. We can then transfer this length to the number line.

Operations: Adding & Subtracting Irrationals

  • We can only add or subtract 'like' irrational terms (same radical part).
  • Examples: √2 + 3√2 = 4√2; 5√3 - 2√3 = 3√3.
  • If they are unlike, they remain as is: √2 + √3.

🎙️ Think of it like combining 'apples' and 'oranges'. You can add 2 apples and 3 apples to get 5 apples, but you can't combine apples and oranges into a single 'fruit' term.

Operations: Multiplying & Dividing Irrationals

  • Product/Quotient of two irrational numbers can be rational or irrational.
  • Examples: √2 * √3 = √6 (irrational); √2 * √2 = 2 (rational).
  • Rules: √a * √b = √(ab); √a / √b = √(a/b).

🎙️ This is where it gets interesting! Sometimes, multiplying two irrational numbers can result in a rational number, like √2 times √2 giving 2.

Rationalizing the Denominator

  • It's common practice to avoid irrational numbers in the denominator.
  • Multiply both numerator and denominator by a suitable irrational term.
  • Example: 1/√2 = (1 * √2) / (√2 * √2) = √2 / 2.

🎙️ This process makes calculations easier and standardizes the form of expressions. We essentially remove the irrationality from the bottom part of the fraction.

  • Represented by the symbol 'R'.

🎙️ Real numbers are the complete set of numbers we encounter in everyday mathematics. They cover every single point on the number line.

Properties of Real Numbers

  • Commutative Property: a+b = b+a; a*b = b*a.
  • Associative Property: (a+b)+c = a+(b+c); (a*b)*c = a*(b*c).
  • Distributive Property: a*(b+c) = a*b + a*c.

🎙️ These fundamental properties govern how real numbers behave under addition and multiplication. They are crucial for solving equations and simplifying expressions.

🔗

Laws of Exponents for Real Numbers

  • a^m * a^n = a^(m+n)
  • (a^m)^n = a^(mn)
  • a^m / a^n = a^(m-n) (if a ≠ 0)
  • a^0 = 1 (if a ≠ 0)
  • a^(-n) = 1/a^n

🎙️ These laws simplify working with powers and roots of real numbers. They extend naturally from integers to rational and even real exponents.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Confusing Whole Numbers with Natural Numbers (0 is the key!).
  • Assuming all square roots are irrational (√9 = 3 is rational).
  • Incorrectly rationalizing denominators (e.g., multiplying by only √a instead of conjugate).
  • Forgetting that 'q' cannot be zero in p/q form.

🎙️ Many students make these small errors. Being aware of them will help you avoid losing marks.

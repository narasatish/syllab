What is a Circle?

  • A closed curve where all points are equidistant from a central point.
  • The central point is called the 'center'.
  • The fixed distance is called the 'radius'.

🎙️ Let's start with the very basics. A circle is a perfectly round shape. Imagine a string tied to a pole, and you walk around the pole keeping the string taut. The path you trace is a circle. The pole is the center, and the string's length is the radius.

Key Parts of a Circle

  • Radius (r): Distance from center to any point on the circle.
  • Diameter (d): A chord passing through the center. d = 2r.
  • Chord: A line segment joining any two points on the circle.

🎙️ Remember these important terms: The radius 'r' is half the diameter 'd'. The diameter is the longest chord in a circle. These measurements are crucial for all our calculations today.

🎙️ Think of the circumference as the boundary of the circle. If you were to cut a circular wire and straighten it, its length would be the circumference. It tells us how long the 'path' around the circle is.

Calculating Circumference

  • The ratio of circumference to diameter is constant: π (pi).
  • Formula: Circumference (C) = 2πr or πd.
  • Value of π ≈ 22/7 or 3.14 (use as specified).

🎙️ The number 'pi' (π) is a very special mathematical constant. It's approximately 3.14 or 22/7. We use this constant to calculate the circumference. Remember, 'r' is the radius, and 'd' is the diameter.

📐
C = 2πr

Example: Bike Wheel's Distance

  • A bicycle wheel has a radius of 35 cm.
  • How much distance does it cover in one full rotation?
  • Use π = 22/7.

🎙️ Let's apply the formula. The distance covered in one rotation is equal to the wheel's circumference. So, C = 2 * (22/7) * 35. This gives us 220 cm. This is a practical application of circumference.

Area: The Space a Circle Covers

  • The amount of surface enclosed by the circle.
  • It's like how much paint you need to cover a circular table.
  • Measured in square units (cm², m²).

🎙️ While circumference is about the boundary, area is about the space inside. If you have a circular field, its area tells you how much land it occupies. It's always measured in square units.

Calculating Area of a Circle

  • Formula: Area (A) = πr².
  • Remember to square the radius, not multiply by 2.
  • Units are always square units.

🎙️ The formula for the area of a circle is A = πr². Be careful not to confuse it with the circumference formula. The 'r²' means 'r times r'. This is a very common formula you'll use often.

🌐
A = πr²

What is a Sector?

  • The region of a circle enclosed by two radii and their corresponding arc.
  • Minor Sector: The smaller part of the circle.
  • Major Sector: The larger part of the circle.

🎙️ Formally, a sector is defined by two radii and the arc between them. Just like a pizza slice, there's a smaller part (minor sector) and a larger part (major sector). The sum of their angles is 360 degrees.

🥧
Part of a Pie

Area of a Sector: Portion of the Whole

  • The area of a sector is a fraction of the total area of the circle.
  • This fraction is determined by the angle (θ) of the sector.
  • Formula: A_sector = (θ/360°) × πr².

🎙️ Since a sector is just a part of the circle, its area is a fraction of the total circle's area. The angle 'theta' (θ) is the angle at the center formed by the two radii. If the angle is 60°, it's 60/360 or 1/6th of the circle's area.

Fraction of Area

Example: Area of a Clock Sector

  • Find the area of a sector with radius 6 cm and angle 60°.
  • Use π = 22/7.
  • This could be a segment of a clock face.

🎙️ Here, r = 6 cm and θ = 60°. Plugging these values into the formula: A_sector = (60/360) * (22/7) * 6². This simplifies to (1/6) * (22/7) * 36 = 132/7 cm². Always write the units!

Arc Length: The Curved Edge

🎙️ Just as a sector has an area, its curved edge has a length, called the arc length. This is a part of the circle's circumference. The formula is similar to the area of a sector, but it uses the circumference formula instead of the area formula.

What is a Segment?

  • The region of a circle enclosed by a chord and its corresponding arc.
  • Minor Segment: The smaller part cut off by the chord.
  • Major Segment: The larger part remaining after cutting the minor segment.

🎙️ A segment is the area bounded by a chord and the arc it subtends. Just like sectors, there are minor and major segments. The major segment is simply the rest of the circle after removing the minor segment.

🔪
Cut Part

Area of a Segment: Sector Minus Triangle

  • Area of Minor Segment = Area of Minor Sector - Area of Triangle.
  • The triangle is formed by the two radii and the chord.
  • Formula for triangle area: (1/2)r²sinθ (for general θ) or (1/2)base×height.

🎙️ This is a key concept. To find the area of a segment, you first find the area of the sector that includes the segment. Then, you subtract the area of the triangle formed by the two radii and the chord. This leaves you with the segment's area.

➖
Area = Sector - Triangle

Example: Area of a Pond Section

  • Find the area of the minor segment of a circle with radius 10 cm and angle 90°.
  • Use π = 3.14.
  • This could be a section of a circular pond.

🎙️ First, calculate the sector area: (90/360) * 3.14 * 10² = 78.5 cm². Next, the triangle is a right-angled triangle with base and height as radii: (1/2) * 10 * 10 = 50 cm². So, the segment area is 78.5 - 50 = 28.5 cm².

Example: Shaded Area in a Square

  • A circle of radius 7 cm is cut from a square of side 14 cm.
  • Find the area of the remaining (shaded) portion.
  • Use π = 22/7.

🎙️ Here, we have a square and a circle. The area of the square is 14 cm * 14 cm = 196 cm². The area of the circle is (22/7) * 7² = 154 cm². The shaded area is the area of the square minus the area of the circle: 196 - 154 = 42 cm².

Watch Out! Common Mistakes

  • Confusing circumference and area formulas (2πr vs πr²).
  • Using the wrong value of π (e.g., 3.14 instead of 22/7 when specified).
  • Incorrectly calculating triangle area for segments.
  • Forgetting units or using wrong units (cm vs cm²).

🎙️ These are some common pitfalls. Always double-check which formula you're using. Pay attention to the value of π given in the question. Be precise with your calculations, especially for segments, and never forget to write the correct units for your answer.

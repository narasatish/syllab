What are Algebraic Expressions?

  • Combination of variables, constants, and operations.
  • Like addition, subtraction, multiplication, division.
  • Example: 2x + 5

🎙️ An algebraic expression is like a mathematical phrase. It combines numbers (constants), letters (variables), and mathematical operations to represent a value. It's the basic building block of algebra.

Breaking Down Expressions: Terms

  • Parts of an expression separated by '+' or '-'.
  • Each term can be a constant, a variable, or a product.
  • Example: In 2x + 5y - 3, the terms are 2x, 5y, and -3.

🎙️ Think of terms as the individual building blocks of an expression. They are the pieces that are added or subtracted together to form the whole expression. Identifying terms is the first step in understanding expressions.

🧩🧩🧩
Building Blocks

Variables and Constants

  • Variables: Symbols (like x, y) that can take different values.
  • Constants: Fixed numerical values that do not change.
  • Example: In 3x + 7, 'x' is a variable, '3' and '7' are constants.

🎙️ Variables are like placeholders that can change their value, while constants are numbers that always stay the same. Understanding this difference is key to working with algebraic expressions effectively.

Defining Polynomials

  • An algebraic expression with non-negative integer powers of variables.
  • Terms are combined using addition, subtraction, multiplication.
  • No division by variables, no negative/fractional exponents.

🎙️ A polynomial is a special kind of algebraic expression. The most important rule is that the powers of the variables must always be whole numbers, like 0, 1, 2, 3, and so on. This makes them predictable and easy to work with.

📜
The Definition
6 / 25

Polynomials vs. Non-Polynomials

  • Polynomial: 3x² + 2x - 1 (powers are 2, 1, 0)
  • Non-Polynomial: 3x⁻¹ + 2 (negative power)
  • Non-Polynomial: √x + 5 (fractional power)

🎙️ It's crucial to distinguish between them. Remember, if you see a variable in the denominator, or a square root of a variable, or a negative exponent, it's not a polynomial. Always check the powers of the variables.

Non-Polynomial (x⁻¹)

🎙️ Polynomials aren't just abstract math; they describe many real-world phenomena. From the trajectory of a rocket to the design of roller coasters, they are everywhere and help us understand the world.

The Degree of a Polynomial

  • The highest power of the variable in a polynomial.
  • Determines the polynomial's behavior and type.
  • Example: In 5x³ + 2x² - 7, the degree is 3.

🎙️ The degree is a very important characteristic of a polynomial. It tells us the 'rank' or 'order' of the polynomial and helps us classify it. Always look for the term with the largest exponent.

⬆️
Highest Power

Classifying Polynomials by Degree

  • Degree 1: Linear (e.g., ax+b)
  • Degree 2: Quadratic (e.g., ax²+bx+c)
  • Degree 3: Cubic (e.g., ax³+bx²+cx+d)

🎙️ Based on their degree, polynomials have specific names. Linear polynomials form straight lines when graphed, quadratics form parabolas, and cubics have more complex curves. These names are essential to remember.

Linear (x¹)
Quadratic (x²)
Cubic (x³)

Linear Polynomials (Degree 1)

  • General form: ax + b, where a ≠ 0.
  • Graph is always a straight line.
  • Example: Cost of 1 kg apples is ₹50. Total cost = 50x.

🎙️ Linear polynomials are the simplest type. Think of simple relationships where one quantity changes directly with another, like distance covered at a constant speed. They are fundamental in basic algebra.

📏
Straight Line
11 / 25

Quadratic Polynomials (Degree 2)

  • General form: ax² + bx + c, where a ≠ 0.
  • Graph is a parabola (U-shaped curve).
  • Example: Area of a square with side 'x' is x².

🎙️ Quadratic polynomials are very common in physics, like describing the path of a projectile or the shape of a satellite dish. The 'x²' term gives them their characteristic curved shape, known as a parabola.

🎢
Parabolic Curve

Cubic Polynomials (Degree 3)

  • General form: ax³ + bx² + cx + d, where a ≠ 0.
  • Graph has two turning points.
  • Example: Volume of a cube with side 'x' is x³.

🎙️ Cubic polynomials can model more complex situations. They are used in engineering to design curves for roads or in economics to model cost functions. Their graphs show more intricate patterns than linear or quadratic.

📦
Volume Calculation

Classifying Polynomials by Number of Terms

  • One term: Monomial (e.g., 5x²)
  • Two terms: Binomial (e.g., 3x + 7)
  • Three terms: Trinomial (e.g., x² - 2x + 1)

🎙️ Besides degree, we can also classify polynomials by how many terms they have. This helps us quickly describe their structure and recognize common forms. These classifications are quite intuitive.

Monomials (One Term)

  • A polynomial with exactly one term.
  • Can be a constant, a variable, or a product.
  • Example: 7, y, -4x², 5xy³

🎙️ A monomial is the simplest type of polynomial, consisting of just one part. It can be a simple number or a combination of numbers and variables multiplied together. They are the fundamental building blocks of all polynomials.

  • Terms are separated by '+' or '-'.
  • Example: x + 5, 2y² - 3, ab + c

🎙️ Binomials are expressions with exactly two distinct terms. They are very common in algebra, especially when we learn about factorization and special products. You'll encounter them frequently.

Trinomials (Three Terms)

  • A polynomial with exactly three terms.
  • Terms are separated by '+' or '-'.
  • Example: x² + 2x - 1, a + b + c

🎙️ Trinomials, as the name suggests, have three terms. Quadratic polynomials are often trinomials, and we'll spend a lot of time factoring them later on. Understanding their structure is important for solving quadratic equations.

Zeroes of a Polynomial

  • A value of the variable that makes the polynomial equal to zero.
  • Also called 'roots' of the polynomial.
  • Example: For P(x) = x - 2, the zero is x = 2 (because 2 - 2 = 0).

🎙️ Finding the zeroes of a polynomial is like finding the special input value that makes the entire expression evaluate to zero. It's a fundamental concept for solving polynomial equations and understanding their behavior.

🎯
Target Value

How to Find Zeroes of a Linear Polynomial

  • Set the polynomial equal to zero.
  • Solve the resulting linear equation for the variable.
  • Example: For P(x) = 2x + 6, set 2x + 6 = 0. Then 2x = -6, so x = -3.

🎙️ Finding zeroes for linear polynomials is straightforward. You just need to solve a simple linear equation. This skill is a prerequisite for understanding more complex polynomials and their solutions.

1Set P(x) = 0
2Isolate variable (x)
3Solve for x

Real-Life Application: Area Problem

  • A rectangular garden has length (x+5) meters and width (x) meters.
  • Its area is represented by the polynomial P(x) = x(x+5) = x² + 5x.
  • This is a quadratic polynomial, used to model area.

🎙️ Here's how polynomials help us in everyday life. If we know the dimensions of a garden in terms of 'x', we can represent its area using a polynomial. This helps in design, planning, and optimization.

Geometric Meaning of Zeroes

  • For a polynomial P(x), its zeroes are the x-coordinates where the graph of y = P(x) intersects the x-axis.
  • A linear polynomial has at most one zero.
  • A quadratic polynomial has at most two zeroes.

🎙️ When we plot a polynomial on a graph, its zeroes are simply the points where the graph crosses or touches the x-axis. This visual understanding is very powerful and connects algebra to geometry.

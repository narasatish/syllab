Recap: Right-Angled Triangles

  • One angle is 90 degrees.
  • Hypotenuse: Side opposite the right angle.
  • Opposite Side: Side opposite the reference angle.
  • Adjacent Side: Side next to the reference angle.

🎙️ Before we dive into applications, let's quickly remember the basics of a right-angled triangle. These definitions – hypotenuse, opposite, and adjacent – are crucial for setting up our trigonometric ratios correctly.

Recap: Trigonometric Ratios

  • Sine (sin θ) = Opposite / Hypotenuse
  • Cosine (cos θ) = Adjacent / Hypotenuse
  • Tangent (tan θ) = Opposite / Adjacent

🎙️ Remember SOH CAH TOA? This mnemonic helps us recall the three primary trigonometric ratios. These ratios link the angles of a right triangle to the lengths of its sides, which is the core idea we'll use.

Concept: Line of Sight

  • Imaginary line from observer's eye to the object.
  • It's how you 'look' at something.
  • Forms the hypotenuse of our imaginary triangle.

🎙️ The line of sight is a fundamental concept. Imagine you are looking at the top of a tree; the straight line from your eye to the treetop is your line of sight. This line will always be part of the right-angled triangle we construct.

Concept: Angle of Elevation

  • Angle between the line of sight and the horizontal.
  • When you look UP at an object.
  • Observer is below the object's height.

🎙️ When you tilt your head upwards to see something tall, like a kite in the sky, you are creating an angle of elevation. This angle is always measured from the horizontal ground or eye level, upwards to your line of sight.

Real Example: Angle of Elevation

  • You stand 10m from a building.
  • You look up at its top.
  • The angle formed is the angle of elevation.

🎙️ Let's visualize this. If you're standing on the ground and looking at the top of a tall building, the angle your line of sight makes with the ground is the angle of elevation. This angle, along with your distance from the building, can help us find the building's height.

Concept: Angle of Depression

  • Angle between the line of sight and the horizontal.
  • When you look DOWN at an object.
  • Observer is above the object's height.

🎙️ Conversely, if you are on a tall building or a cliff and look down at an object on the ground, the angle your line of sight makes with the horizontal is the angle of depression. Remember, the horizontal is always drawn from the observer's eye level.

Real Example: Angle of Depression

  • From a lighthouse, you spot a boat.
  • You look down at the boat.
  • The angle formed is the angle of depression.

🎙️ Imagine you are a lighthouse keeper. When you spot a ship far away on the sea, you're looking downwards. The angle between your horizontal line of sight (parallel to the sea) and your actual line of sight to the ship is the angle of depression. This is crucial for navigation.

Key Idea: Horizontal Level

  • Always parallel to the ground.
  • Reference for both elevation and depression angles.
  • Often drawn from the observer's eye level.

🎙️ It's vital to correctly identify the horizontal level in every problem. This imaginary line is always parallel to the ground. Both the angle of elevation and the angle of depression are measured with respect to this horizontal line.

Problem Type 1: Finding Height or Distance

  • Involves a single right-angled triangle.
  • Given one side and one angle (other than 90°).
  • Use appropriate trigonometric ratio (sin, cos, tan).

🎙️ The simplest type of problem involves a single right triangle. If you know one side length and one acute angle, you can use sine, cosine, or tangent to find any other unknown side. This is where your SOH CAH TOA knowledge comes in handy!

Worked Example 1: Height of a Pole

  • A pole casts a shadow 15m long.
  • The angle of elevation of the sun is 60°.
  • Find the height of the pole.

🎙️ Here, we have a right-angled triangle formed by the pole, its shadow, and the line of sight to the sun. The shadow is the adjacent side, and the pole's height is the opposite side. Which trigonometric ratio connects opposite and adjacent?

Problem Type 2: Two Triangles

  • Involves two right-angled triangles.
  • Often share a common side or are adjacent.
  • Requires forming equations from both triangles.

🎙️ Sometimes, a problem requires us to work with two right-angled triangles. These triangles might share a common height or a common base. We usually set up two equations using trigonometric ratios and solve them simultaneously to find the unknowns.

Worked Example 2: Width of a River

  • Observer on one bank, sees a tree on the opposite bank.
  • Angles of elevation from two points on the same bank.
  • Find the width of the river.

🎙️ Imagine you're trying to find the width of a river without crossing it. By taking two observations from different points on your bank, you create two distinct right-angled triangles. We can then use tangent to find the river's width.

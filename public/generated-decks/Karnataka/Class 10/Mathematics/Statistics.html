  • They help us summarize and compare data easily.

🎙️ Imagine trying to describe a whole class's performance with just one number. Measures of central tendency help us do exactly that! They give us a 'typical' value for our data.

Mean for Ungrouped Data

  • The most common 'average'.
  • Calculated by summing all values and dividing by the total count.
  • Formula: Mean (x̄) = (Sum of all observations) / (Total number of observations)

🎙️ If you have marks like 10, 20, 30, the mean is (10+20+30)/3 = 60/3 = 20. It's like distributing the total equally among all items.

➕
Sum and Divide

Mean for Grouped Data: Direct Method

  • For grouped data, we use class marks (midpoints) for calculations.
  • Formula: x̄ = Σ(fᵢ * xᵢ) / Σfᵢ
  • Where fᵢ is frequency and xᵢ is the class mark of the i-th class interval.

🎙️ Since we don't have individual values in grouped data, we assume each value in an interval is its midpoint or class mark. We then multiply this midpoint by its frequency and sum them up.

1Find Class Mark (xᵢ)
2Calculate fᵢ * xᵢ
3Sum fᵢ * xᵢ and fᵢ
4Divide
12 / 25

Example: Mean (Direct Method)

  • Class Interval | Frequency (fᵢ) | Class Mark (xᵢ) | fᵢ * xᵢ
  • 0-10 | 2 | 5 | 10
  • 10-20 | 3 | 15 | 45
  • 20-30 | 5 | 25 | 125
  • Σfᵢ = 10, Σ(fᵢxᵢ) = 180
  • Mean = 180 / 10 = 18

🎙️ Here's a step-by-step calculation. First, find the midpoint for each interval. Then, multiply it by its frequency. Sum these products and divide by the total frequency to get the mean.

Mean for Grouped Data: Assumed Mean Method

  • Simplifies calculations when xᵢ and fᵢ are large.
  • Choose an 'assumed mean' (A) from the class marks.
  • Formula: x̄ = A + [ Σ(fᵢ * dᵢ) / Σfᵢ ], where dᵢ = xᵢ - A

🎙️ Sometimes, the numbers in the direct method can become very large and tedious to multiply. The assumed mean method helps by shifting the origin, making the numbers smaller and easier to work with.

🤔

Example: Mean (Assumed Mean Method)

  • Using previous data: Assume A = 15 (from class mark 15)
  • Class Interval | fᵢ | xᵢ | dᵢ = xᵢ - 15 | fᵢ * dᵢ
  • 0-10 | 2 | 5 | -10 | -20
  • 10-20 | 3 | 15 | 0 | 0
  • 20-30 | 5 | 25 | 10 | 50
  • Σfᵢ = 10, Σ(fᵢdᵢ) = 30
  • Mean = 15 + (30 / 10) = 15 + 3 = 18

🎙️ Notice how the values for fᵢdᵢ are smaller, making the multiplication easier. The final mean is the same as the direct method, proving its accuracy.

Mean for Grouped Data: Step Deviation Method

  • Further simplifies calculations, especially when class intervals are equal.
  • Choose an assumed mean (A) and common class size (h).
  • Formula: x̄ = A + [ (Σ(fᵢ * uᵢ) / Σfᵢ) * h ], where uᵢ = (xᵢ - A) / h

🎙️ This method is an extension of the assumed mean method. If all your class intervals have the same width 'h', you can divide the deviations by 'h' to get even smaller numbers, making calculations even faster!

🪜
Stepping Up Efficiency
16 / 25

Example: Mean (Step Deviation Method)

  • Using previous data: A = 15, h = 10
  • Class Interval | fᵢ | xᵢ | dᵢ = xᵢ - 15 | uᵢ = dᵢ/10 | fᵢ * uᵢ
  • 0-10 | 2 | 5 | -10 | -1 | -2
  • 10-20 | 3 | 15 | 0 | 0 | 0
  • 20-30 | 5 | 25 | 10 | 1 | 5
  • Σfᵢ = 10, Σ(fᵢuᵢ) = 3
  • Mean = 15 + ( (3 / 10) * 10 ) = 15 + 3 = 18

🎙️ See how the 'uᵢ' values are now very small integers, simplifying the fᵢuᵢ product. This method is highly recommended for efficiency, especially in exams, when class intervals are uniform.

Median for Ungrouped Data

  • The 'middle' value of a dataset when arranged in order.
  • For odd number of observations: Middle value.
  • For even number of observations: Average of the two middle values.

🎙️ To find the median, always arrange your data first, either ascending or descending. The median is literally the value that splits your data into two equal halves.

Median for Grouped Data

  • First, find the 'median class' (the class containing the (N/2)-th observation).
  • Formula: Median = L + [ ( (N/2) - cf ) / f ] * h
  • L = lower limit of median class, N = total frequency, cf = cumulative frequency of class preceding median class, f = frequency of median class, h = class size.

🎙️ For grouped data, we can't find an exact middle value. Instead, we find the class interval where the median lies, called the median class, and then use a formula to pinpoint the median within that class.

1Find N/2
2Identify Median Class
3Apply Formula
20 / 25

Example: Median (Grouped Data)

  • Class Interval | fᵢ | cf
  • 0-10 | 2 | 2
  • 10-20 | 3 | 5
  • 20-30 | 5 | 10
  • N = 10, N/2 = 5. Median class is 10-20 (since cf=5 is here).
  • L=10, cf=2 (preceding), f=3, h=10.
  • Median = 10 + [ ( (10/2) - 2 ) / 3 ] * 10 = 10 + [ (5 - 2) / 3 ] * 10 = 10 + 3/3 * 10 = 10 + 10 = 20

🎙️ Here, N/2 is 5. The cumulative frequency of 5 falls in the 10-20 class, so that's our median class. We then plug in the values into the formula carefully to find the median.

Mode for Ungrouped Data

  • The value that appears most frequently in a dataset.
  • A dataset can have one mode (unimodal), multiple modes (multimodal), or no mode.
  • It's simply the most popular item.

🎙️ If you ask 10 friends their favorite color and 4 say 'blue', 3 say 'red', and 3 say 'green', then 'blue' is the mode because it occurred most often. It's the easiest to spot!

Mode for Grouped Data

  • First, identify the 'modal class' (the class with the highest frequency).
  • Formula: Mode = L + [ (f₁ - f₀) / (2f₁ - f₀ - f₂) ] * h
  • L = lower limit of modal class, f₁ = frequency of modal class, f₀ = frequency of class preceding modal class, f₂ = frequency of class succeeding modal class, h = class size.

🎙️ Similar to median, for grouped data, we find the class interval with the highest frequency – this is our modal class. Then, we use a specific formula that considers the frequencies of the classes around it to calculate the mode.

1Identify Modal Class
2Find f₀, f₁, f₂
3Apply Formula
23 / 25

Example: Mode (Grouped Data)

  • Class Interval | fᵢ
  • 0-10 | 2
  • 10-20 | 3
  • 20-30 | 5
  • Highest frequency is 5, so modal class is 20-30.
  • L=20, f₁=5, f₀=3 (preceding), f₂=0 (succeeding, or assume if no next class), h=10.
  • Mode = 20 + [ (5 - 3) / (2*5 - 3 - 0) ] * 10 = 20 + [ 2 / (10 - 3) ] * 10 = 20 + (2/7) * 10 = 20 + 2.86 = 22.86 (approx)

🎙️ In this example, the class 20-30 has the highest frequency of 5, making it the modal class. We then carefully substitute the values of L, f₁, f₀, f₂, and h into the formula to calculate the mode.

What is a Circle?

  • Imagine a fixed point (centre).
  • Imagine a string of fixed length (radius).
  • The path traced by the other end of the string is a circle.

🎙️ A circle is simply a collection of all points in a plane that are at a fixed distance from a fixed point. Think of drawing a rangoli: you hold a point fixed and move your hand around it at a constant distance. That's how a circle is formed!

A perfect round shape

Key Terms: Centre and Radius

  • Centre (O): The fixed point inside the circle.
  • Radius (r): The fixed distance from the centre to any point on the circle.
  • All radii of the same circle are equal.

🎙️ Just like the 'nābhi' (navel) is the central point of our body, the centre is the heart of the circle. The radius is like the length of a string you use to draw the circle – it's always the same distance from the centre to any point on the edge.

📍
The heart of the circle

Key Terms: Diameter and Chord

  • Chord: A line segment joining any two points on the circle.
  • Diameter (d): A chord passing through the centre of the circle.
  • The diameter is the longest chord and is twice the radius (d = 2r).

🎙️ Imagine cutting a pizza. Any straight cut across it is a chord. If your cut goes right through the middle, that's the diameter! It's the longest possible straight cut you can make across the pizza, and it always splits the circle into two equal halves.

↔️
Crossing the circle

Key Terms: Arc and Segment

  • Arc: A continuous piece of a circle.
  • Segment: The region bounded by a chord and an arc.
  • Minor segment (smaller) and Major segment (larger).

🎙️ Think of an arc as a curved part of the circle's boundary, like the crust of a pizza slice. When you draw a chord, it cuts the circle into two pieces. Each piece, along with its curved boundary, is called a segment. One is usually smaller (minor) and the other larger (major).

🍰
A piece of the circle

Key Terms: Sector

  • Sector: The region bounded by two radii and an arc.
  • Minor sector (smaller) and Major sector (larger).
  • Looks like a slice of pizza or cake.

🎙️ A sector is like a slice of pizza or a piece of a circular cake. It's formed by taking two radii and the arc between their endpoints. Just like segments, we have minor sectors and major sectors, depending on the size of the arc.

Line and a Circle: Case 1 - Non-intersecting Line

  • The line does not touch the circle at any point.
  • The shortest distance from the centre to the line is greater than the radius.
  • No common points between the line and the circle.

🎙️ Imagine a train track running parallel to a circular pond. The train track (line) never touches the pond (circle). This is a non-intersecting line. There are no points common to both the line and the circle.

🚫
No contact
12 / 30

Line and a Circle: Case 2 - Secant

  • The line intersects the circle at two distinct points.
  • The shortest distance from the centre to the line is less than the radius.
  • It cuts through the circle.

🎙️ Think of cutting a 'roti' with a knife. The knife (line) passes through the 'roti' (circle) at two points. This line is called a secant. It 'cuts' the circle and has two points in common with it.

✂️
Cutting through

Line and a Circle: Case 3 - Tangent

  • The line touches the circle at exactly one point.
  • The shortest distance from the centre to the line is equal to the radius.
  • It 'grazes' the circle at a single point.

🎙️ Imagine a car wheel rolling along a straight road. At any instant, the wheel (circle) touches the road (line) at only one point. This line is called a tangent. It just 'kisses' the circle at a unique point, called the point of contact.

Tangent: Definition and Point of Contact

  • A tangent to a circle is a line that intersects the circle at only one point.
  • This unique common point is called the point of contact.
  • The word 'tangent' comes from the Latin word 'tangere', meaning 'to touch'.

🎙️ The tangent is a very special line. It's like a line that just brushes past the circle without going inside. The single point where it touches is crucial for understanding its properties and related theorems. Remember, only one point of contact!

📏
A precise touch

Theorem 1: Tangent is Perpendicular to Radius

  • The tangent at any point of a circle is perpendicular to the radius through the point of contact.
  • This means the angle between the radius and the tangent at the point of contact is 90°.
  • This is a fundamental property of tangents.

🎙️ This theorem is extremely important and forms the basis for many problems. Imagine a string tied from the centre to the point where the car wheel touches the road. That string (radius) will always be at a right angle to the road (tangent). This property helps us solve many geometric problems.

📐
Right angle rule

Worked Example 1: Applying Theorem 1

  • Question: A tangent PQ at a point P of a circle of radius 5 cm meets a line through the centre O at a point Q so that OQ = 12 cm. Find the length of PQ.
  • Solution:

🎙️ Let's put Theorem 1 into practice. We have a right-angled triangle formed by the radius, the tangent, and the line from the centre to the external point. This means we can use the powerful Pythagoras theorem to find unknown lengths. Always start by drawing a clear diagram!

1Draw diagram: OP is radius (5cm), PQ is tangent, OQ=12cm.
2Apply Theorem 1: OP ⊥ PQ, so ΔOPQ is a right-angled triangle at P.
3Use Pythagoras Theorem: OQ² = OP² + PQ².
4Substitute values: 12² = 5² + PQ² → 144 = 25 + PQ².
5Calculate PQ: PQ² = 144 - 25 = 119 → PQ = √119 cm.

Case 1: Point Inside the Circle

  • If a point lies inside the circle, no tangent can be drawn through it.
  • Any line passing through an interior point will be a secant.
  • It will always intersect the circle at two points.

🎙️ Imagine trying to draw a line that just touches the edge of a 'roti' from a point inside it. It's impossible! Any line you draw from an inside point will always cut through the 'roti' at two places, making it a secant, not a tangent. So, zero tangents from an interior point.

❌
No tangent possible

Case 2: Point On the Circle

  • If a point lies on the circle, exactly one tangent can be drawn through it.
  • This tangent is unique for that specific point of contact.
  • We've already seen this in Theorem 1.

🎙️ If your point is exactly on the boundary of the circle, like a specific spot on the edge of a bangle, you can draw only one straight line that just touches the bangle at that spot. This is the unique tangent at that point of contact.

☝️
One unique tangent

Case 3: Point Outside the Circle

  • If a point lies outside the circle, exactly two tangents can be drawn from it to the circle.
  • These two tangents will meet the circle at two distinct points of contact.
  • They originate from the same external point.

🎙️ Now, if your point is outside the circle, like a light source placed away from a sphere, you can draw two lines that just touch the sphere. Similarly, from an external point, you can always draw exactly two tangents to a circle. This is a very common scenario in problems.

Theorem 2: Lengths of Tangents from an External Point

  • The lengths of tangents drawn from an external point to a circle are equal.
  • If PA and PB are two tangents from point P to a circle with centre O, then PA = PB.
  • This theorem is also very frequently used in problems.

🎙️ This theorem is another cornerstone of circles. It states that if you draw two tangents from the same point outside a circle, the segments from that external point to the points of contact will always be of equal length. This creates symmetrical figures and is useful for calculations.

⚖️
Equal measure

Worked Example 2: Using Theorem 2

  • Question: In the given figure, if TP and TQ are the two tangents to a circle with centre O so that ∠POQ = 110°, then find ∠PTQ.
  • Solution:

🎙️ Here, we combine Theorem 1 with the properties of quadrilaterals. The figure formed by the two radii, the two tangents, and the external point is a quadrilateral. Knowing three angles allows us to find the fourth. This is a common type of question in exams.

1Identify quadrilateral OPTQ. Sum of angles in a quadrilateral is 360°.
2Apply Theorem 1: OP ⊥ TP and OQ ⊥ TQ. So ∠OPT = 90°, ∠OQT = 90°.
3Given ∠POQ = 110°.
4Sum of angles: ∠OPT + ∠POQ + ∠OQT + ∠PTQ = 360°.
5Substitute and solve: 90° + 110° + 90° + ∠PTQ = 360° → 290° + ∠PTQ = 360° → ∠PTQ = 70°.

Tangents from an External Point: Angle Properties

  • The line joining the centre to the external point bisects the angle between the two tangents.
  • It also bisects the angle between the two radii to the points of contact.
  • The two triangles formed (ΔOPA and ΔOPB) are congruent by RHS criterion (using Theorem 2).

🎙️ When two tangents are drawn from an external point, a beautiful symmetry emerges. The line connecting the centre to the external point acts as an angle bisector for both the angle formed by the tangents and the angle formed by the radii. This congruence is very useful for proving other properties.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Confusing secant with tangent.
  • Assuming a chord is a diameter.
  • Not applying Pythagoras Theorem correctly.
  • Incorrectly identifying the right angle in problems.

🎙️ Students often mix up secants and tangents, remember a secant cuts through two points, a tangent touches one. Also, don't assume every chord is a diameter; only the one passing through the centre is. Always double-check your application of Pythagoras Theorem and ensure the right angle is correctly identified at the point of contact between radius and tangent.

Recap: Key Concepts of Circles

  • A circle is a set of points equidistant from a centre.
  • Key terms: centre, radius, diameter, chord, arc, segment, sector.
  • Lines can be non-intersecting, secant (2 points), or tangent (1 point).
  • Theorem 1: Radius is perpendicular to tangent at point of contact.
  • Theorem 2: Tangents from an external point are equal in length.

Slide 2: What is a Circle? (A Quick Review)

  • A circle is a set of all points in a plane.
  • These points are equidistant from a fixed central point.
  • Think of drawing with a compass!
  • It's a fundamental shape in geometry.

🎙️ Before we jump into new ideas, let's quickly recall what a circle is. It's essentially a path traced by a point moving at a constant distance from another fixed point. This fixed point is the heart of the circle.

Slide 3: Parts of a Circle - Center & Radius

  • The 'Center' (O) is the fixed point inside the circle.
  • The 'Radius' (r) is the distance from the center to any point on the circle.
  • All radii of the same circle are equal in length.
  • It's like the spoke of a bicycle wheel.

🎙️ The two most important parts of a circle are its center and radius. The center is literally the middle point, and the radius defines the 'size' of the circle. Remember, every point on the circle is exactly one radius away from the center.

Slide 4: Parts of a Circle - Diameter & Chord

  • A 'Diameter' (d) is a chord passing through the center.
  • It's the longest chord in a circle (d = 2r).
  • A 'Chord' is any line segment connecting two points on the circle.
  • Think of a guitar string as a chord.

🎙️ Building on radius, we have diameter. A diameter is just two radii joined end-to-end, passing through the center. A chord is a more general term for any line segment connecting two points on the circle, and the diameter is a special type of chord.

Slide 5: Parts of a Circle - Arc & Sector

  • An 'Arc' is a continuous piece of a circle.
  • Imagine a slice of pizza – its crust is an arc.
  • A 'Sector' is the region enclosed by two radii and an arc.
  • It's like the entire slice of pizza!

🎙️ Circles aren't just about lines; they also have curved parts. An arc is a segment of the circle's boundary. When you combine an arc with two radii, you get a sector, which is an area within the circle.

Slide 7: Lines and a Circle – Three Possibilities

  • A line can interact with a circle in three ways.
  • It can not touch the circle at all.
  • It can cut through the circle at two points (Secant).
  • It can touch the circle at exactly one point (Tangent).

🎙️ Now, let's consider how a straight line can relate to a circle. There are three distinct ways a line can position itself relative to a circle. We will explore each of these in detail.

Slide 8: Case 1: Non-Intersecting Line

  • The line does not touch the circle at all.
  • There are no common points between the line and the circle.
  • The shortest distance from the center to the line is greater than the radius.
  • Imagine a wall far away from a round pond.

🎙️ The first case is straightforward: the line and the circle have no points in common. They simply exist in the same plane without ever meeting. The distance from the center of the circle to this line is always more than the radius.

Slide 9: Case 2: Secant to a Circle

  • A 'Secant' is a line that intersects the circle at two distinct points.
  • It cuts through the interior of the circle.
  • A chord is a segment of a secant.
  • Think of a knife slicing through a round cake.

🎙️ Next, we have a secant. This line passes right through the circle, creating two points of intersection. The segment of the secant that lies inside the circle is what we call a chord. So, a secant is an extended chord.

Slide 10: Case 3: Tangent to a Circle – Our Main Focus!

  • A 'Tangent' is a line that intersects the circle at exactly one point.
  • This single point is called the 'Point of Contact'.
  • The tangent just 'touches' the circle without cutting through.
  • Imagine a car tire touching the road at one point.

🎙️ And finally, the star of our chapter: the tangent! This line is special because it meets the circle at only one unique point, known as the point of contact. It grazes the circumference without entering the circle's interior. This is a crucial concept for Class 10.

Slide 11: Property 1: Tangent is Perpendicular to Radius

  • Theorem: The tangent at any point of a circle is perpendicular to the radius through the point of contact.
  • This means the angle between the tangent and the radius is always 90 degrees.
  • This is a fundamental property of tangents.
  • Represented by the symbol '⊥'.

🎙️ This is our first major theorem about tangents. It states a very important relationship: wherever a tangent touches a circle, the radius drawn to that exact point of contact will always form a perfect right angle with the tangent line. This property is used extensively in solving problems.

Slide 12: Visualizing Tangent Property 1

  • Draw a circle with center O.
  • Draw a tangent AB at point P on the circle.
  • Draw radius OP.
  • Angle OPB (or OPA) will be 90 degrees.

🎙️ Let's visualize this. Imagine our circle with center O. If we draw a line that just touches the circle at point P, and then draw a line from O to P (which is the radius), these two lines will always meet at a right angle. This forms a perfect 'L' shape at the point of contact.

Slide 13: Number of Tangents from a Point - Case A: Inside the Circle

  • If a point 'P' lies inside the circle.
  • No tangent can be drawn to the circle from such a point.
  • Any line passing through P will be a secant.
  • It will always cut the circle at two points.

🎙️ Now, let's explore how many tangents we can draw from different positions. If you pick a point *inside* the circle, try to draw a line that only touches the circle at one point. You'll find it's impossible. Any line from an internal point will always pass through the circle twice, making it a secant, not a tangent.

Slide 14: Number of Tangents from a Point - Case B: On the Circle

  • If a point 'P' lies on the circle itself.
  • Exactly 'one' tangent can be drawn through this point.
  • This tangent is unique for that specific point of contact.
  • It's like a single track touching the edge of a record.

🎙️ What if the point is *on* the circle? In this case, you can draw exactly one tangent. This tangent will pass through that point and touch the circle only there. Any other line through that point would either be a secant or not a line at all.

☝️
One tangent

Slide 15: Number of Tangents from a Point - Case C: Outside the Circle

  • If a point 'P' lies outside the circle.
  • Exactly 'two' tangents can be drawn from this external point.
  • These two tangents will have different points of contact.
  • Imagine two hands reaching out to touch a ball.

🎙️ And finally, if the point is *outside* the circle, you can draw exactly two tangents to the circle. These two lines will originate from the external point and touch the circle at two distinct points of contact. This is a very important case for problem-solving.

✌️✌️

Slide 16: Property 2: Lengths of Tangents from External Point are Equal

  • Theorem: The lengths of tangents drawn from an external point to a circle are equal.
  • If P is an external point, and PA and PB are tangents.
  • Then, the length of PA will be equal to the length of PB (PA = PB).
  • This property is often used in geometric proofs.

🎙️ Here's our second major theorem about tangents. When you draw two tangents from the same external point to a circle, the segments from the external point to their respective points of contact will always have the same length. This is a powerful tool in geometry.

Slide 17: Visualizing Tangent Property 2

  • Draw a circle with center O.
  • Take an external point P.
  • Draw tangents PA and PB to the circle.
  • Measure PA and PB – they will be equal!

🎙️ Let's visualize this. Imagine a point P outside our circle. From P, we draw two lines that just touch the circle at points A and B. If you were to measure the distance from P to A, and from P to B, you would find they are exactly the same. This can be proven using congruence of triangles.

Slide 18: Worked Example 1: Applying Perpendicularity

  • Problem: A tangent PQ at a point P of a circle of radius 5 cm meets a line through the center O at a point Q so that OQ = 12 cm. Find the length of PQ.
  • Solution: OP is radius (5 cm), PQ is tangent, so ∠OPQ = 90°.
  • Using Pythagoras theorem in ΔOPQ: OQ² = OP² + PQ².
  • 12² = 5² + PQ² => 144 = 25 + PQ² => PQ² = 119 => PQ = √119 cm.

🎙️ Let's put our first property into practice. This problem involves a tangent, a radius, and an external line, forming a right-angled triangle. By applying the Pythagorean theorem, which you're already familiar with, we can easily find the unknown length of the tangent. Remember, the right angle is always at the point of contact.

Slide 19: Worked Example 2: Applying Equal Tangent Lengths

  • Problem: From a point Q, the length of the tangent to a circle is 24 cm and the distance of Q from the center is 25 cm. Find the radius of the circle.
  • Solution: Let the point of contact be P. PQ = 24 cm, OQ = 25 cm.
  • ∠OPQ = 90° (Radius ⊥ Tangent).
  • Using Pythagoras theorem in ΔOPQ: OQ² = OP² + PQ².
  • 25² = OP² + 24² => 625 = OP² + 576 => OP² = 49 => OP = 7 cm (Radius).

🎙️ Here's another example, this time to find the radius. Notice how both tangent properties often lead us back to the powerful Pythagorean theorem. Always draw a diagram to visualize the problem; it makes it much clearer where the right angle is and which sides are involved.

Slide 24: Recap: Key Takeaways on Circles & Tangents

  • A tangent touches a circle at exactly one point (point of contact).
  • The radius is perpendicular to the tangent at the point of contact (∠90°).
  • Zero tangents from inside, one from on, two from outside a circle.
  • Lengths of tangents from an external point to a circle are equal.

🎙️ Let's quickly summarize what we've learned today. The concept of a tangent and its two main properties are essential. Remember the number of tangents from different points and how these properties help us solve geometric problems, often with Pythagoras.

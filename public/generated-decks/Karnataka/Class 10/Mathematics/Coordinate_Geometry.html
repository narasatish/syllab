The Cartesian Plane

  • Two perpendicular number lines: X-axis (horizontal) and Y-axis (vertical).
  • They intersect at the Origin (0,0).
  • Divides the plane into four quadrants.

🎙️ Imagine drawing two number lines, one flat and one standing tall, crossing at zero. This creates our Cartesian plane, named after René Descartes. The point where they cross is called the origin, our starting point.

Plotting Points (x, y)

  • A point is represented by an ordered pair (x, y).
  • x is the x-coordinate (abscissa), distance from Y-axis.
  • y is the y-coordinate (ordinate), distance from X-axis.

🎙️ Every point on the Cartesian plane has a unique address, like a house number and street name. The first number tells us how far right or left to go (x), and the second tells us how far up or down (y).

Deriving the Distance Formula

  • Consider two points P(x1, y1) and Q(x2, y2).
  • Draw a right-angled triangle with PQ as hypotenuse.
  • Apply Pythagoras Theorem: (Hypotenuse)² = (Base)² + (Height)².

🎙️ The beauty of coordinate geometry is how it connects to other math concepts. We can use the familiar Pythagoras theorem, which you learned in earlier classes, to find the distance between any two points.

The Distance Formula

  • Distance d between (x1, y1) and (x2, y2) is:
  • d = √[(x2 - x1)² + (y2 - y1)²]
  • Remember to square the differences and then add before taking the square root.

🎙️ This is the powerful formula we derived. It might look a bit complex, but it's just a direct application of Pythagoras. Practice using it, and it will become second nature.

📐
The Formula
9 / 25

Worked Example 1: Finding Distance

  • Find the distance between A(2, 3) and B(5, 7).
  • x1=2, y1=3, x2=5, y2=7
  • d = √[(5-2)² + (7-3)²] = √[3² + 4²] = √[9+16] = √25 = 5 units.

🎙️ Let's work through an example together. We substitute the coordinates into the formula, carefully calculate the squares, add them up, and finally take the square root. The distance is 5 units.

Collinearity of Points

  • Three or more points are collinear if they lie on the same straight line.
  • Using distance formula: If AB + BC = AC, then A, B, C are collinear.
  • This means the sum of two smaller distances equals the largest distance.

🎙️ Collinear points are like beads on a single thread. We can check if three points are collinear by calculating the distances between each pair. If the two shorter distances add up to the longest one, they are collinear.

Deriving the Section Formula

  • Consider points A(x1, y1) and B(x2, y2).
  • Point P(x, y) divides AB in ratio m1:m2.
  • Use similar triangles concept to find x and y coordinates.

🎙️ Similar to the distance formula, the section formula can also be derived using geometric principles. By drawing parallel lines and applying the properties of similar triangles, we can find the coordinates of point P.

The Section Formula

  • For a point P(x, y) dividing AB in ratio m1:m2:
  • x = (m1x2 + m2x1) / (m1 + m2)
  • y = (m1y2 + m2y1) / (m1 + m2)

🎙️ This formula helps us find the coordinates of the dividing point. Notice how the ratio 'm1' is multiplied by the coordinates of the *second* point (x2, y2) and 'm2' by the *first* point (x1, y1).

➗
Dividing Ratio

Worked Example 2: Using Section Formula

  • Find the coordinates of the point that divides the line segment joining A(1, 7) and B(4, -3) in the ratio 2:3 internally.
  • x = (2*4 + 3*1) / (2+3) = (8+3)/5 = 11/5
  • y = (2*(-3) + 3*7) / (2+3) = (-6+21)/5 = 15/5 = 3
  • The point is (11/5, 3).

🎙️ Let's apply the section formula. We carefully plug in the x and y coordinates and the ratio values into the respective formulas. Remember to perform the calculations step-by-step.

Midpoint Formula: A Special Case

  • The midpoint divides a line segment in the ratio 1:1.
  • Simply average the x-coordinates and y-coordinates.
  • Midpoint M(x, y) = [(x1+x2)/2, (y1+y2)/2]

🎙️ The midpoint is a very common special case of the section formula. When the ratio is 1:1, the formula simplifies greatly – you just find the average of the x-coordinates and the average of the y-coordinates.

  • How to find the area of a triangle when vertices are given as coordinates.
  • No need for base and height measurements.
  • Useful for complex shapes and proofs.

🎙️ You've learned to find the area of a triangle using base and height. But what if you only have the coordinates of its corners? Coordinate geometry provides a neat formula for this too.

The Area of a Triangle Formula

  • For a triangle with vertices (x1, y1), (x2, y2), (x3, y3):
  • Area = 1/2 |x1(y2 - y3) + x2(y3 - y1) + x3(y1 - y2)|
  • The absolute value ensures the area is always positive.

🎙️ This formula might look long, but it follows a pattern. Remember to keep the order of coordinates correct and be careful with signs. The absolute value at the end makes sure our area is positive, as area cannot be negative.

Worked Example 3: Area of a Triangle

  • Find the area of the triangle with vertices A(1, -1), B(-4, 6), C(-3, -5).
  • Area = 1/2 |1(6 - (-5)) + (-4)(-5 - (-1)) + (-3)(-1 - 6)|
  • = 1/2 |1(11) - 4(-4) - 3(-7)|
  • = 1/2 |11 + 16 + 21| = 1/2 |48| = 24 square units.

🎙️ Let's calculate an area. Substitute the values carefully into the formula. Pay close attention to the subtractions involving negative numbers. The final result is 24 square units.

Euclid's Division Lemma: A Powerful Tool

  • Given positive integers 'a' and 'b'
  • There exist unique integers 'q' and 'r'
  • Such that a = bq + r, where 0 ≤ r < b

🎙️ Now, let's delve into a fundamental concept: Euclid's Division Lemma. This lemma provides a formal way to understand the division process. It states that for any two positive integers, you can always find a unique quotient and remainder.

Breaking Down the Lemma: a = bq + r

  • 'a' is the Dividend
  • 'b' is the Divisor
  • 'q' is the Quotient
  • 'r' is the Remainder

🎙️ In the equation a = bq + r, 'a' is the number being divided, 'b' is the number dividing it, 'q' is how many times 'b' goes into 'a', and 'r' is what's left over. Remember, the remainder 'r' must always be less than the divisor 'b'.

Sharing Sweets: A Lemma Example

  • You have 10 sweets (a=10)
  • Share among 3 friends (b=3)
  • Each friend gets 3 sweets (q=3)
  • 1 sweet is left over (r=1)

🎙️ Let's use a simple example. If you have 10 sweets and want to share them equally among 3 friends, each friend gets 3 sweets. You'll have 1 sweet remaining. Here, a=10, b=3, q=3, and r=1, satisfying 10 = 3*3 + 1.

Euclid's Division Algorithm: Finding HCF

  • A technique to compute the HCF (Highest Common Factor)
  • Repeatedly apply Euclid's Division Lemma
  • The HCF is the divisor when the remainder becomes zero

🎙️ Euclid's Division Algorithm is a practical application of the lemma. It's an efficient method to find the HCF of two positive integers. You keep applying the division lemma until the remainder becomes zero, and the last non-zero divisor is your HCF.

1Apply Lemma a=bq+r
2If r=0, b is HCF
3If r≠0, set a=b, b=r
4Repeat until r=0

The Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic: Building Blocks

  • Every composite number can be factored
  • Unique prime factorization
  • Order of prime factors does not matter

🎙️ This theorem is a cornerstone of number theory. It states that any composite number can be uniquely expressed as a product of prime numbers, regardless of the order in which you write the prime factors. Think of prime numbers as the 'atoms' of all numbers.

Prime Factorization: Breaking Down Numbers

  • Example: Factorize 60
  • 60 = 2 × 30
  • 30 = 2 × 15
  • 15 = 3 × 5

🎙️ Let's see how this works with an example. To factorize 60, we break it down into its prime factors. The unique prime factorization of 60 is 2 × 2 × 3 × 5, or 2² × 3 × 5.

HCF and LCM Using Prime Factorization

  • HCF = product of smallest powers of common prime factors
  • LCM = product of greatest powers of all prime factors
  • For any two positive integers a, b: HCF(a,b) × LCM(a,b) = a × b

🎙️ The Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic is very useful for finding HCF and LCM. For example, to find HCF and LCM of 6 (2×3) and 20 (2²×5), HCF is 2¹ = 2, and LCM is 2²×3×5 = 60. Notice that 2 × 60 = 6 × 20, which is 120.

Euclid's Division Lemma (EDL)

  • Given positive integers 'a' and 'b', there exist unique integers 'q' and 'r'.
  • Such that a = bq + r, where 0 ≤ r < b.
  • 'a' is dividend, 'b' is divisor, 'q' is quotient, 'r' is remainder.

🎙️ EDL is a statement that has already been proven. It simply formalizes our everyday division process. The remainder 'r' is always less than the divisor 'b'.

The Division Rule

EDL Example: Dividing 15 by 4

  • Dividend (a) = 15
  • Divisor (b) = 4
  • 15 = 4 × 3 + 3
  • Quotient (q) = 3, Remainder (r) = 3

🎙️ Here, we can clearly see how 15 is expressed in the form bq + r. The remainder 3 is indeed less than the divisor 4, satisfying the condition.

Euclid's Division Algorithm (EDA): Finding HCF

  • A technique to compute the Highest Common Factor (HCF) of two positive integers.
  • Based on Euclid's Division Lemma.
  • Repeatedly apply EDL until the remainder is zero.

🎙️ EDA is not just a lemma; it's an algorithm, a step-by-step procedure. It's an efficient way to find the HCF, which is the largest number that divides both given numbers exactly.

📏
Common Measure
11 / 30

Steps for Euclid's Division Algorithm

  • Step 1: Apply EDL to c and d (c > d) to find q and r.
  • Step 2: If r = 0, then d is the HCF.
  • Step 3: If r ≠ 0, apply EDL to d and r. Continue the process.
  • The divisor at which the remainder becomes zero is the HCF.

🎙️ Remember, the process continues by making the previous divisor the new dividend, and the remainder the new divisor. This iterative process guarantees we find the HCF.

1a = bq + r
2If r=0, HCF=b
3Else, b=rq'+r'
4Repeat until r=0
12 / 30

EDA Worked Example: HCF of 135 and 225

  • Step 1: 225 = 135 × 1 + 90 (r ≠ 0)
  • Step 2: 135 = 90 × 1 + 45 (r ≠ 0)
  • Step 3: 90 = 45 × 2 + 0 (r = 0)
  • The HCF is 45.

🎙️ As you can see, we continued the process until the remainder became zero. The divisor at that final step, which is 45, is our HCF.

1225 = 135(1) + 90
2135 = 90(1) + 45
390 = 45(2) + 0
4HCF = 45

Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic: Building Blocks

  • Think of numbers as houses built from LEGO blocks.
  • The prime numbers are the basic, indivisible LEGO blocks.
  • Every house (composite number) can be built in only one way from these blocks.

🎙️ This analogy helps visualize the core idea. Prime numbers are the fundamental components, and every other number is uniquely constructed from them through multiplication.

Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic (FToA)

  • Every composite number can be expressed (factorised) as a product of primes.
  • This factorization is unique, apart from the order of its prime factors.
  • Example: 12 = 2 × 2 × 3, or 2² × 3.

🎙️ This theorem is incredibly important. It guarantees that no matter how you factorize a composite number, you will always end up with the same set of prime factors, only their order might change.

🔢
Unique Factors

FToA: Prime Factorization Example - 140

  • Divide 140 by the smallest prime, 2: 140 ÷ 2 = 70.
  • Divide 70 by 2: 70 ÷ 2 = 35.
  • Divide 35 by the next prime, 5: 35 ÷ 5 = 7.
  • Divide 7 by 7: 7 ÷ 7 = 1.
  • So, 140 = 2 × 2 × 5 × 7 = 2² × 5 × 7.

🎙️ This step-by-step method, often called the factor tree method or repeated division, helps us find all the prime factors. Always start with the smallest prime and move upwards.

1140 ÷ 2 = 70

HCF & LCM using Prime Factorization

  • HCF: Product of the smallest power of each common prime factor.
  • LCM: Product of the greatest power of each prime factor (common or not).
  • This method is often simpler for larger numbers.

🎙️ Understanding the difference between HCF and LCM here is crucial. HCF takes only common factors with their lowest power, while LCM takes all factors with their highest power.

HCF (common, min power)
LCM (all, max power)
18 / 30

Worked Example: HCF & LCM of 6 and 20

  • Prime factors of 6: 2 × 3
  • Prime factors of 20: 2 × 2 × 5 = 2² × 5
  • HCF(6, 20): Common prime is 2, smallest power is 2¹. So, HCF = 2.
  • LCM(6, 20): All primes are 2, 3, 5. Greatest powers are 2², 3¹, 5¹. So, LCM = 2² × 3 × 5 = 4 × 3 × 5 = 60.

🎙️ Notice how for HCF, we only look at the '2' which is common to both. For LCM, we consider all unique prime factors (2, 3, 5) and take their highest powers.

16 = 2 × 3
220 = 2² × 5
3HCF = 2
4LCM = 60

Relationship between HCF, LCM and Numbers

  • For any two positive integers 'a' and 'b':
  • HCF(a, b) × LCM(a, b) = a × b
  • This relation is very useful for verifying answers or finding one value if others are known.

🎙️ This is an important formula to remember. Let's check with our previous example: HCF(6, 20) = 2, LCM(6, 20) = 60. Product of numbers = 6 × 20 = 120. HCF × LCM = 2 × 60 = 120. It matches!

Revisiting Irrational Numbers: The Unfit

  • Numbers that cannot be expressed in the form p/q, where p and q are integers and q ≠ 0.
  • Their decimal expansions are non-terminating and non-repeating.
  • Examples: √2, √3, π (pi).

🎙️ Think of irrational numbers as numbers that don't 'fit' neatly into a fraction. They have endless, random-looking decimal parts, unlike rational numbers which either stop or repeat.

Proof of Irrationality: Why √2 is Irrational

  • We use a method called 'proof by contradiction'.
  • Assume √2 is rational (can be written as a/b in simplest form).
  • Show that this assumption leads to a contradiction, proving our initial assumption wrong.

🎙️ This is a classic proof. It's like saying, 'Let's pretend this is true, and see if it breaks logic.' When it breaks logic, we know our initial 'pretend' was false.

√

Proof of Irrationality: Steps for √2

  • 1. Assume √2 = a/b, where a, b are coprime integers.
  • 2. Square both sides: 2 = a²/b² => a² = 2b².
  • 3. This means a² is even, so 'a' must also be even (let a = 2k).
  • 4. Substitute a=2k: (2k)² = 2b² => 4k² = 2b² => b² = 2k².
  • 5. This means b² is even, so 'b' must also be even.
  • 6. Contradiction! If 'a' and 'b' are both even, they are not coprime. Thus, √2 is irrational.

🎙️ The key is that if both 'a' and 'b' are even, they share a common factor of 2, which contradicts our initial assumption that they were coprime. This means our starting assumption (√2 is rational) must be false.

1Assume √2 = a/b
2a² = 2b² (a is even)
3b² = 2k² (b is even)
4Contradiction (a,b not coprime)

Rational Numbers & Decimal Expansions: Packaging Sweets

  • Imagine sweets packaged in boxes.
  • If you can perfectly fill boxes (denominator is 2s or 5s), it's a terminating decimal.
  • If you always have some left over (other prime factors), it's non-terminating repeating.

🎙️ This analogy helps connect the prime factors of the denominator to the behavior of the decimal expansion. Only powers of 2 and 5 allow for a 'perfect' division in base 10.

Terminating Decimal Expansions

  • A rational number p/q (where HCF(p,q)=1) has a terminating decimal expansion.
  • IF the prime factorization of the denominator 'q' is of the form 2ⁿ5ᵐ, where n and m are non-negative integers.
  • Example: 3/8 = 3/(2³) = 0.375.

🎙️ When the denominator only has 2s and 5s as prime factors, we can always multiply it by some power of 2 or 5 to make it a power of 10, resulting in a decimal that stops.

Non-Terminating Repeating Decimal Expansions

  • A rational number p/q (where HCF(p,q)=1) has a non-terminating repeating decimal expansion.
  • IF the prime factorization of the denominator 'q' has prime factors other than 2 or 5.
  • Example: 1/3 = 0.333... (repeating), 1/7 = 0.142857...

🎙️ If the denominator contains prime factors other than 2 or 5, you can never make it a perfect power of 10. This leads to the digits in the decimal expansion repeating in a pattern.

Worked Example: Classifying Decimals

  • 3/8: Denominator 8 = 2³. (Form 2ⁿ5ᵐ). Hence, terminating.
  • 1/7: Denominator 7. (Has prime factor 7, not 2 or 5). Hence, non-terminating repeating.
  • 13/125: Denominator 125 = 5³. (Form 2ⁿ5ᵐ). Hence, terminating.

🎙️ Always simplify the fraction first, then check the prime factors of the denominator. This is the quickest way to determine the nature of its decimal expansion.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Not simplifying fractions before checking decimal expansions.
  • Errors in prime factorization when finding HCF/LCM.
  • Forgetting the condition 0 ≤ r < b in EDL.
  • Assuming √3 is rational without proper proof by contradiction.

🎙️ These are typical pitfalls. Always double-check your calculations, especially prime factorizations. And remember the core conditions for each theorem and lemma.

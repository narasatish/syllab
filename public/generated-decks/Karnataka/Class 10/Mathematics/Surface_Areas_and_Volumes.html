Recap: 2D vs. 3D Shapes

  • 2D: Flat shapes, have length and breadth (e.g., square, circle).
  • 3D: Solid shapes, have length, breadth, and height (e.g., cube, cylinder).
  • 2D shapes have Area and Perimeter.
  • 3D shapes have Surface Area and Volume.

🎙️ Before diving into 3D, let's quickly recall the difference. 2D shapes are flat, like a drawing on paper. 3D shapes, on the other hand, are solid objects that you can hold and feel, occupying space.

Basic 2D Shapes: A Quick Look

  • Square: Area = side², Perimeter = 4 × side.
  • Rectangle: Area = l × b, Perimeter = 2(l + b).
  • Circle: Area = πr², Circumference = 2πr.
  • Triangle: Area = ½ × base × height.

🎙️ We've learned about these fundamental 2D shapes in previous classes. Their areas tell us the space they cover on a flat surface, and their perimeters tell us the length of their boundary.

What is Surface Area?

  • The total area of all the surfaces (faces) of a 3D object.
  • Measured in square units (e.g., cm², m²).
  • Can be Total Surface Area (TSA) or Lateral/Curved Surface Area (LSA/CSA).

🎙️ Imagine you want to paint a box. The amount of paint you need would depend on the total surface area of that box. It's essentially the 'skin' or outer covering of a 3D shape.

What is Volume?

  • The amount of space a 3D object occupies.
  • Also, the capacity of a container.
  • Measured in cubic units (e.g., cm³, m³, litres).

🎙️ Now, if you want to fill that same box with water or sand, the amount it can hold is its volume. Volume tells us how much 'stuff' can fit inside a 3D object or how much space the object itself takes up.

The Cube

  • A 3D shape with 6 identical square faces.
  • All edges are of equal length (let's call it 'a').
  • Total Surface Area (TSA) = 6a².
  • Lateral Surface Area (LSA) = 4a² (area of 4 side faces).
  • Volume = a³.

🎙️ The cube is one of the simplest 3D shapes, like a dice or a Rubik's cube. Since all its faces are identical squares, its surface area and volume formulas are quite straightforward.

Cube: Real-Life Example

  • A gift box is shaped like a cube.
  • Its side length is 10 cm.
  • Find the total surface area of the box.
  • Find the volume of the box.

🎙️ Imagine wrapping this gift box. You would need to cover all 6 faces. Using the formula TSA = 6a², we get 6 × (10 cm)² = 600 cm². If you fill it with chocolates, its volume would be a³ = (10 cm)³ = 1000 cm³.

The Cuboid

  • A 3D shape with 6 rectangular faces.
  • Has length (l), breadth (b), and height (h).
  • Total Surface Area (TSA) = 2(lb + bh + hl).
  • Lateral Surface Area (LSA) = 2h(l + b) (area of 4 side walls).
  • Volume = l × b × h.

🎙️ A cuboid is like an elongated cube, where the sides can have different lengths. Think of a shoebox or a classroom. Its formulas account for the different dimensions of its rectangular faces.

🧱

Cuboid: Real-Life Example

  • A book has length 25 cm, breadth 20 cm, and height 4 cm.
  • Find the total surface area of the book.
  • Find the volume of the book.

🎙️ Let's calculate for this book. TSA = 2(25×20 + 20×4 + 4×25) = 2(500 + 80 + 100) = 2(680) = 1360 cm². The volume, representing the space it occupies, is 25 × 20 × 4 = 2000 cm³.

The Cylinder

  • A 3D shape with two circular bases and a curved surface.
  • Has radius (r) and height (h).
  • Curved Surface Area (CSA) = 2πrh.
  • Total Surface Area (TSA) = 2πr(h + r) (CSA + area of two bases).
  • Volume = πr²h (Area of base × height).

🎙️ Cylinders are everywhere – from water bottles to storage tanks. They have a curved surface and two flat circular ends. Understanding its curved and total surface areas is key for applications like painting pipes or designing containers.

Cylinder: Real-Life Example

  • A cylindrical water bottle has a radius of 3.5 cm and height of 20 cm.
  • Find its curved surface area.
  • Find the volume of water it can hold.

🎙️ For this bottle, the CSA = 2 × (22/7) × 3.5 × 20 = 440 cm². This is the area of the label. The volume it can hold is πr²h = (22/7) × (3.5)² × 20 = 770 cm³. This tells us its capacity.

The Cone

  • A 3D shape with a circular base and a curved surface tapering to a point (vertex).
  • Has radius (r), height (h), and slant height (l).
  • Slant height (l) = √(r² + h²).
  • Curved Surface Area (CSA) = πrl.
  • Total Surface Area (TSA) = πr(l + r) (CSA + area of base).
  • Volume = (1/3)πr²h.

🎙️ Cones are familiar from ice cream cones or party hats. Notice the introduction of 'slant height', which is important for its curved surface area. The volume is one-third that of a cylinder with the same base and height.

Cone: Real-Life Example

  • A conical tent has a base radius of 7 m and a height of 24 m.
  • Find the slant height of the tent.
  • Find the area of canvas needed for the tent (CSA).

🎙️ First, calculate the slant height: l = √(7² + 24²) = √(49 + 576) = √625 = 25 m. Now, the canvas needed is the CSA = πrl = (22/7) × 7 × 25 = 550 m². This is practical for tent manufacturers.

The Sphere

  • A perfectly round 3D object, where every point on its surface is equidistant from its center.
  • Has only a radius (r).
  • Surface Area (SA) = 4πr².
  • Volume = (4/3)πr³.

🎙️ Think of a football or a globe. A sphere has no flat faces, only a single curved surface. It's defined purely by its radius, making its formulas quite elegant.

⚽

Sphere: Real-Life Example

  • A spherical cricket ball has a radius of 3.5 cm.
  • Find the surface area of the ball.
  • Find the volume of the material used to make the ball.

🎙️ The surface area of the ball is SA = 4πr² = 4 × (22/7) × (3.5)² = 154 cm². The volume, which represents the material, is (4/3)πr³ = (4/3) × (22/7) × (3.5)³ = 179.67 cm³ (approx).

The Hemisphere

  • Exactly half of a sphere.
  • Has a curved surface and a flat circular base.
  • Has only a radius (r).
  • Curved Surface Area (CSA) = 2πr².
  • Total Surface Area (TSA) = 3πr² (CSA + area of base).
  • Volume = (2/3)πr³.

🎙️ A hemisphere is like a bowl or half an orange. Its curved surface area is half that of a full sphere. However, its total surface area includes the flat circular base, making it 3πr².

Hemisphere: Real-Life Example

  • A hemispherical bowl has a radius of 7 cm.
  • Find the curved surface area of the bowl.
  • Find the total surface area of the bowl.
  • Find the volume of liquid it can hold.

🎙️ For this bowl, CSA = 2πr² = 2 × (22/7) × 7² = 308 cm². TSA = 3πr² = 3 × (22/7) × 7² = 462 cm². The volume of liquid it holds is (2/3)πr³ = (2/3) × (22/7) × 7³ = 718.67 cm³ (approx).

Combination of Solids

  • Many real-world objects are combinations of basic 3D shapes.
  • Examples: a toy (cone on a hemisphere), a capsule (cylinder with hemispheres at ends).
  • To find surface area: sum the *exposed* surface areas of individual parts.
  • To find volume: sum the volumes of individual parts.

🎙️ This is where it gets interesting! We rarely see perfectly isolated shapes. When shapes are combined, we need to be careful with surface area – only count the parts that are on the *outside*. Volume is simpler; you just add them up.

Combined Solids: Worked Example

  • A toy is in the form of a cone mounted on a hemisphere.
  • The radius of the hemisphere and cone is 3.5 cm.
  • The total height of the toy is 15.5 cm.
  • Find the total surface area of the toy.

🎙️ Here, the cone's height is 15.5 - 3.5 = 12 cm. First, find the cone's slant height, l = √(3.5² + 12²) = 12.5 cm. Total Surface Area = CSA of cone + CSA of hemisphere = πrl + 2πr² = (22/7)×3.5×12.5 + 2×(22/7)×(3.5)² = 137.5 + 77 = 214.5 cm².

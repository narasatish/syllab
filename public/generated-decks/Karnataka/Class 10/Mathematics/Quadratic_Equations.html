What are Polynomials?

  • An expression with variables, constants, and exponents.
  • Exponents must be whole numbers (0, 1, 2, ...).

🎙️ Before we dive into quadratic equations, let's quickly recall what polynomials are. Think of them as mathematical expressions built using variables like 'x', constants like '5' or '3', and operations like addition, subtraction, and multiplication. The key rule is that the powers of the variables must always be whole numbers.

Degree of a Polynomial

  • The highest power of the variable in the polynomial.
  • Determines the type of polynomial.
  • Examples: 2x+5 (degree 1), 3x^2-7x+1 (degree 2).

🎙️ The 'degree' of a polynomial is simply the highest power or exponent of the variable present in the expression. For example, in '2x + 5', the highest power of 'x' is 1, so its degree is 1. In '3x^2 - 7x + 1', the highest power of 'x' is 2, making its degree 2. This degree helps us classify polynomials.

What is a Quadratic Equation?

  • An equation where the highest power of the variable is 2.
  • Looks like: ax^2 + bx + c = 0.
  • Here, 'a' cannot be zero (a ≠ 0).

🎙️ So, what exactly is a quadratic equation? It's an equation where the highest power of the variable, usually 'x', is exactly 2. This means you'll see an 'x-squared' term in it. It's always set equal to zero, and importantly, the coefficient of the x-squared term, 'a', must not be zero, otherwise it wouldn't be quadratic anymore!

Standard Form

  • ax^2 + bx + c = 0
  • Where a, b, c are real numbers.
  • And 'a' must not be zero (a ≠ 0).

🎙️ Every quadratic equation can be written in a specific 'standard form'. This form helps us identify and work with these equations systematically. It's 'ax-squared plus bx plus c equals zero'. Remember, 'a', 'b', and 'c' are just numbers, and 'a' cannot be zero. If 'a' were zero, the x-squared term would vanish, and it would become a linear equation.

Is it a Quadratic Equation?

  • Check if the highest power of the variable is 2.
  • Check if it can be rearranged into standard form.
  • Example: (x+1)^2 = 2x - 3

🎙️ Now, let's practice identifying quadratic equations. The key is to look for the highest power of the variable. If it's 2, and the 'a' coefficient isn't zero, then it's a quadratic. Sometimes, an equation might not look like a quadratic at first glance, but after simplifying, it transforms into the standard form. Always expand and simplify to be sure!

Real-Life Example: Area

  • A rectangular garden has length (x+5) meters and width x meters.
  • Its area is 150 square meters.
  • Formulate the equation.

🎙️ Quadratic equations are not just abstract math; they help us solve everyday problems! Imagine you're designing a rectangular garden. If its length is 5 meters more than its width, and the total area you need is 150 square meters, how would you find the dimensions? By letting the width be 'x', the length becomes 'x+5'. Area is length times width, so x(x+5) = 150, which simplifies to x^2 + 5x - 150 = 0. This is a quadratic equation!

Roots of a Quadratic Equation

  • The values of the variable that satisfy the equation.
  • Also called 'solutions' or 'zeros'.
  • A quadratic equation has at most two roots.

🎙️ What are the 'roots' of a quadratic equation? These are the special values of 'x' that, when substituted into the equation, make the entire equation true, meaning the left side equals zero. Think of them as the answers or solutions to the equation. Because the highest power is 2, a quadratic equation can have at most two distinct roots.

How to Solve Quadratic Equations?

  • 1. Factorization Method
  • 2. Completing the Square Method
  • 3. Quadratic Formula Method

🎙️ Great! We know what quadratic equations are and what their roots mean. Now, how do we find these roots? There are three main methods we'll learn: the factorization method, the completing the square method, and the quadratic formula. Each method has its own strengths, and mastering them will make you a quadratic equation expert!

Method 1: Factorization

  • Split the middle term (bx) into two terms.
  • Find two numbers whose product is 'ac' and sum is 'b'.
  • Group terms and factor out common factors.

🎙️ The factorization method involves breaking down the quadratic expression into a product of two linear factors. The most common technique is 'splitting the middle term'. We find two numbers that multiply to 'ac' and add up to 'b'. Then, we rewrite the equation, group terms, and factor out common factors to find the roots.

2Group and factor

11 / 25

Example: Factorization

  • Solve: x^2 - 5x + 6 = 0
  • Product = 6, Sum = -5. Numbers are -2, -3.
  • x^2 - 2x - 3x + 6 = 0

🎙️ Let's work through an example: x^2 - 5x + 6 = 0. Here, a=1, b=-5, c=6. We need two numbers that multiply to (1*6)=6 and add to -5. These numbers are -2 and -3. So, we rewrite -5x as -2x - 3x. Then we group the terms: (x^2 - 2x) and (-3x + 6). Factor out common terms: x(x-2) - 3(x-2). This gives (x-2)(x-3)=0. Setting each factor to zero, we get x=2 or x=3.

Method 2: Completing the Square

  • Transform the equation into a perfect square trinomial.
  • Add a specific constant to both sides.
  • Use the identity: (x+k)^2 or (x-k)^2.

🎙️ The 'completing the square' method is a powerful technique that transforms a quadratic equation into a form where we can easily take the square root. The goal is to manipulate the x-squared and x terms to create a perfect square trinomial, like (x+k)^2 or (x-k)^2. This involves adding a specific constant to both sides of the equation.

Example: Completing the Square

  • Solve: x^2 + 6x + 5 = 0
  • Move constant: x^2 + 6x = -5
  • Add (6/2)^2 = 9 to both sides.

🎙️ Let's solve x^2 + 6x + 5 = 0 using this method. First, move the constant term to the right: x^2 + 6x = -5. Now, to complete the square, we take half of the coefficient of x (which is 6/2 = 3) and square it (3^2 = 9). Add 9 to both sides: x^2 + 6x + 9 = -5 + 9. This simplifies to (x+3)^2 = 4. Taking the square root of both sides, x+3 = ±2. So, x = -3+2 = -1 or x = -3-2 = -5.

Method 3: The Quadratic Formula

  • A direct formula to find the roots.
  • Always works for any quadratic equation.
  • x = [-b ± √(b^2 - 4ac)] / 2a

🎙️ The quadratic formula is a universal soldier for solving quadratic equations. It's derived from the completing the square method and provides the roots directly, no matter how complex the equation. You just need to identify the values of 'a', 'b', and 'c' from the standard form and plug them into this formula. It's a formula worth memorizing!

✨
Magic Formula
15 / 25

Example: Quadratic Formula

  • Solve: 2x^2 + 5x - 3 = 0
  • Identify a=2, b=5, c=-3.
  • Substitute into the formula.

🎙️ Let's apply the quadratic formula to 2x^2 + 5x - 3 = 0. Here, a=2, b=5, and c=-3. Substitute these values into the formula: x = [-5 ± √(5^2 - 4*2*(-3))] / (2*2). This simplifies to x = [-5 ± √(25 + 24)] / 4, which is x = [-5 ± √49] / 4. So, x = [-5 ± 7] / 4. This gives two roots: x = (-5+7)/4 = 2/4 = 1/2, and x = (-5-7)/4 = -12/4 = -3.

The Discriminant (D)

  • Part of the quadratic formula: D = b^2 - 4ac.
  • Determines the 'nature' of the roots.
  • Tells us if roots are real, equal, or not real.

🎙️ Inside the quadratic formula, there's a very important expression: b^2 - 4ac. This is called the 'discriminant', often denoted by 'D'. The discriminant doesn't give us the roots directly, but it tells us a lot about what kind of roots the equation will have – whether they are real, equal, or even real at all. It's like a judge for the roots!

⚖️
Judge of Roots
17 / 25

Nature of Roots: D > 0

  • If D > 0 (positive value).
  • The equation has two distinct real roots.
  • Example: x^2 - 5x + 6 = 0 (D = 1 > 0, roots 2, 3).

🎙️ When the discriminant (D) is greater than zero, meaning it's a positive number, the quadratic equation will have two distinct real roots. This means you'll get two different numerical values for 'x' that are real numbers. For instance, in x^2 - 5x + 6 = 0, D = (-5)^2 - 4(1)(6) = 25 - 24 = 1, which is greater than 0, and we found roots 2 and 3.

✅✅
Two Real Solutions
18 / 25

Nature of Roots: D = 0

  • If D = 0 (zero value).
  • The equation has two equal real roots.
  • Example: x^2 - 4x + 4 = 0 (D = 0, roots 2, 2).

🎙️ If the discriminant (D) turns out to be exactly zero, then the quadratic equation has two equal real roots. This means both solutions for 'x' will be the same real number. For example, in x^2 - 4x + 4 = 0, D = (-4)^2 - 4(1)(4) = 16 - 16 = 0. The roots are x = [-(-4) ± √0] / 2(1) = 4/2 = 2. So, both roots are 2.

One Repeated Solution
19 / 25

Nature of Roots: D < 0

  • If D < 0 (negative value).
  • The equation has no real roots.
  • The roots are complex (imaginary).

🎙️ Finally, if the discriminant (D) is less than zero, meaning it's a negative number, then the quadratic equation has no real roots. This means there are no real numbers that will satisfy the equation. The roots in this case are complex or imaginary numbers, which you will learn about in higher classes. For now, just know that there are no real solutions.

Real-Life Example: Projectile Motion

  • Height of a ball thrown upwards: h = -5t^2 + 20t + 1.
  • When does the ball hit the ground (h=0)?
  • This forms a quadratic equation.

🎙️ Quadratic equations are essential in physics! For example, when you throw a ball upwards, its height over time can be described by a quadratic equation. If the height 'h' is given by h = -5t^2 + 20t + 1, and you want to know when the ball hits the ground, you set h=0. This gives you -5t^2 + 20t + 1 = 0, a quadratic equation whose roots will tell you the time 't' when the ball is at ground level. Isn't that fascinating?

Summary & Key Takeaways

  • Quadratic equations have degree 2 (ax^2+bx+c=0).
  • Roots are solutions that satisfy the equation.
  • Solved by factorization, completing the square, or formula.

🎙️ Alright, let's quickly recap what we've learned today. Quadratic equations are powerful tools defined by their highest power of 2. We learned that their solutions are called roots, and there are at most two of them. We explored three robust methods to find these roots: factorization, completing the square, and the ever-reliable quadratic formula. We also understood how the discriminant helps us predict the nature of these roots.

What is a Quadratic Equation?

  • An equation with the highest power of the variable as 2.
  • It must contain an x² term.
  • No x³ or higher powers allowed.

🎙️ Formally, a quadratic equation is any equation where the highest power of the variable, usually 'x', is 2. It means you'll always see an x-squared term. If you see x-cubed or anything higher, it's not a quadratic equation.

The Standard Form: ax² + bx + c = 0

  • This is the general way to write a quadratic equation.
  • 'a', 'b', and 'c' are real numbers.
  • 'a' cannot be zero (otherwise it's linear!).

🎙️ Every quadratic equation can be written in this standard form: ax² + bx + c = 0. Here, 'a', 'b', and 'c' are constants, and 'a' is crucial—it must not be zero. If 'a' were zero, the x² term would vanish, and it would become a linear equation.

  • Example: 2x² + 5x - 3 = 0
  • Here, a = 2 (coefficient of x²)

Is it a Quadratic Equation? (Example 1)

  • Equation: (x + 1)² = 2(x - 3)
  • Expand: x² + 2x + 1 = 2x - 6
  • Rearrange: x² + 2x - 2x + 1 + 6 = 0
  • Simplify: x² + 7 = 0. Yes, it is!

🎙️ Sometimes, equations don't look like quadratic equations at first glance. You need to simplify them. For (x + 1)² = 2(x - 3), expand both sides and bring all terms to one side. After simplifying, we get x² + 7 = 0, which clearly has an x² term, so it is quadratic.

Is it a Quadratic Equation? (Example 2)

  • Equation: (x - 2)(x + 1) = (x - 1)(x + 3)
  • Expand LHS: x² - x - 2
  • Expand RHS: x² + 2x - 3

What are 'Roots' or 'Solutions'?

  • The values of 'x' that satisfy the equation.
  • When you substitute a root into the equation, it makes the equation true (LHS = RHS).
  • A quadratic equation can have at most two roots.

🎙️ The 'roots' or 'solutions' of a quadratic equation are the special values of 'x' that make the equation balance, meaning the left side equals the right side. Since it's a quadratic, it can have up to two distinct roots, or sometimes one repeated root.

Method 1: Factorisation – The 'Breaking Down' Method

  • Analogy: Breaking a big problem into smaller, manageable parts.
  • We express the quadratic polynomial as a product of two linear factors.
  • If (x-a)(x-b) = 0, then x-a=0 or x-b=0.

🎙️ Factorisation is like taking a complex puzzle and breaking it down into two simpler pieces. If we can write our quadratic equation as a product of two linear expressions, then finding the roots becomes much easier, as we just set each factor to zero.

Breaking into parts

Factorisation Steps

  • 1. Write equation in standard form: ax² + bx + c = 0.
  • 2. Find two numbers whose product is 'ac' and sum is 'b'.
  • 3. Split the middle term 'bx' using these two numbers.
  • 4. Factor by grouping the terms.
  • 5. Set each factor to zero to find the roots.

🎙️ These are the systematic steps for factorisation. The key is finding those two magic numbers that help us split the middle term, making it possible to group and factor the expression. Once factored, finding the roots is straightforward.

2Find Numbers

Factorisation Example: x² - 3x - 10 = 0

  • 1. Here, a=1, b=-3, c=-10.
  • 2. Product 'ac' = 1*(-10) = -10. Sum 'b' = -3.
  • 3. Numbers are -5 and 2 (-5*2 = -10, -5+2 = -3).
  • 4. Split: x² - 5x + 2x - 10 = 0
  • 5. Group: x(x - 5) + 2(x - 5) = 0
  • 6. Factor: (x - 5)(x + 2) = 0
  • 7. Roots: x - 5 = 0 => x = 5; x + 2 = 0 => x = -2

🎙️ Let's walk through an example. For x² - 3x - 10 = 0, we need two numbers that multiply to -10 and add to -3. These are -5 and 2. We then replace -3x with -5x + 2x, group the terms, factor out common terms, and finally set each bracket to zero to find our roots: 5 and -2.

Factorisation Example: 6x² + x - 2 = 0

  • 1. Here, a=6, b=1, c=-2.
  • 2. Product 'ac' = 6*(-2) = -12. Sum 'b' = 1.
  • 3. Numbers are 4 and -3 (4*(-3) = -12, 4+(-3) = 1).
  • 4. Split: 6x² + 4x - 3x - 2 = 0
  • 5. Group: 2x(3x + 2) - 1(3x + 2) = 0
  • 6. Factor: (3x + 2)(2x - 1) = 0
  • 7. Roots: 3x + 2 = 0 => x = -2/3; 2x - 1 = 0 => x = 1/2

🎙️ Here's a slightly more complex example. The process is the same: find two numbers that multiply to 'ac' (6 * -2 = -12) and add to 'b' (1). These are 4 and -3. Split the middle term, factor by grouping, and solve for x. The roots are -2/3 and 1/2.

Method 2: Completing the Square – Building a Perfect Garden

  • Analogy: Imagine you have a rectangular garden and want to make it a perfect square.
  • You add a small piece to make it square.
  • We transform the equation into a perfect square trinomial.

🎙️ Completing the square is a powerful algebraic technique. Think of it like adjusting a shape to make it 'perfect'. We manipulate the quadratic expression so that one side becomes a perfect square, which then allows us to easily take the square root and solve for x.

🏡

Completing the Square Steps

  • 1. Make the coefficient of x² equal to 1 (divide by 'a' if needed).
  • 2. Move the constant term 'c' to the RHS.
  • 3. Add (b/2)² to both sides of the equation.
  • 4. Factor the LHS as a perfect square: (x + b/2)². Simplify RHS.
  • 5. Take the square root of both sides (remember ±).
  • 6. Solve for 'x'.

🎙️ These steps guide us through completing the square. The crucial step is adding (b/2)² to both sides, which ensures the left-hand side becomes a perfect square trinomial. Remember to handle the square root carefully, considering both positive and negative possibilities.

1x² Coeff = 1
2Move Constant
3Add (b/2)²

5Square Root
6Solve for x

Completing the Square Example: 2x² - 5x + 3 = 0

  • 1. Divide by 2: x² - (5/2)x + 3/2 = 0
  • 2. Move constant: x² - (5/2)x = -3/2
  • 3. Add (b/2)² = (-5/4)² = 25/16 to both sides.
  • 4. x² - (5/2)x + 25/16 = -3/2 + 25/16
  • 5. (x - 5/4)² = -24/16 + 25/16 = 1/16
  • 6. x - 5/4 = ±√(1/16) = ±1/4
  • 7. x = 5/4 ± 1/4 => x = 6/4 = 3/2 or x = 4/4 = 1

🎙️ Let's apply the steps. First, ensure the x² coefficient is 1. Then, move the constant. Calculate (b/2)² and add it to both sides. The left side becomes (x - 5/4)². Take the square root, remembering the plus-minus sign, and solve for x. The roots are 3/2 and 1.

Method 3: Quadratic Formula – The Universal Key

  • Analogy: A master key that opens any quadratic lock.
  • Works for all quadratic equations, even when factorisation is hard.
  • Derived from the completing the square method.

🎙️ The quadratic formula is a universal method. It's like a master key because it can solve any quadratic equation, regardless of whether it's easily factorable or not. This formula is actually derived directly from the completing the square method, making it very powerful.

The Quadratic Formula

  • For ax² + bx + c = 0, the roots are given by:
  • x = [-b ± √(b² - 4ac)] / 2a
  • Memorize this formula! It's very important.

🎙️ This is the quadratic formula. It allows you to find the roots of any quadratic equation by simply substituting the values of 'a', 'b', and 'c'. Make sure you memorize this formula perfectly, as it will be your go-to tool for solving quadratic equations.

➗
The Equation Solver

Quadratic Formula Example: 2x² + 4x + 1 = 0

  • Here, a=2, b=4, c=1.
  • Substitute into formula: x = [-4 ± √(4² - 4*2*1)] / (2*2)
  • x = [-4 ± √(16 - 8)] / 4
  • x = [-4 ± √8] / 4
  • x = [-4 ± 2√2] / 4
  • x = -1 ± √2 / 2

🎙️ Let's use the formula. Identify 'a', 'b', and 'c'. Carefully substitute these values into the formula. Pay close attention to the signs and perform the calculations step-by-step, especially inside the square root. Simplify the final expression to get the two roots.

Quadratic Formula Example: x² - 7x + 12 = 0

  • Here, a=1, b=-7, c=12.
  • x = [-(-7) ± √((-7)² - 4*1*12)] / (2*1)
  • x = [7 ± √(49 - 48)] / 2
  • x = [7 ± √1] / 2
  • x = [7 ± 1] / 2
  • x = (7 + 1)/2 = 8/2 = 4 or x = (7 - 1)/2 = 6/2 = 3

🎙️ This example shows how the quadratic formula can solve equations that are also easily factorable. Notice that we get the same roots, 4 and 3, as we would by factorisation. This demonstrates the consistency and reliability of the formula.

Nature of Roots: What Type of Solutions?

  • We can predict the type of roots without actually solving the equation.
  • The 'discriminant' (D = b² - 4ac) tells us this.
  • It's the part under the square root in the quadratic formula.

🎙️ Before solving, we can get a sneak peek into the characteristics of the roots. The discriminant, D = b² - 4ac, is the key. Its value tells us if the roots are real and distinct, real and equal, or not real at all.

🤔
Predicting the outcome
26 / 31

The Discriminant (D = b² - 4ac)

  • 1. If D > 0: Two distinct real roots.
  • 2. If D = 0: Two equal real roots (or one repeated root).
  • 3. If D < 0: No real roots (roots are complex/imaginary).

🎙️ Here are the three crucial conditions for the discriminant. If D is positive, you'll get two different real numbers as roots. If D is zero, both roots will be the same real number. If D is negative, there are no real solutions, meaning the roots are complex numbers.

D > 0 (Distinct)
D = 0 (Equal)
D < 0 (No Real)

Nature of Roots Example: 3x² - 2x + 1/3 = 0

  • Here, a=3, b=-2, c=1/3.
  • Calculate D = b² - 4ac
  • D = (-2)² - 4 * 3 * (1/3)
  • D = 4 - 4
  • D = 0
  • Conclusion: Two equal real roots.

🎙️ Let's find the nature of roots for this equation. First, identify 'a', 'b', and 'c'. Then, substitute these values into the discriminant formula. Since D turns out to be 0, we know this equation has two equal real roots. This means the quadratic graph touches the x-axis at exactly one point.

Common Mistakes to Avoid!

  • Sign errors: Be careful with negative numbers in calculations.
  • Not simplifying: Always reduce fractions and radicals.
  • Incorrect 'a', 'b', 'c': Ensure equation is in standard form.
  • Forgetting ±: When taking square roots, remember both positive and negative.

🎙️ Many students lose marks due to these common errors. Always double-check your signs, especially when substituting into the quadratic formula. Make sure your equation is in standard form before identifying 'a', 'b', and 'c'. And never forget the ± when taking a square root!

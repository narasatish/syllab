Laws of Reflection

  • 1. The angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection.
  • 2. Incident ray, reflected ray, and normal lie in the same plane.

🎙️ These two laws are fundamental to understanding reflection. The first law means if light hits a mirror at a 30-degree angle, it will bounce off at exactly a 30-degree angle. The second law ensures that all these rays and the imaginary perpendicular line to the surface, called the normal, are all on the same flat surface.

Plane Mirrors: Our Everyday Reflection

  • The mirrors we use daily are plane mirrors.

Concave Mirrors: The Converging Mirror

  • Reflecting surface is curved inwards (like the inside of a spoon).
  • They converge parallel rays of light to a single point.
  • Used in torches, headlights, and shaving mirrors.

Key Terms for Spherical Mirrors (Part 1)

  • Pole (P): Centre of the mirror's reflecting surface.
  • Centre of Curvature (C): Centre of the sphere from which the mirror is cut.
  • Radius of Curvature (R): Distance from Pole to Centre of Curvature.

🎙️ To understand how spherical mirrors work, we need to know a few important terms. The Pole is the exact middle of the mirror. The Centre of Curvature is like the imaginary center of the huge sphere from which our small mirror piece was cut. The distance from the pole to this center is the radius of curvature.

Key Terms for Spherical Mirrors (Part 2)

  • Principal Axis: Straight line passing through P and C.
  • Principal Focus (F): Point where parallel rays converge (concave) or appear to diverge (convex).
  • Focal Length (f): Distance from Pole to Principal Focus (f = R/2).

🎙️ The Principal Axis is the imaginary line that helps us draw ray diagrams. The Principal Focus is a very special point – for a concave mirror, all parallel rays meet here. For a convex mirror, they seem to spread out from this point. The focal length is half the radius of curvature, a key relationship to remember.

Ray Rules for Concave Mirrors (1 & 2)

  • 1. A ray parallel to the principal axis passes through the principal focus (F) after reflection.
  • 2. A ray passing through the principal focus (F) becomes parallel to the principal axis after reflection.

🎙️ These rules are essential for drawing accurate ray diagrams and understanding image formation. Imagine light coming from a distant object. If it's parallel to the principal axis, it will always hit the mirror and then go through the focus. The reverse is also true – if it passes through the focus, it will reflect parallel.

Ray Rules for Concave Mirrors (3 & 4)

  • 3. A ray passing through the centre of curvature (C) is reflected back along the same path.
  • 4. A ray incident obliquely to the principal axis, towards the pole (P), is reflected obliquely, making equal angles with the principal axis.

🎙️ The ray through C is special because it hits the mirror normally, so it reflects right back. The ray to the pole is like reflection from a plane mirror at that point, following the law of reflection. Knowing these four rules allows us to predict where images will form for any object placed in front of a concave mirror.

Image Formation: Concave Mirror (Object at Infinity)

  • When an object is very far away (at infinity), rays are parallel.
  • Image is formed at the Principal Focus (F).
  • Image is real, inverted, and highly diminished (point-sized).

🎙️ Think of the sun's rays reaching us. They are practically parallel. When these parallel rays strike a concave mirror, they all converge at the principal focus. This is why if you hold a concave mirror towards the sun, you can get a tiny, bright spot of light at its focus – powerful enough to burn paper!

Image Formation: Concave Mirror (Object between C and F)

  • Object placed between the Centre of Curvature (C) and Focus (F).
  • Image is formed beyond C.
  • Image is real, inverted, and magnified (larger than object).

🎙️ This case shows the magnifying property of a concave mirror. When an object is placed between C and F, the reflected rays diverge to form a larger, real, and inverted image beyond C. This is how dentists use concave mirrors to see magnified images of teeth.

Image Formation by Convex Mirror

  • Object at Infinity: Image formed at F (behind mirror), virtual, erect, highly diminished.
  • Object between Infinity and Pole: Image formed between P and F (behind mirror), virtual, erect, diminished.

🎙️ A convex mirror simplifies things – it only has two main cases for image formation. Regardless of the object's position, the image is always formed behind the mirror, between the pole and the focus. It's always virtual, erect, and smaller than the object. This consistent behaviour makes them very predictable.

New Cartesian Sign Convention

  • All distances are measured from the Pole (P).
  • Distances measured in the direction of incident light are positive (+).
  • Distances measured opposite to incident light are negative (-).
  • Heights above principal axis are positive, below are negative.

🎙️ To perform calculations with mirrors, we use a standard set of rules called the New Cartesian Sign Convention. Imagine the pole as the origin (0,0) of a graph. Distances to the right of the pole are positive, and to the left are negative. Similarly, upwards is positive, and downwards is negative. This consistency is vital for solving numerical problems.

Mirror Formula and Magnification

  • Mirror Formula: 1/v + 1/u = 1/f
  • u = object distance, v = image distance, f = focal length.
  • Magnification (m) = h'/h = -v/u
  • h' = image height, h = object height.

🎙️ These two formulae are your best friends for solving numerical problems related to spherical mirrors. The mirror formula relates the object distance, image distance, and focal length. Magnification tells you how much larger or smaller the image is compared to the object, and whether it's inverted or erect.

Worked Example: Using the Mirror Formula

  • An object is placed 10 cm from a concave mirror of focal length 15 cm.
  • Using 1/v + 1/u = 1/f, find image distance (v).
  • u = -10 cm (object left of mirror), f = -15 cm (concave mirror).
  • 1/v + 1/(-10) = 1/(-15) => 1/v = 1/10 - 1/15 = (3-2)/30 = 1/30.
  • So, v = 30 cm. Image is virtual and formed behind the mirror.

🎙️ Let's apply the sign convention and the mirror formula. Since the mirror is concave, its focal length is negative. The object is placed to the left, so its distance is also negative. Plugging these values into the formula, we find a positive image distance, indicating the image is formed behind the mirror, making it virtual and erect.

Laws of Refraction

  • 1. Incident ray, refracted ray, and normal lie in the same plane.
  • 2. Snell's Law: sin i / sin r = constant (refractive index).
  • Refractive Index: Ratio of speed of light in vacuum to speed in medium.

🎙️ Similar to reflection, refraction also has two fundamental laws. The first law ensures all rays are in the same plane. The second, Snell's Law, is quantitative. It states that the ratio of the sine of the angle of incidence to the sine of the angle of refraction is a constant, known as the refractive index of the second medium with respect to the first. This index tells us how much light bends.

Lenses: Focusing and Spreading Light

  • Transparent materials that refract light to form images.
  • Made of glass or plastic, with one or two spherical surfaces.
  • Two main types: Convex (converging) and Concave (diverging).

🎙️ Lenses are like the 'eyes' of cameras, spectacles, and microscopes. They use refraction to bend light in specific ways. A convex lens is thicker in the middle and converges light, while a concave lens is thinner in the middle and diverges light. Understanding these differences is key to their applications.

Common Mistakes to Avoid!

  • Confusing concave and convex mirror/lens properties.
  • Incorrectly applying the New Cartesian Sign Convention.
  • Not drawing clear ray diagrams with arrows.
  • Mixing up mirror formula with lens formula.

🎙️ Students often make these common errors. Always remember: concave mirrors converge, convex mirrors diverge. For lenses, it's the opposite! Practice the sign convention diligently. And always draw your ray diagrams neatly with arrows to show the direction of light, it helps avoid confusion and earns marks.

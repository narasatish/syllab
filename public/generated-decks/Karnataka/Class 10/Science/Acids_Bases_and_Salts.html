What are Acids?

  • Substances that taste sour.
  • Turn blue litmus paper red.
  • Release hydrogen ions (H+) in water.

🎙️ Think about the taste of a lemon or tamarind – that sourness comes from acids. In chemistry, acids are defined by their ability to donate hydrogen ions when dissolved in water. This property is key to understanding their reactions.

Common Acids Around Us

  • Citric acid in oranges and lemons.
  • Lactic acid in curd (yogurt).
  • Acetic acid in vinegar.

🎙️ Acids are not just found in labs; they are present in many foods we eat daily. For example, citrus fruits like oranges and lemons contain citric acid, while the sourness in curd is due to lactic acid. Even vinegar, used in cooking, contains acetic acid.

Properties of Acids

  • Corrosive nature (can burn skin).
  • React with metals to produce hydrogen gas.
  • Conduct electricity in aqueous solutions.

🎙️ While some acids are mild, many concentrated acids are highly corrosive and can cause severe burns, so always handle them carefully. They also react with certain metals, producing hydrogen gas, which can be seen as bubbles. Their ability to conduct electricity makes them useful in various applications.

What are Bases?

  • Substances that taste bitter.
  • Feel soapy or slippery to touch.
  • Turn red litmus paper blue.

🎙️ Unlike acids, bases have a distinct bitter taste and a slippery feel, much like soap. Chemically, bases are substances that release hydroxide ions (OH-) when dissolved in water. This is the defining characteristic that differentiates them from acids.

🧼
Soapy feel

Common Bases Around Us

  • Sodium hydroxide (caustic soda) in soaps.
  • Magnesium hydroxide in antacids (milk of magnesia).
  • Calcium hydroxide (lime water) used in whitewashing.

🎙️ Just like acids, bases are also common in our daily lives. Soaps and detergents contain strong bases like sodium hydroxide. Antacids, which we take for indigestion, contain mild bases like magnesium hydroxide to neutralize excess stomach acid. Even lime water, used in construction, is a base.

🥛🥛🥛
Antacids contain bases

Properties of Bases

  • Can be corrosive (strong bases).
  • Do not react with most metals.
  • Conduct electricity in aqueous solutions.

🎙️ While bases have a slippery feel, strong bases can also be corrosive, so caution is needed. Unlike acids, bases generally do not react with metals to produce hydrogen gas. Similar to acids, their aqueous solutions are good conductors of electricity due to the presence of ions.

What are Indicators?

  • Substances that show if a solution is acidic or basic.
  • Change colour when added to acid or base.
  • Help us identify substances without tasting them.

🎙️ Since tasting or touching unknown substances can be dangerous, we use indicators. These are special chemicals that change their colour when they come into contact with an acid or a base. They are like traffic signals for chemists, telling us what kind of substance we are dealing with.

🌈
Colour changes

Natural Indicators

  • Litmus: extracted from lichens, most common.
  • Turmeric: yellow in acid, reddish-brown in base.
  • Red Cabbage: extract shows various colours.

🎙️ Nature provides us with many indicators. Litmus paper, derived from lichens, is perhaps the most well-known, turning red in acid and blue in base. Turmeric, a common spice, remains yellow in acidic solutions but turns reddish-brown in basic solutions. Even red cabbage juice can act as an indicator, displaying a range of colours depending on the pH.

Synthetic Indicators

🎙️ In addition to natural indicators, scientists have developed synthetic indicators for more precise measurements in laboratories. Phenolphthalein is colourless in acidic solutions but turns a vibrant pink in basic solutions. Methyl orange, another common synthetic indicator, shows a red colour in acidic mediums and a yellow colour in basic mediums.

Acid-Base Reactions: Neutralization

  • Acids and bases react to form salt and water.
  • This reaction is called neutralization.
  • The acidic and basic properties are cancelled out.

🎙️ One of the most fundamental reactions in chemistry is neutralization. When an acid and a base react, they essentially cancel out each other's properties, producing a neutral substance called salt and water. This reaction is exothermic, meaning it releases heat.

💥
Acid + Base = Neutral
13 / 25

Example: HCl + NaOH

  • Hydrochloric acid (HCl) is a strong acid.
  • Sodium hydroxide (NaOH) is a strong base.
  • They react to form Sodium Chloride (NaCl) and water (H₂O).

🎙️ Let's look at a classic example: the reaction between hydrochloric acid and sodium hydroxide. HCl is a strong acid, and NaOH is a strong base. When they react, they form sodium chloride, which is common table salt, and water. This is a perfect example of a neutralization reaction.

1HCl (Acid)
2+ NaOH (Base)
3→ NaCl (Salt)
4+ H₂O (Water)

🎙️ Neutralization is incredibly useful! When you have indigestion, you take an antacid, which contains a base, to neutralize the excess acid in your stomach. Farmers use lime (a base) to treat overly acidic soil, making it suitable for crops. Industrial waste, often acidic, is neutralized before being discharged to prevent environmental harm.

The pH Scale

  • A scale to measure the strength of acid or base.
  • Ranges from 0 to 14.
  • pH 7 is neutral; below 7 is acidic; above 7 is basic.

🎙️ The pH scale is a convenient way to express how acidic or basic a solution is. It ranges from 0 to 14. A pH of 7 indicates a neutral solution, like pure water. Solutions with a pH less than 7 are acidic, becoming stronger as the pH approaches 0. Solutions with a pH greater than 7 are basic, becoming stronger as the pH approaches 14.

pH Values of Common Substances

  • Stomach acid: pH ~1.2 (very acidic).
  • Lemon juice: pH ~2.5 (acidic).
  • Pure water: pH 7 (neutral).
  • Baking soda solution: pH ~8.4 (basic).

🎙️ Let's look at some everyday examples on the pH scale. Our stomach acid is very strong, with a pH of about 1.2. Lemon juice is also acidic, around pH 2.5. Pure water is perfectly neutral at pH 7. A solution of baking soda is mildly basic, with a pH of about 8.4. These examples help us understand the range of pH in our environment.

What are Salts?

  • Ionic compounds formed from the reaction of an acid and a base.
  • Consist of a positive ion (cation) and a negative ion (anion).
  • Many salts are neutral, but some can be acidic or basic.

🎙️ Salts are the products of neutralization reactions. They are ionic compounds made up of a positive ion from a base and a negative ion from an acid. While we often think of salts as neutral, depending on the strength of the parent acid and base, a salt solution can actually be acidic, basic, or neutral.

Common table salt

Families of Salts

  • Salts of strong acid + strong base (e.g., NaCl, neutral pH).
  • Salts of strong acid + weak base (e.g., NH₄Cl, acidic pH).
  • Salts of weak acid + strong base (e.g., CH₃COONa, basic pH).

🎙️ Salts can be categorized into different 'families' based on the strength of the acid and base from which they are formed. For example, sodium chloride comes from a strong acid and a strong base, resulting in a neutral solution. Ammonium chloride, from a strong acid and weak base, forms an acidic solution. Sodium acetate, from a weak acid and strong base, forms a basic solution.

  • Formed from hydrochloric acid and sodium hydroxide.
  • Essential for human diet and preserving food.

Beyond the kitchen, NaCl is a crucial raw material in the chemical industry, used to produce substances like sodium hydroxide, chlorine, and washing soda.

Chapter Recap: Acids, Bases and Salts

  • Acids are sour, turn blue litmus red, release H+.
  • Bases are bitter, soapy, turn red litmus blue, release OH-.
  • Indicators help identify acids/bases by colour change.
  • Neutralization forms salt and water (Acid + Base → Salt + Water).
  • The pH scale (0-14) measures acidity/basicity.
  • Salts are ionic compounds with diverse uses.

🎙️ Let's quickly review what we've learned. Acids are sour and release H+ ions, while bases are bitter, soapy, and release OH- ions. Indicators are our tools to distinguish between them safely. Remember the neutralization reaction where acids and bases combine to form salt and water. The pH scale tells us how acidic or basic a solution is, and finally, salts are crucial compounds with countless applications in our daily lives and industries.

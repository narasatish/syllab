What is Heredity?

  • Passing of traits from parents to offspring.
  • Explains why family members look similar.
  • Genes are the units of heredity.

🎙️ Heredity is the reason you might have your mother's eyes or your father's smile. It's the biological process that ensures characteristics are transferred across generations, making us unique yet connected to our ancestors.

Understanding Variation

  • Differences among individuals of the same species.
  • Can be visible (e.g., height) or invisible (e.g., blood group).
  • Essential for evolution and adaptation.

🎙️ Look around your classroom – everyone is human, but no two individuals are exactly alike! These differences are called variations. They are crucial because they provide the raw material for evolution to act upon, allowing species to adapt to changing environments.

How Variations Accumulate

  • Variations arise during reproduction.
  • Sexual reproduction creates more variation than asexual.
  • Passed on through generations, leading to significant changes over time.

🎙️ Imagine a small difference in height in one generation. If this trait is beneficial, it might become more common in the next generation. Over many generations, these small differences can add up, leading to new features or even new species.

Gregor Mendel: Father of Genetics

  • Austrian monk who studied pea plants.
  • Discovered fundamental laws of inheritance.
  • His work was rediscovered decades later.

🎙️ Gregor Mendel's meticulous experiments with pea plants laid the foundation for modern genetics. He carefully tracked traits like height, seed colour, and flower position, revealing patterns that had eluded scientists for centuries.

Monohybrid Cross: Studying One Trait

  • Cross between two parents differing in one trait.
  • Example: Tall pea plant x Dwarf pea plant.
  • F1 generation shows only dominant trait.
  • F2 generation shows both dominant and recessive traits in 3:1 ratio.

🎙️ Mendel crossed a pure tall pea plant with a pure dwarf pea plant. Surprisingly, all offspring in the first generation (F1) were tall. But when he self-pollinated the F1 plants, the second generation (F2) showed both tall and dwarf plants in a predictable ratio.

Dominant vs. Recessive Traits

  • Dominant trait: Expressed even with one gene copy (e.g., Tallness).
  • Recessive trait: Expressed only with two gene copies (e.g., Dwarfness).
  • Represented by capital (dominant) and lowercase (recessive) letters.

🎙️ Think of dominant as the 'stronger' trait that always shows up if it's there. Recessive traits are 'hidden' when a dominant trait is present, only appearing when two recessive genes are inherited.

Genotype and Phenotype

  • Genotype: Genetic makeup of an organism (e.g., TT, Tt, tt).
  • Phenotype: Observable physical characteristics (e.g., Tall, Dwarf).
  • Genotype determines phenotype.

🎙️ Genotype is like the blueprint or instruction manual inside your cells, while phenotype is the actual building that results. Two individuals might have the same phenotype (e.g., both are tall), but different genotypes (one might be TT, the other Tt).

Dihybrid Cross: Two Traits at Once

  • Cross involving two different traits (e.g., seed shape AND seed colour).
  • Mendel found traits are inherited independently.
  • F2 generation ratio is 9:3:3:1 for two unlinked traits.

🎙️ Mendel also studied how two traits, like seed shape (round/wrinkled) and seed colour (yellow/green), were inherited together. He discovered that the inheritance of one trait doesn't influence the inheritance of another, a principle called independent assortment.

How Genes Control Traits

  • Genes are segments of DNA.
  • DNA contains instructions for making proteins.
  • Proteins determine characteristics and functions.

🎙️ At a molecular level, our genes are like recipes. Each gene provides instructions for building a specific protein. These proteins then carry out various functions in our body, ultimately determining our traits, from eye colour to how our cells work.

Determining Sex in Humans

  • Humans have 23 pairs of chromosomes.
  • 22 pairs are autosomes, 1 pair is sex chromosomes (X and Y).
  • Females: XX, Males: XY.
  • Father determines the sex of the child.

🎙️ In humans, the sex of a child is determined by the father. Females produce only X gametes, while males produce both X and Y gametes. If an X sperm fertilizes the egg, it's a girl (XX); if a Y sperm fertilizes the egg, it's a boy (XY).

What is Evolution?

  • Gradual change in inherited traits of a population over generations.
  • Leads to diversity of life on Earth.
  • Not about individuals changing, but populations.

🎙️ Evolution is a slow, continuous process where species change over vast periods. It's not about an individual transforming within its lifetime, but rather how the genetic makeup of entire populations shifts across many generations.

🌍
Changing Earth

Acquired vs. Inherited Traits

  • Acquired traits: Developed during an individual's lifetime (e.g., learned skills). Not passed on.
  • Inherited traits: Passed from parents to offspring (e.g., eye colour).
  • Only inherited traits are relevant for evolution.

🎙️ If you learn to play the guitar, that's an acquired trait – you won't pass that skill directly to your children. However, your natural hair colour is an inherited trait, passed down through your genes. Evolution only works on inherited traits.

Natural Selection: Survival of the Fittest

  • Process where organisms better adapted to their environment tend to survive and produce more offspring.
  • Proposed by Charles Darwin.
  • Example: Darker moths surviving better in polluted areas.

🎙️ Imagine a population of deer. Some might be faster, some might have better camouflage. In an environment with predators, the faster or better-camouflaged deer are more likely to survive, reproduce, and pass on their advantageous traits. This is natural selection.

Speciation: Formation of New Species

  • Process by which new species arise from existing ones.
  • Caused by geographical isolation, genetic drift, or natural selection.
  • Example: Different finch species on Galapagos Islands.

🎙️ When a population is separated, perhaps by a mountain range or an ocean, the isolated groups can evolve independently. Over long periods, they accumulate enough differences that they can no longer interbreed, becoming distinct species.

Genetic Drift: Random Changes

  • Random changes in the frequency of alleles (gene variations) in a population.
  • More significant in small populations.
  • Can lead to loss of genetic variation.

🎙️ Imagine a small group of beetles. If a few individuals with a rare colour trait are accidentally stepped on, that trait might disappear from the population by pure chance, not because it was disadvantageous. This random change is genetic drift.

Evolution and Classification

  • Evolutionary relationships are used to classify organisms.
  • More closely related organisms share more common ancestors.
  • Hierarchical classification reflects shared ancestry.

🎙️ The way we classify living things, from kingdoms down to species, reflects their evolutionary history. Organisms grouped together often share a more recent common ancestor, just like how you share more recent ancestors with your cousins than with a distant relative.

Evidence of Evolution: Homologous Organs

  • Organs with similar basic structure but different functions.
  • Indicate common ancestry.
  • Example: Forelimbs of humans, birds, whales.

🎙️ The fact that a human arm, a bird's wing, and a whale's flipper all share the same underlying bone structure strongly suggests they evolved from a common ancestor. They've adapted for different uses, but the basic blueprint is still there.

Evidence of Evolution: Analogous Organs

  • Organs with different basic structure but similar functions.
  • Do NOT indicate common ancestry.
  • Example: Wings of birds and wings of insects.

🎙️ While both a bird's wing and an insect's wing are used for flight, their internal structures are vastly different. This means they evolved independently to serve a similar purpose, not from a common flying ancestor. This is called convergent evolution.

Fossils: Records of Past Life

  • Preserved remains or traces of ancient organisms.
  • Show how life forms have changed over geological time.
  • Provide direct evidence of evolutionary pathways.

🎙️ Fossils are like snapshots from Earth's ancient past. By studying them, scientists can piece together the evolutionary history of species, observing the gradual changes in their forms over millions of years and understanding how new species emerged.

The Story of Human Evolution

  • Humans (Homo sapiens) evolved from primate ancestors.
  • Characterized by bipedalism, larger brains, use of tools.
  • No "missing link" but a branching tree of ancestors.

🎙️ Our own species has a fascinating evolutionary history. From early ape-like ancestors, various human-like species evolved, developing traits like walking upright and increasing brain size, eventually leading to modern humans.

Heredity and Evolution: Key Takeaways

  • Heredity explains trait inheritance.
  • Variation is the raw material for evolution.
  • Natural selection drives evolutionary change.
  • Evidence from fossils, homologous/analogous organs.

🎙️ We've covered a vast and fascinating area today! Remember that heredity explains how traits pass on, and evolution describes how populations change over time, driven by mechanisms like natural selection and genetic drift, supported by a wealth of evidence.

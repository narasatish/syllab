Types of Charges & Their Interactions

  • Two types: Positive (+) and Negative (-).
  • Like charges repel each other.
  • Unlike charges attract each other.

🎙️ Remember how magnets work? Positive and negative charges behave similarly. Two positive charges will push each other away, but a positive and a negative charge will pull towards each other. This interaction is key to electricity.

Measuring Electric Charge: The Coulomb (C)

  • The SI unit of electric charge is the Coulomb (C).
  • One electron has a charge of 1.6 x 10⁻¹⁹ C.
  • One Coulomb is roughly the charge of 6 x 10¹⁸ electrons!

🎙️ To measure charge, we use the unit called Coulomb, named after the French physicist Charles-Augustin de Coulomb. It's a very large unit, so individual electrons carry an incredibly tiny amount of charge.

Measuring Current: The Ampere (A)

  • The SI unit of electric current is the Ampere (A).
  • 1 Ampere = 1 Coulomb of charge flowing per second (1 C/s).
  • Named after André-Marie Ampère.

🎙️ We measure electric current in Amperes, often shortened to 'Amps'. If one Coulomb of charge passes a point in a circuit every second, we say the current is one Ampere. This tells us how much charge is moving.

📏
Unit of Current

Direction of Electric Current

  • Conventional current: Flow from positive to negative terminal.
  • Electron flow: Actual flow from negative to positive terminal.
  • For calculations, we usually use conventional current.

🎙️ This can be a bit tricky! Historically, current was thought to flow from positive to negative. We still use this 'conventional current' in circuit diagrams. However, we now know that it's the negatively charged electrons that actually move, from negative to positive.

🎙️ Charges don't just flow on their own; they need a 'push'. This push is called potential difference, or voltage. It's the energy supplied per unit charge to make them move, much like a pump gives energy to water to flow.

Measuring Potential Difference: The Volt (V)

  • The SI unit of potential difference is the Volt (V).
  • 1 Volt = 1 Joule of work done per Coulomb of charge (1 J/C).
  • Named after Alessandro Volta, inventor of the electric battery.

🎙️ The unit for potential difference is the Volt. If one Joule of energy is used to move one Coulomb of charge, then the potential difference is one Volt. Higher voltage means a stronger 'push' for the charges.

Ohm's Law: The Relationship!

  • States that current (I) is directly proportional to potential difference (V).
  • This is true for a given conductor at constant temperature.
  • The relationship is V = I × R.

🎙️ Ohm's Law is a cornerstone of electricity. It tells us that if you increase the 'push' (voltage), more 'flow' (current) will happen, provided the 'path' (resistance) stays the same. This law helps us understand how circuits work.

📐
Law of Resistance

Resistance: Opposing the Flow

  • It's the opposition offered by a conductor to the flow of current.
  • Think of friction in a pipe slowing down water.
  • The SI unit of resistance is the Ohm (Ω).

🎙️ Every material resists the flow of current to some extent. This property is called resistance. A conductor with high resistance will allow less current to flow for a given voltage, just like a narrow pipe restricts water flow.

What Affects Resistance?

  • Length of the conductor (longer = more R).
  • Area of cross-section (thinner = more R).
  • Nature of the material (e.g., copper vs. iron).
  • Temperature (higher temp often = more R).

🎙️ Imagine trying to walk through a long, narrow corridor versus a short, wide hallway. The long, narrow one offers more resistance. Similarly, the physical dimensions and material of a wire significantly impact its resistance.

Resistivity: A Material's Property

  • It's the inherent resistance of a material of unit length and unit area.
  • Represented by the Greek letter rho (ρ).
  • Unit is Ohm-meter (Ωm).

🎙️ While resistance depends on size and shape, resistivity is a fundamental property of the material itself. Copper has low resistivity, making it a good conductor, while rubber has very high resistivity, making it an insulator.

Resistors in Series: One Path for Current

  • Components are connected end-to-end.
  • Same current flows through each resistor.
  • Total resistance is the sum of individual resistances: R_total = R₁ + R₂ + ...

🎙️ When resistors are in series, it's like a single road with multiple speed bumps. The current has only one path to follow, so it's the same through each resistor. The total resistance simply adds up, making the overall resistance higher.

Resistors in Parallel: Multiple Paths for Current

  • Components are connected across the same two points.
  • Voltage across each resistor is the same.
  • Total resistance is less than the smallest individual resistance: 1/R_total = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + ...

🎙️ In a parallel connection, current has multiple paths, like several roads leading to the same destination. This means the total resistance decreases, and more current can flow overall. This is how household circuits are usually wired.

Heating Effect of Electric Current

  • When current flows through a resistor, electrical energy is converted to heat energy.
  • This is due to collisions between electrons and atoms of the conductor.
  • Given by Joule's Law of Heating: H = I²Rt.

🎙️ Have you ever noticed your phone or laptop getting warm after prolonged use? That's the heating effect of electric current at play. The resistance of the components converts some electrical energy into heat.

🎙️ Electric power tells us how quickly electrical energy is being used. A 100-Watt bulb consumes energy faster than a 60-Watt bulb. Understanding power helps us choose efficient appliances and calculate energy costs.

Recap: Key Concepts of Electricity!

  • Electric Charge: Fundamental property, measured in Coulombs.
  • Current: Flow of charge, measured in Amperes (I = Q/t).
  • Potential Difference: 'Push' for charge, measured in Volts (V = W/Q).
  • Ohm's Law: V = IR, linking Voltage, Current, and Resistance.
  • Resistance: Opposition to flow, measured in Ohms (depends on L, A, material, T).
  • Heating Effect: H = I²Rt, used in heaters, bulbs, fuses.
  • Power: Rate of energy consumption, measured in Watts (P = VI).

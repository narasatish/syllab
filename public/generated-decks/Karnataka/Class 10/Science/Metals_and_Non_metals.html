Metals: The Strong & Shiny Ones

  • Generally hard and shiny.
  • Good conductors of heat and electricity.
  • Can be shaped easily.

🎙️ When you think of metals, picture things like gold, silver, or iron. They are typically strong, have a distinctive shine, and are excellent at conducting heat and electricity. We use them for many purposes, from jewellery to construction.

Non-metals: The Diverse Group

  • Can be solids, liquids, or gases.
  • Often dull and brittle.
  • Poor conductors of heat and electricity.

🎙️ On the other hand, non-metals are a much more diverse group. They can be found in all three states of matter. Think of carbon (charcoal), sulfur, or even the oxygen we breathe. They usually lack the shine of metals and don't conduct well.

Physical Property 1: Lustre (Shine)

  • Metals have a shiny surface (metallic lustre).
  • Non-metals generally have a dull appearance.
  • Example: Gold jewellery vs. Charcoal.

🎙️ The first physical property is lustre, which refers to how shiny a material is. Metals, like gold or silver, have a characteristic metallic shine. Non-metals, such as wood or sulfur, usually appear dull.

✨
Shiny vs. Dull

Physical Property 2: Hardness

  • Metals are generally hard (e.g., Iron).
  • Non-metals are usually soft (e.g., Sulfur).
  • Exception: Diamond (a form of carbon) is the hardest natural substance.

🎙️ Hardness is about a material's resistance to scratching or indentation. Most metals are quite hard and strong. Non-metals tend to be softer, although there's a famous exception: diamond, which is a non-metal and the hardest known material!

💪
Hard vs. Soft

Physical Property 3: Malleability (Sheets)

  • Metals can be hammered into thin sheets.
  • This property allows making foils and sheets.
  • Non-metals are brittle and break when hammered.

🎙️ Malleability is the ability of a material to be hammered or pressed permanently out of shape without breaking. Think of aluminium foil or gold sheets. Metals are malleable, but non-metals would just shatter if you tried to hammer them.

🔨
Hammer into Sheets

Physical Property 4: Ductility (Wires)

  • Metals can be drawn into thin wires.
  • Example: Copper wires in electrical circuits.
  • Non-metals are not ductile and break easily.

🎙️ Ductility is the ability to be drawn into thin wires. This is why we have copper wires for electricity or gold wires in intricate jewellery. Non-metals lack this property; they are brittle and cannot be stretched into wires.

Physical Property 5: Conductivity (Heat & Electricity)

  • Metals are good conductors of heat and electricity.
  • Example: Cooking utensils (heat), Electrical wires (electricity).
  • Non-metals are generally poor conductors (insulators), except graphite.

🎙️ Conductivity refers to a material's ability to allow heat or electricity to pass through it. Metals are excellent conductors, which is why we use them for cooking pots and electrical wiring. Non-metals are usually insulators, meaning they resist the flow of heat and electricity, though graphite is a notable exception.

Physical Property 6: Sonority (Sound)

  • Metals produce a ringing sound when struck.
  • Example: School bell made of metal.
  • Non-metals do not produce a ringing sound.

🎙️ Sonority is the property of producing a ringing sound when struck. Think of a temple bell or a school bell – they're made of metal because metals produce a clear, resonant sound. Non-metals simply make a dull thud.

🔔
Ringing Sound

Physical Property 7: Melting & Boiling Points

  • Metals generally have high melting and boiling points.
  • Example: Iron needs very high temperatures to melt.
  • Non-metals usually have lower melting and boiling points.

🎙️ The melting and boiling points tell us at what temperature a substance changes state. Metals typically require a lot of heat to melt or boil, indicating strong bonds. Non-metals, on the other hand, often have much lower melting and boiling points.

🌡️

Chemical Properties: Why Do They React?

  • Atoms react to achieve a stable electron configuration.
  • Metals tend to lose electrons to form positive ions.
  • Non-metals tend to gain electrons to form negative ions.

🎙️ Now let's move to chemical properties, which describe how substances interact with each other. Atoms react to become more stable, usually by achieving a full outer shell of electrons. Metals typically give away electrons, while non-metals prefer to accept them.

⚛️
Atoms Seeking Stability

Chemical Property: Reaction with Oxygen

  • Metals react with oxygen to form basic oxides.
  • Example: Iron rusting (Iron + Oxygen → Iron Oxide).
  • Non-metals react with oxygen to form acidic or neutral oxides.

🎙️ When metals burn in air or react slowly with oxygen, they form metal oxides, which are generally basic in nature. A common example is the rusting of iron. Non-metals also react with oxygen, but they form oxides that are typically acidic or neutral.

🔥
Oxidation

Chemical Property: Reaction with Water

  • Metals react with water/steam to form metal oxides/hydroxides and hydrogen gas.
  • Example: Sodium reacts vigorously with cold water.
  • Non-metals generally do not react with water.

🎙️ Many metals react with water, some very vigorously like sodium, producing hydrogen gas. Others react only with steam. Non-metals, however, typically do not react with water or steam at all.

💧
Water Reaction

Chemical Property: Reaction with Acids

  • Metals react with dilute acids to produce metal salts and hydrogen gas.
  • Example: Zinc + Hydrochloric Acid → Zinc Chloride + Hydrogen.
  • Non-metals generally do not react with dilute acids.

🎙️ When metals react with dilute acids, they typically displace hydrogen from the acid, forming a metal salt and releasing hydrogen gas. Non-metals, on the other hand, usually do not react with dilute acids.

Reactivity Series: Who is Strongest?

  • A list of metals arranged in decreasing order of their reactivity.
  • More reactive metals can displace less reactive metals from their salt solutions.
  • Helps predict reaction outcomes.

🎙️ The reactivity series is like a ranking system for metals. It tells us which metals are more reactive than others. A more reactive metal can 'push out' a less reactive metal from its compound, which is important for understanding many chemical reactions.

Ionic Compounds: Transferring Electrons

  • Formed by the transfer of electrons from a metal to a non-metal.
  • Metals lose electrons to form positive ions (cations).
  • Non-metals gain electrons to form negative ions (anions).
  • Example: Sodium Chloride (NaCl).

🎙️ When a metal reacts with a non-metal, they often form ionic compounds. The metal atom gives away its electrons to the non-metal atom. This creates oppositely charged ions that are strongly attracted to each other, forming an ionic bond.

🔗
Electron Transfer

Properties of Ionic Compounds

  • Are solid and generally hard.
  • Have high melting and boiling points.
  • Conduct electricity in molten state or in solution, but not in solid state.

🎙️ Ionic compounds have distinct properties due to their strong electrostatic forces. They are typically hard solids with high melting points. While they don't conduct electricity as solids, they do so when melted or dissolved in water because their ions become free to move.

💡
Conductivity in Solution

Occurrence of Metals: Where Are They?

  • Metals are found in the Earth's crust as minerals.
  • Minerals are naturally occurring chemical substances.
  • Ores are minerals from which metals can be profitably extracted.

🎙️ Most metals are not found in their pure form in nature. Instead, they exist as compounds within rocks called minerals. An ore is a specific type of mineral from which we can economically extract a metal.

Extraction of Metals: Getting the Pure Metal

  • The process of obtaining pure metals from their ores is called metallurgy.
  • Different methods are used based on the metal's reactivity.
  • Includes concentration, reduction, and refining steps.

🎙️ Metallurgy is the entire process of extracting metals from their ores. This involves several steps, including concentrating the ore, reducing the metal compound to get the crude metal, and then refining it to achieve high purity. The method chosen depends on how reactive the metal is.

Corrosion: The Enemy of Metals!

  • The gradual eating away of a metal by its surroundings.
  • Example: Rusting of iron (reaction with oxygen and moisture).
  • Leads to damage and loss of metal properties.

🎙️ Corrosion is a natural process that degrades metals. Think of how iron turns reddish-brown when it rusts, or how silver tarnishes. It's a chemical reaction, usually with oxygen or other substances in the environment, that slowly destroys the metal.

Preventing Corrosion: Protecting Our Metals

  • Painting: forms a protective barrier (e.g., iron gates).
  • Oiling/Greasing: prevents contact with air and moisture.
  • Galvanizing: coating with a layer of zinc.
  • Alloying: mixing metals to improve resistance (e.g., stainless steel).

🎙️ Since corrosion causes significant damage, we have many ways to prevent it. These methods generally involve creating a barrier between the metal and its environment, or changing the metal's properties through alloying.

🛡️
Protection Methods

Alloys: Better Together!

  • Homogeneous mixtures of two or more metals, or a metal and a non-metal.
  • Made to enhance properties like strength, hardness, or corrosion resistance.
  • Example: Steel (Iron + Carbon), Brass (Copper + Zinc).

🎙️ Alloys are special mixtures made by combining metals, or a metal with a non-metal, to create a new material with improved properties. For example, steel is an alloy of iron and carbon, which is much stronger than pure iron.

Common Mistakes & Exam Tips

  • Confusing malleability and ductility.
  • Not remembering the reactivity series order.
  • Forgetting exceptions (e.g., graphite conducts, diamond is hard).
  • Practice writing balanced chemical equations for reactions.
  • Focus on differences between metals and non-metals.

🎙️ Here are some common pitfalls to avoid. Make sure you understand the subtle differences between similar terms. For exams, practice writing reactions and remember the key characteristics and uses of different metals and non-metals.

Recap: Key Takeaways

  • Metals are generally shiny, hard, malleable, ductile, good conductors, and sonorous.
  • Non-metals are diverse, dull, brittle, and poor conductors (except graphite).
  • Chemical reactions are driven by electron transfer (ionic bonds).
  • Metals are extracted from ores and protected from corrosion.
  • Alloys improve metal properties.

🎙️ To summarize, we've learned that metals and non-metals are fundamental categories of elements with distinct physical and chemical properties. Their interactions lead to the formation of various compounds, and understanding these properties is crucial for their applications and protection.

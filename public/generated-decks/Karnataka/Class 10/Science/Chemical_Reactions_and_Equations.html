What is a Chemical Reaction?

  • Process changing reactants into products
  • New substances formed with new properties
  • Atoms rearrange, but no new atoms created.

🎙️ A chemical reaction is basically a process where one or more substances transform into different substances. Think of it like ingredients in a recipe becoming a completely new dish. The atoms just rearrange themselves.

Physical vs. Chemical Changes

  • Physical: No new substance, easily reversible
  • Chemical: New substance formed, often irreversible

🎙️ It's important to distinguish between physical and chemical changes. A physical change, like melting ice, doesn't create anything new. But a chemical change, like burning wood, creates ash and smoke, which are entirely new substances.

How to Write a Chemical Equation

  • Write formulas for reactants on left
  • Write formulas for products on right
  • Use '+' between multiple reactants/products
  • Use '→' for 'yields' or 'forms'

🎙️ To write an equation, first identify your reactants and products. Then, write their correct chemical formulas. Place the reactants on the left, products on the right, and connect them with an arrow. Don't forget the plus signs for multiple substances!

Balancing Chemical Equations

  • Unbalanced: Unequal atoms on both sides
  • Balanced: Equal atoms of each element on both sides
  • Essential for accurate chemistry

🎙️ Imagine a seesaw. If one side has more weight, it's unbalanced. Similarly, in an unbalanced chemical equation, the number of atoms of an element isn't equal on both sides. We need to balance them to obey a fundamental law.

Balance Scale

Law of Conservation of Mass

  • Mass can neither be created nor destroyed
  • Total mass of reactants = Total mass of products
  • This means atoms must be conserved

🎙️ This law is the core reason why we balance equations. It states that during any chemical reaction, the total mass of the substances remains constant. If mass is conserved, then the number of atoms of each element must also be conserved.

⚖️
Mass is Conserved

Steps to Balance an Equation

  • 1. Write unbalanced equation
  • 2. Count atoms of each element on both sides
  • 3. Balance elements one by one (metals, non-metals, O, H)
  • 4. Check balanced equation

🎙️ Balancing an equation is a systematic process. Start by writing the equation, then meticulously count atoms. Balance elements one by one, usually starting with metals, then non-metals, and saving oxygen and hydrogen for last. Always double-check your work!

Balancing Example 1: Water Formation

  • Unbalanced: H₂ + O₂ → H₂O
  • Balance Oxygen: H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O
  • Balance Hydrogen: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O (Balanced!)

🎙️ Let's balance the formation of water. Initially, we have 2 oxygens on the left and 1 on the right. We put a '2' in front of H₂O. Now we have 4 hydrogens on the right, so we put a '2' in front of H₂ on the left. Now it's balanced!

Balancing Example 2: Iron Steam Reaction

  • Unbalanced: Fe + H₂O → Fe₃O₄ + H₂
  • Balance Fe: 3Fe + H₂O → Fe₃O₄ + H₂
  • Balance O: 3Fe + 4H₂O → Fe₃O₄ + H₂
  • Balance H: 3Fe + 4H₂O → Fe₃O₄ + 4H₂ (Balanced!)

🎙️ This one is a bit more complex. Start with Iron: 3 Fe on the right, so add '3' to Fe on the left. Next, Oxygen: 4 O on the right, so add '4' to H₂O on the left. Finally, Hydrogen: 8 H on the left, so add '4' to H₂ on the right. And it's balanced!

Combination Reaction: Joining Together

  • Two or more reactants combine
  • Form a single product
  • A + B → AB type reaction

🎙️ As the name suggests, a combination reaction is when two or more simple substances, or even compounds, come together to form one more complex product. It's like building a larger structure from smaller blocks.

Decomposition Reaction: Breaking Apart

  • A single reactant breaks down
  • Forms two or more simpler products
  • AB → A + B type reaction

🎙️ Decomposition is the opposite of combination. Here, a single compound breaks down into two or more simpler substances. This often requires energy, like heat, light, or electricity, to break the bonds.

Displacement Reaction: The Stronger Takes Over

  • More reactive element displaces less reactive element
  • Takes its place in a compound
  • A + BC → AC + B type reaction

🎙️ In a displacement reaction, a more reactive element essentially kicks out a less reactive element from its compound. Think of it like a stronger player replacing a weaker player on a team. The reactivity series helps us predict these reactions.

Double Displacement: Partner Swap

  • Exchange of ions between two compounds
  • Forms two new compounds
  • AB + CD → AD + CB type reaction

🎙️ In a double displacement reaction, it's like two couples swapping partners. The positive and negative ions of two different compounds exchange places, leading to the formation of two entirely new compounds. Often, one of the products is insoluble and forms a precipitate.

🎙️ When you mix solutions of barium chloride and sodium sulphate, you immediately see a white, cloudy substance forming. This is barium sulphate, which is insoluble in water and settles down as a precipitate. Sodium chloride is the other product, remaining dissolved.

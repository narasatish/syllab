Physical Changes: No New Substance Formed

  • The substance's identity remains the same.
  • Only its form, size, or state changes.
  • These changes are often reversible.

🎙️ In a physical change, you don't create anything new. Think of ice melting into water, or sugar dissolving in tea. The water is still water, and the sugar is still sugar. You can often reverse these changes easily.

Chemical Changes: New Substances are Born!

  • One or more new substances are formed.
  • The original substance loses its identity.
  • These changes are usually irreversible.

🎙️ Now, for chemical changes, it's different. Here, completely new substances with new properties are formed. When wood burns, it turns into ash and smoke – you can't turn that ash back into wood. This is the essence of a chemical reaction.

Parts of a Chemical Equation: Reactants and Products

  • **Reactants:** Substances that undergo change (Left Hand Side).
  • **Products:** New substances formed (Right Hand Side).
  • An arrow (→) indicates the direction of the reaction.

🎙️ In a chemical equation, the starting materials are on the left, and the newly formed substances are on the right. The arrow means 'yields' or 'forms'. We also use symbols like (s), (l), (g), (aq) to show the physical states.

Balancing Chemical Equations: Law of Conservation of Mass

  • Matter cannot be created or destroyed.
  • The total mass of reactants must equal the total mass of products.
  • This means the number of atoms of each element must be the same on both sides!

🎙️ Why do we balance equations? It's all about the Law of Conservation of Mass, a fundamental principle in chemistry. Just like a balance scale, both sides of our chemical equation must have equal 'weight' in terms of atoms.

⚖️
Balance Scale

Steps to Balance an Equation (Hit and Trial Method)

  • 1. Write the unbalanced equation.
  • 2. Count atoms of each element on both sides.
  • 3. Start balancing elements, usually metals, then non-metals, then H and O.
  • 4. Check if all atoms are balanced. Repeat if necessary.

🎙️ Balancing equations is a skill, and we often use the 'hit and trial' method. It involves systematically adjusting coefficients in front of the chemical formulas until the atom count matches on both sides. Let's try an example.

Worked Example: Balancing Fe + H₂O → Fe₃O₄ + H₂

  • **Step 1:** Fe + H₂O → Fe₃O₄ + H₂
  • **Step 2:** Balance Fe (add 3Fe)
  • **Step 3:** Balance O (add 4H₂O)
  • **Step 4:** Balance H (add 4H₂)

🎙️ Let's balance the reaction of iron with steam. First, count atoms. We see Fe is 1 on LHS and 3 on RHS. So, we put '3' before Fe. Then, oxygen: 1 on LHS, 4 on RHS. So, we put '4' before H₂O. Finally, balance hydrogen. This systematic approach helps achieve a balanced equation.

1. Combination Reaction: Two Become One!

  • **Analogy:** Two friends joining hands to form a team.
  • **Definition:** Two or more reactants combine to form a single product.
  • **General Form:** A + B → AB
  • **Example:** Burning of coal: C (s) + O₂ (g) → CO₂ (g)

🎙️ In a combination reaction, you have multiple starting materials, but they come together to form just one new substance. Think of it as 'addition' in chemistry. Burning coal is a perfect example, where carbon and oxygen combine to form carbon dioxide.

🤝🤝
Joining Together
19 / 30

2. Decomposition Reaction: Breaking Apart!

  • **Analogy:** Breaking a large laddoo into smaller pieces.
  • **Definition:** A single reactant breaks down into two or more simpler products.
  • **General Form:** AB → A + B
  • **Example:** Decomposition of limestone: CaCO₃ (s) → CaO (s) + CO₂ (g)

🎙️ Decomposition is the opposite of combination. Here, one complex substance breaks down into simpler ones, often requiring energy like heat, light, or electricity. Heating limestone to produce quicklime and carbon dioxide is a classic example.

🍪
Breaking Down

3. Displacement Reaction: The Stronger Player Wins!

  • **Analogy:** A stronger player replaces a weaker one in a game.
  • **Definition:** A more reactive element displaces a less reactive element from its compound.
  • **General Form:** A + BC → AC + B
  • **Example:** Iron nail in copper sulphate solution: Fe (s) + CuSO₄ (aq) → FeSO₄ (aq) + Cu (s)

🎙️ Imagine a competition! In a displacement reaction, one element is more reactive and kicks out another, less reactive element from its compound. When an iron nail is dipped in blue copper sulphate solution, the iron displaces copper, turning the solution green and depositing red-brown copper on the nail.

4. Double Displacement Reaction: Partner Swapping!

  • **Analogy:** Two couples swapping partners at a dance.
  • **Definition:** Two different atoms or groups of atoms exchange places.
  • **General Form:** AB + CD → AD + CB
  • **Example:** Sodium sulphate and barium chloride: Na₂SO₄ (aq) + BaCl₂ (aq) → BaSO₄ (s) + 2NaCl (aq)

🎙️ In a double displacement reaction, it's like a grand exchange! Ions from two different compounds switch places, forming two new compounds. A common outcome is the formation of a precipitate, like the white barium sulphate when sodium sulphate and barium chloride solutions are mixed.

5. Oxidation and Reduction (Redox Reactions)

  • **Oxidation:** Gain of oxygen OR loss of hydrogen OR loss of electrons.
  • **Reduction:** Loss of oxygen OR gain of hydrogen OR gain of electrons.
  • These reactions always occur together!

🎙️ Oxidation and reduction are two sides of the same coin. When one substance is oxidized, another is simultaneously reduced. Think of it in terms of oxygen or hydrogen transfer, or more fundamentally, electron transfer. For example, when copper oxide reacts with hydrogen, copper oxide is reduced and hydrogen is oxidized.

Redox reactions involve electron transfer between reactants. Oxidation = loss of electrons

Effects of Oxidation in Everyday Life: Corrosion

  • **Analogy:** An old bicycle rusting in the rain.
  • **Definition:** The slow eating up of metals by the action of air, moisture, or a chemical.
  • **Example:** Rusting of iron (Fe₂O₃.xH₂O).

🎙️ Corrosion is a destructive effect of oxidation that we see often. When iron objects are exposed to moist air, they react with oxygen and water to form a reddish-brown flaky substance called rust. This weakens the metal and causes significant damage.

Effects of Oxidation in Everyday Life: Rancidity

  • **Analogy:** The stale smell and taste of old potato chips.
  • **Definition:** The oxidation of fats and oils in food items exposed to air.
  • This leads to unpleasant smell and taste.

🎙️ Rancidity affects our food. When fatty or oily foods are left exposed to air for a long time, the fats and oils get oxidized, causing them to become stale. This is why chips packets are flushed with nitrogen gas – to prevent oxidation and keep them fresh.

Common Mistakes to Avoid!

  • Not balancing equations correctly (violating Conservation of Mass).

  • Incorrectly identifying oxidizing/reducing agents.

🎙️ Students often make these mistakes. Always double-check your atom count when balancing. Remember to include state symbols for completeness. Make sure you understand the unique characteristics of each reaction type to avoid confusion.

Covalent Bonds: Sharing Electrons

  • Atoms need a stable outer shell.
  • Carbon has 4 electrons in its outer shell.
  • It can neither lose nor gain 4 electrons easily.
  • It shares electrons to achieve stability.

🎙️ Just like we share toys or food with friends, atoms share electrons to become stable. Carbon has four electrons in its outermost shell, and it needs four more to complete it. Instead of losing or gaining, it prefers to share.

🔗
Shared Connection

Methane: A Perfect Example of Sharing

  • Carbon shares 1 electron with each of 4 Hydrogen atoms.
  • Each Hydrogen shares 1 electron with Carbon.
  • Both achieve a stable electron configuration.
  • Forms a strong covalent bond.

🎙️ Let's look at Methane, a simple carbon compound. One carbon atom shares its four outer electrons, one with each of the four hydrogen atoms. In return, each hydrogen shares its single electron with carbon. This sharing creates strong covalent bonds, making methane a stable molecule.

Carbon's Valency: Four Hands to Connect!

  • Valency is an atom's combining capacity.
  • Carbon has a valency of 4.
  • It can form 4 covalent bonds.
  • This makes it highly versatile.

🎙️ Imagine carbon having four hands ready to shake! This 'four-handed' nature is called tetravalency. It means carbon can form four covalent bonds with other atoms, allowing it to connect in many different ways and build complex structures.

Catenation: Carbon's Self-Linking Power!

  • Carbon atoms can link with other carbon atoms.
  • Forms long chains, branched chains, and rings.
  • This property is unique and very strong.
  • Leads to millions of carbon compounds.

🎙️ Catenation is like carbon atoms holding hands with each other to form super long chains, or even loops and branches! This self-linking ability is incredibly strong and allows carbon to build an enormous variety of compounds, more than any other element.

Alkanes: The Saturated Family (Single Bonds)

  • Carbon atoms linked by ONLY single bonds.
  • They are 'saturated' with hydrogen atoms.
  • General formula: CnH2n+2.
  • Examples: Methane (CH₄), Ethane (C₂H₆).

🎙️ Alkanes are like a train where all the carriages are connected by just one hook each. Every carbon atom forms only single bonds with other atoms. They are called 'saturated' because they have the maximum possible number of hydrogen atoms attached, making them quite stable.

➖➖➖

Alkenes: The Unsaturated Family (Double Bonds)

  • Contain at least one Carbon-Carbon double bond.
  • They are 'unsaturated' – can add more atoms.
  • General formula: CnH2n.
  • Examples: Ethene (C₂H₄), Propene (C₃H₆).

🎙️ Alkenes are like a train where at least two carriages are connected by two hooks! This double bond makes them 'unsaturated' because they have fewer hydrogen atoms than alkanes and can undergo reactions to add more atoms across the double bond.

⚌⚌
One Double Bond

Alkynes: The Highly Unsaturated Family (Triple Bonds)

  • Contain at least one Carbon-Carbon triple bond.
  • Even more 'unsaturated' than alkenes.
  • General formula: CnH2n-2.
  • Examples: Ethyne (C₂H₂), Propyne (C₃H₄).

🎙️ Alkynes are the most 'connected' train carriages, with a triple bond between two carbon atoms. They are highly unsaturated and very reactive, meaning they are eager to break one or two of those bonds to form new connections with other atoms.

Isomers: Same Recipe, Different Dish!

  • Compounds with the same molecular formula.
  • But different structural arrangements of atoms.
  • Leads to different physical and chemical properties.
  • Example: Butane and Isobutane (both C₄H₁₀).

🎙️ Think of isomers like having the same ingredients in your kitchen but arranging them differently to make two completely different dishes. Butane and Isobutane both have 4 carbons and 10 hydrogens, but their atoms are connected differently, giving them distinct properties.

Alcohols: The -OH Group

  • Contains a hydroxyl group (-OH) attached to carbon.
  • General formula: R-OH (where R is a hydrocarbon).
  • Examples: Methanol (CH₃OH), Ethanol (C₂H₅OH).
  • Ethanol is found in alcoholic beverages and sanitizers.

🎙️ Alcohols are compounds where a hydrogen atom in a hydrocarbon is replaced by a hydroxyl (-OH) group. Ethanol, a common alcohol, is used in hand sanitizers and is also the alcohol found in drinks. It's a key ingredient in many industrial processes too.

Carboxylic Acids: The -COOH Group

  • Contains a carboxyl group (-COOH).
  • These compounds are acidic in nature.
  • General formula: R-COOH.
  • Examples: Ethanoic acid (Acetic acid in vinegar).

🎙️ Carboxylic acids are characterized by the -COOH group. They are weak acids, and a common example is ethanoic acid, which is the main component of vinegar, giving it its distinctive sour taste and smell. They play vital roles in biochemistry and industry.

Aldehydes & Ketones: Carbonyl Compounds

  • Both contain a Carbonyl group (C=O).
  • Aldehydes: Carbonyl group at the end of the chain (-CHO).
  • Ketones: Carbonyl group in the middle of the chain (>C=O).
  • Examples: Formaldehyde (aldehyde), Acetone (ketone).

🎙️ Aldehydes and ketones both contain a carbon double-bonded to an oxygen, called a carbonyl group. The difference is its position: at the end of a chain for aldehydes, and within the chain for ketones. Acetone, a common nail polish remover, is a ketone.

Combustion: Burning Carbon Compounds

  • Carbon compounds burn in oxygen.
  • Release heat and light (exothermic).
  • Produce Carbon Dioxide (CO₂) and Water (H₂O).
  • Used as fuels (LPG, petrol, diesel).

🎙️ Combustion is simply the burning of carbon compounds, like the LPG in your kitchen stove. When they burn in sufficient oxygen, they release a lot of energy in the form of heat and light, producing carbon dioxide and water. This property makes them excellent fuels.

Oxidation: Adding Oxygen Atoms

  • Carbon compounds can be oxidized.
  • Oxygen is added to the compound.
  • Usually involves oxidizing agents (e.g., KMnO₄, K₂Cr₂O₇).
  • Example: Ethanol to Ethanoic Acid.

🎙️ Oxidation is a chemical reaction where oxygen atoms are added to a compound, or hydrogen atoms are removed. For instance, if you leave wine (which contains ethanol) open, it can slowly oxidize to vinegar (ethanoic acid) due to bacteria and air.

Addition Reaction: Filling the Gaps

  • Unsaturated hydrocarbons (alkenes, alkynes) undergo this.
  • Atoms are added across the double or triple bond.
  • Converts unsaturated to saturated compounds.
  • Example: Hydrogenation of vegetable oils (Ni catalyst).

🎙️ Imagine an unsaturated hydrocarbon having 'empty seats' because of its double or triple bonds. In an addition reaction, new atoms, like hydrogen, come and fill these seats, breaking the multiple bonds and converting the compound into a saturated one. This is how vegetable oils are converted to vanaspati ghee.

2New atoms attach
3Becomes saturated

Substitution Reaction: Swapping Places

  • Saturated hydrocarbons (alkanes) undergo this.
  • One atom or group replaces another atom or group.
  • Requires specific conditions (e.g., sunlight for methane-chlorine).
  • Example: Methane reacting with Chlorine.

🎙️ Unlike addition, substitution happens with saturated compounds that have no 'empty seats'. It's like a swap: one atom or group is replaced by another. For example, methane can react with chlorine in the presence of sunlight, where a hydrogen atom is replaced by a chlorine atom.

Soaps & Detergents: Your Cleaning Allies!

  • Long chain carboxylic acids (soaps) or sulfonates (detergents).
  • Have two parts: a 'water-loving' head and a 'oil-loving' tail.
  • The tail is a long hydrocarbon chain.
  • The head is an ionic part (-COO⁻Na⁺ or -SO₃⁻Na⁺).

🎙️ Soaps and detergents are amazing cleaning agents! They are special molecules with two distinct ends: one end loves water (hydrophilic head) and the other end loves oil and grease (hydrophobic tail). This dual nature is key to their cleaning power.

🧼
Cleaning Action

Micelle Formation: Trapping the Dirt!

  • Hydrophobic tails surround dirt/oil particles.
  • Hydrophilic heads face outwards into water.
  • Forms a spherical structure called a micelle.
  • The micelle traps dirt and gets washed away with water.

🎙️ When you mix soap with oily dirt and water, the soap molecules cleverly arrange themselves. Their oil-loving tails dive into the dirt, while their water-loving heads stay on the outside, facing the water. This forms tiny spheres called micelles, which encapsulate the dirt, allowing it to be rinsed away.

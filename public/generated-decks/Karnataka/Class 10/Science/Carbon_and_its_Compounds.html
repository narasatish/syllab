Why is Carbon Special?

  • Carbon is a non-metal element.
  • Its atomic number is 6.
  • It forms millions of compounds, more than any other element!

🎙️ Carbon is a non-metal, found in Group 14 of the periodic table. Its ability to form an incredibly vast number of compounds is what makes it stand out. From the air we breathe to the food we eat, carbon is fundamental.

🎙️ Carbon is truly ubiquitous! It's a key component of the air, part of rocks and minerals, and absolutely essential for all forms of life. Think about how many things around you contain carbon.

Bonding in Carbon: Covalent Bonds

  • Carbon achieves stability by sharing electrons.
  • It doesn't lose or gain electrons.
  • Sharing electrons forms strong covalent bonds.

🎙️ Unlike ionic bonds where electrons are transferred, carbon forms covalent bonds. It shares its valence electrons with other atoms, allowing both atoms to achieve a stable electron configuration. This sharing creates very strong and stable bonds.

Valency of Carbon: Tetravalency

  • Carbon has 4 valence electrons.
  • It can form 4 covalent bonds.
  • This allows it to bond with many other atoms.

🎙️ The term 'tetra' means four, and 'valency' refers to its combining capacity. Carbon's ability to form four bonds makes it incredibly versatile. It can bond with other carbon atoms or a variety of other elements like hydrogen, oxygen, and nitrogen.

Catenation: Carbon's Self-Linking Property

  • Carbon atoms can link with other carbon atoms.
  • They form long chains, branched chains, or rings.
  • This property gives rise to a vast number of compounds.

🎙️ Catenation is a unique property of carbon, allowing it to form extensive structures. Imagine building blocks that can connect in endless ways – that's what carbon atoms do. This is a primary reason for the immense diversity of organic compounds.

Allotropes of Carbon: Different Forms

  • Allotropes are different structural forms of the same element.
  • They have different physical properties but similar chemical properties.
  • Carbon has several well-known allotropes.

🎙️ Think of allotropes as different outfits for the same person. Carbon can arrange its atoms in various ways, leading to distinct materials like diamond and graphite. Each allotrope has unique characteristics due to its atomic arrangement.

💎
Different Structures
8 / 25

Allotrope: Diamond

  • Each carbon atom is bonded to four other carbon atoms.
  • Forms a rigid, 3D tetrahedral structure.
  • Extremely hard, non-conductor of electricity.

🎙️ Diamond is famous for its brilliance and hardness. Its unique structure, where every carbon atom is tightly bound to four others, makes it the hardest natural substance. It's used in jewelry and for cutting tools.

💍
Hardest Natural Substance
9 / 25

Allotrope: Graphite

  • Each carbon atom is bonded to three other carbon atoms.
  • Forms hexagonal layers that can slide over each other.
  • Soft, slippery, and a good conductor of electricity.

🎙️ Graphite is what you find in your pencil lead. Its layered structure allows the layers to slide past each other, making it soft and slippery. Unlike diamond, it's an excellent conductor of electricity due to free electrons within its layers.

✏️
Pencil Lead

Allotrope: Fullerene

  • A spherical molecule made of 60 carbon atoms (C60).
  • Looks like a football (soccer ball).
  • Known as Buckminsterfullerene.

🎙️ Fullerenes are a more recently discovered allotrope of carbon, with the most famous being C60. Its structure resembles a soccer ball, with pentagonal and hexagonal rings. They have potential uses in medicine and nanotechnology.

Hydrocarbons: Carbon + Hydrogen

  • Compounds made only of carbon and hydrogen atoms.
  • They are the simplest organic compounds.
  • Examples include methane, ethane, propane.

🎙️ Hydrocarbons are the building blocks of many important substances, including fuels like petrol and natural gas. Their structures can vary greatly depending on how the carbon and hydrogen atoms are arranged and bonded.

Saturated Hydrocarbons: Alkanes

  • Contain only single bonds between carbon atoms.
  • They are very stable and less reactive.
  • General formula: CnH2n+2. Example: Methane (CH4).

🎙️ Saturated hydrocarbons, or alkanes, are like a straight, strong chain with no weak links. Because all bonds are single, they are 'saturated' with hydrogen atoms and are generally unreactive. Methane, the primary component of natural gas, is a common alkane.

➖
Single Bonds
13 / 25

Unsaturated Hydrocarbons: Alkenes

  • Contain at least one double bond between carbon atoms.
  • More reactive than alkanes due to the double bond.
  • General formula: CnH2n. Example: Ethene (C2H4).

🎙️ Alkenes introduce a 'double knot' in the carbon chain. This double bond makes them 'unsaturated' because they could potentially hold more hydrogen atoms. They are more reactive and are important in the plastics industry, like for making polythene.

🪢
Double Bond
14 / 25

Unsaturated Hydrocarbons: Alkynes

  • Contain at least one triple bond between carbon atoms.
  • Even more reactive than alkenes.
  • General formula: CnH2n-2. Example: Ethyne (C2H2).

🎙️ Alkynes have a 'triple knot' between carbon atoms, making them the most unsaturated and reactive of the hydrocarbon series. Ethyne, also known as acetylene, is used in welding due to its high combustion temperature.

Triple Bond

Functional Group: Halogens

  • Includes Chlorine (-Cl), Bromine (-Br), Iodine (-I).
  • Replaces a hydrogen atom in a hydrocarbon.
  • Example: Chloromethane (CH3Cl).

🎙️ Halogens are elements from Group 17 of the periodic table. When a halogen atom attaches to a carbon chain, it forms a haloalkane, which has different properties than the original hydrocarbon. Chloroform, used as a solvent, is an example.

Functional Group: Alcohol

  • Contains the hydroxyl group (-OH).
  • Changes the compound's properties significantly.
  • Example: Ethanol (CH3CH2OH), found in alcoholic beverages.

🎙️ The hydroxyl group (-OH) is characteristic of alcohols. Ethanol is a common alcohol used as a solvent and disinfectant. Methanol is another alcohol but is highly poisonous.

🍷
Hydroxyl Group

Functional Group: Aldehyde

  • Contains the formyl group (-CHO).
  • Always found at the end of a carbon chain.
  • Example: Methanal (HCHO), also known as formaldehyde.

🎙️ Aldehydes have a carbon atom double-bonded to an oxygen atom and single-bonded to a hydrogen atom and another carbon chain. Formaldehyde is used as a preservative and in making resins. It has a pungent smell.

👃
Formyl Group

Functional Group: Ketone

  • Contains the carbonyl group (C=O) within the carbon chain.
  • The carbonyl carbon is bonded to two other carbon atoms.
  • Example: Propanone (CH3COCH3), commonly known as acetone.

🎙️ Ketones also have a carbonyl group, but it's always in the middle of a carbon chain, unlike aldehydes. Acetone is a very common ketone used as a nail polish remover and a solvent in many industries.

🍩
Carbonyl Group

Functional Group: Carboxylic Acid

  • Contains the carboxyl group (-COOH).
  • These compounds are acidic in nature.
  • Example: Ethanoic acid (CH3COOH), found in vinegar.

🎙️ Carboxylic acids are organic acids, identifiable by their -COOH group. Ethanoic acid, or acetic acid, is the main component of vinegar, giving it its sour taste. These acids can react with bases to form salts.

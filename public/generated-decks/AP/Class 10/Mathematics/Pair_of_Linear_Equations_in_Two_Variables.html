Equations: A Balancing Act!

  • Think of an equation like a balanced weighing scale.
  • What's on the left side must equal what's on the right side.
  • Example: 2 + 3 = 5 (Both sides are equal!)

🎙️ Before we dive into 'linear equations', let's recall what an 'equation' is. Imagine a traditional weighing scale. For it to be balanced, the weight on one side must be exactly equal to the weight on the other side. An equation works the same way – the expression on the left of the '=' sign must always be equal to the expression on the right.

Linear Equations: Straight Lines!

  • An equation where the highest power of any variable is 1.
  • When graphed, a linear equation always forms a straight line.
  • Example: 2x + 5 = 11 (Power of 'x' is 1).

🎙️ Now, what makes an equation 'linear'? The word 'linear' comes from 'line'. In a linear equation, the variables are never squared or cubed; their highest power is always 1. This is important because when you plot the solutions of such an equation on a graph, they always fall on a perfectly straight line, never a curve!

Linear Equation in One Variable: A Quick Look

  • Only one unknown quantity (e.g., 'x').
  • General form: ax + b = 0, where 'a' ≠ 0.
  • Always has exactly one unique solution.
  • Example: 3x - 6 = 0 => x = 2 (One solution)

🎙️ You've already worked with linear equations in one variable, like 2x + 4 = 10. Here, there's only one unknown, 'x'. These equations are straightforward; you usually perform some basic algebra to isolate the variable and find its single, unique value. For instance, in 3x - 6 = 0, x must be 2.

Linear Equation in Two Variables: New Horizons!

  • Involves two unknown quantities (e.g., 'x' and 'y').
  • General form: ax + by + c = 0, where 'a', 'b' ≠ 0.
  • Each solution is a pair of values (x, y).
  • Example: 2x + 3y = 10

🎙️ Now, let's add another dimension! What if our problem has two unknowns, say 'x' and 'y'? This is a linear equation in two variables. The solutions aren't single numbers anymore; they are pairs of numbers (x, y) that make the equation true. For example, in 2x + 3y = 10, (5, 0) is a solution, and (2, 2) is also a solution! There can be many such pairs.

Pair of Linear Equations: Working Together!

  • When we have two linear equations involving the same two variables.
  • We are looking for a single pair of (x, y) values that satisfies BOTH equations.
  • Example: x + y = 5 AND 2x - y = 1

🎙️ This is the heart of our chapter! A 'pair' means we have two such equations. Our goal is to find a unique pair of values for 'x' and 'y' that works for the first equation AND the second equation simultaneously. It's like finding a common ground for both equations. This common ground, or common solution, is what we aim to find.

  • We have two primary ways to find the common solution (x, y):
  • 1. Graphical Method: Drawing lines and finding their intersection.

🎙️ Just like there are different routes to reach a destination, there are different methods to solve a pair of linear equations. We'll explore two main categories: the graphical method, where we literally draw the lines, and algebraic methods, which involve more number crunching. Both methods lead to the same solution!

Graphical Method: Drawing the Lines!

  • Each linear equation represents a straight line on a graph.
  • The solution to the pair of equations is the point where these two lines intersect.
  • It's like finding where two roads cross each other!

🎙️ Let's start with the graphical method. It's very visual! Imagine each equation is a unique straight line. When you draw both lines on the same graph paper, if they cross, that crossing point is special. That point (with its x and y coordinates) is the solution that satisfies both equations.

📈

Graphical Method - Step 1: Find Points

  • For each equation, find at least two pairs of (x, y) values that satisfy it.
  • Choose simple values for x (like 0, 1, 2) and calculate the corresponding y.
  • Example: For x + y = 5, if x=0, y=5; if x=5, y=0. Points: (0,5) and (5,0).

🎙️ To draw a straight line, you only need two points. So, for each of your two equations, you'll find a couple of (x, y) pairs that make the equation true. A good trick is to set x=0 and find y, then set y=0 and find x. This gives you the points where the line crosses the axes, which are usually easy to plot.

Graphical Method - Step 2: Plot and Draw!

  • Draw a coordinate plane (x-axis and y-axis).
  • Plot the points you found for each equation.
  • Use a ruler to draw a straight line through the points for each equation.

🎙️ Once you have your points, it's time to bring out your graph paper and ruler! Carefully plot the (x, y) coordinates you found for the first equation. Then, connect them with a straight line. Do the same for the second equation. Make sure your lines are extended properly, as the intersection might not be near your initial points.

Graphical Method - Step 3: Where Do They Meet?

  • Observe where the two lines cross each other.
  • The coordinates (x, y) of this intersection point are the solution.
  • If they don't cross, there's no unique solution!

🎙️ This is the most exciting part! Look closely at your graph. Do the two lines intersect? If they do, that specific point is your solution. Read its x-coordinate and its y-coordinate. That (x, y) pair is the unique answer that satisfies both equations. If they don't meet, it tells us something special about the equations, which we'll see next.

Case 1: Intersecting Lines (Unique Solution)

  • The two lines cross at exactly one point.
  • This point represents the one and only solution (x, y) for the pair of equations.
  • This system of equations is called 'consistent' and 'independent'.

🎙️ When you draw your lines and they cross each other at a single point, congratulations! You've found a unique solution. This is the most common scenario. It means there's one specific pair of 'x' and 'y' values that makes both equations true. We call such a system 'consistent' because it has a solution.

Case 2: Parallel Lines (No Solution)

  • The two lines run side-by-side and never meet, no matter how far they extend.
  • Since they never intersect, there is no common point, meaning no solution.
  • This system is called 'inconsistent'.

🎙️ What if your lines never cross? If they are parallel, like railway tracks, they will never meet. In this case, there is no point (x, y) that lies on both lines simultaneously. Therefore, the pair of equations has 'no solution'. We call such a system 'inconsistent' because it fails to have a common solution.

Case 3: Coincident Lines (Infinitely Many Solutions)

  • The two lines are actually the same line; one lies exactly on top of the other.
  • Every point on the line is a common solution.
  • This system is 'consistent' and 'dependent' (one equation depends on the other).

🎙️ The third possibility is quite interesting: what if the two equations represent the exact same line? This means one line perfectly overlaps the other. In this case, every single point on that line is a common solution. Since a line has infinite points, there are 'infinitely many solutions'. This system is 'consistent' but also 'dependent', as one equation can be derived from the other.

Algebraic Methods: Solving with Math!

  • Sometimes drawing graphs can be time-consuming or imprecise.
  • Algebraic methods use step-by-step calculations to find exact solutions.
  • We'll learn two main algebraic methods: Substitution and Elimination.

🎙️ While the graphical method is great for visualizing, it can sometimes be tricky to get precise answers, especially if the intersection point isn't on exact whole numbers. That's where algebraic methods come in! These methods use logical mathematical steps to guarantee an exact solution. We'll explore two powerful ones: Substitution and Elimination.

Substitution Method: Swapping Values!

  • Step 1: Express one variable (e.g., 'x') in terms of the other variable (e.g., 'y') from one equation.
  • Step 2: Substitute this expression into the SECOND equation.
  • Step 3: Solve the resulting equation, which now has only one variable.
  • Step 4: Substitute the found value back into the expression from Step 1 to find the other variable.

🎙️ The substitution method is like replacing a detective with a clue! You rearrange one equation to say 'x equals something with y'. Then, you take that 'something with y' and literally substitute it into the other equation in place of 'x'. This leaves you with an equation with only 'y', which you can solve easily. Once you have 'y', you can find 'x'.

1Isolate a variable
2Substitute into other equation
3Solve for one variable
4Find the second variable
22 / 30

Example: Let's Substitute!

  • Equation 1: x + y = 5
  • Equation 2: 2x - y = 1
  • From (1), x = 5 - y.
  • Substitute into (2): 2(5 - y) - y = 1 => 10 - 2y - y = 1 => 10 - 3y = 1 => 3y = 9 => y = 3.
  • Substitute y=3 into x = 5 - y => x = 5 - 3 => x = 2.
  • Solution: (x, y) = (2, 3)

🎙️ Let's work through an example. We have two equations. First, I'll take the simpler equation (x + y = 5) and express x in terms of y: x = 5 - y. Now, I'll substitute this entire expression (5 - y) wherever I see 'x' in the second equation. This gives me an equation with only 'y'. Solving for y, I get 3. Then, I plug y=3 back into my expression for x to find x=2. So, the solution is (2, 3).

Elimination Method: Making Variables Disappear!

  • Step 1: Multiply equations by constants so that the coefficients of one variable are equal (or opposite).
  • Step 2: Add or subtract the modified equations to 'eliminate' one variable.
  • Step 3: Solve the resulting equation for the remaining variable.
  • Step 4: Substitute the found value into either original equation to find the other variable.

🎙️ The elimination method is like a magic trick! The idea is to make one of the variables vanish. You do this by making sure the 'x' coefficients (or 'y' coefficients) in both equations are the same number, or opposite numbers. Then, you either add or subtract the two equations. This cancels out one variable, leaving you with just one, which you can then solve. Finally, substitute back to find the other.

1Make coefficients equal
2Add/Subtract to eliminate
3Solve for one variable
4Find the second variable

Example: Let's Eliminate!

  • Equation 1: x + y = 5
  • Equation 2: 2x - y = 1
  • Notice 'y' has coefficients +1 and -1. We can add directly!
  • Add (1) + (2): (x + y) + (2x - y) = 5 + 1
  • 3x = 6 => x = 2.
  • Substitute x=2 into (1): 2 + y = 5 => y = 3.
  • Solution: (x, y) = (2, 3)

🎙️ Let's use the same example. Here, notice that 'y' in the first equation has a coefficient of +1, and in the second, it has -1. They are already opposites! This means we can directly add the two equations together. When we do, the 'y' terms cancel out, leaving us with only 'x'. Solving for x gives us 2. Then, plug x=2 into either original equation to find y=3. The solution is (2, 3), the same as with substitution!

Consistent vs. Inconsistent Systems

  • Consistent System: Has at least one solution (intersecting or coincident lines).
  • Inconsistent System: Has no solution (parallel lines).
  • Dependent System (a type of consistent): Infinitely many solutions (coincident lines).

🎙️ These terms help us describe the nature of the solutions. A 'consistent' system is one that has a solution – it could be one unique solution (intersecting lines) or infinitely many solutions (coincident lines). An 'inconsistent' system is one that has no solution at all, which happens when the lines are parallel. Understanding these terms is key for classifying equation pairs.

Common Mistakes to Avoid!

  • Sign Errors: Be very careful with positive and negative signs during calculations.
  • Calculation Mistakes: Double-check your arithmetic, especially when multiplying or dividing.
  • Not Checking Solutions: Always substitute your (x, y) back into BOTH original equations to verify.

🎙️ Even experienced mathematicians make mistakes! The most common errors in this chapter involve simple arithmetic and sign changes. A minus sign in the wrong place can completely change your answer. Always take a moment to double-check your calculations. And most importantly, once you find your solution, plug it back into both original equations to ensure it works for both. This simple step can save you many marks!

Back to Our Mangoes and Bananas!

  • Let 'x' be the price of one mango and 'y' be the price of one banana.
  • Ravi's purchase: 3x + 2y = 70 (Eq 1)
  • Sita's purchase: 2x + 4y = 80 (Eq 2)
  • Using elimination (multiply Eq 1 by 2): 6x + 4y = 140. Subtract Eq 2: (6x+4y)-(2x+4y) = 140-80 => 4x = 60 => x = 15.
  • Substitute x=15 into Eq 1: 3(15) + 2y = 70 => 45 + 2y = 70 => 2y = 25 => y = 12.5.
  • Solution: Mango = ₹15, Banana = ₹12.50

🎙️ Remember our fruit mystery from the beginning? Now we have the tools to solve it! Let's set up the equations: 3x + 2y = 70 and 2x + 4y = 80. I'll use elimination here. If I multiply the first equation by 2, I get 6x + 4y = 140. Now, I can subtract the second equation from this new one to eliminate 'y'. This gives me 4x = 60, so x = 15. Plugging x=15 back into the first equation, I find y = 12.5. So, a mango costs ₹15 and a banana costs ₹12.50! Mystery solved!

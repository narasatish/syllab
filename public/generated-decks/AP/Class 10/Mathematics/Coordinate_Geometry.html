The Cartesian System: Axes

  • Two perpendicular lines: X-axis (horizontal) and Y-axis (vertical).
  • They intersect at the Origin (0,0).
  • Used to locate any point on a plane.

🎙️ The Cartesian system is like a grid. The horizontal line is called the X-axis, and the vertical line is the Y-axis. Where they meet is our starting point, called the Origin, with coordinates (0,0).

Quadrants: Dividing the Plane

  • The axes divide the plane into four regions.
  • These are called Quadrants I, II, III, and IV.
  • Points have specific sign combinations in each quadrant.

🎙️ Just like a compass divides directions, the X and Y axes divide our plane into four sections. We label them starting from the top-right and moving counter-clockwise: Quadrant I, II, III, and IV.

Plotting Points: (x, y)

  • Every point is represented by an ordered pair (x, y).
  • 'x' is the x-coordinate (horizontal distance from Y-axis).
  • 'y' is the y-coordinate (vertical distance from X-axis).

🎙️ To plot a point like (3, 2), we first move 3 units along the X-axis from the origin, then 2 units up parallel to the Y-axis. The order matters, (x,y) is different from (y,x)!

  • Used to find the distance between any two points.
  • Based on the Pythagorean Theorem.
  • Essential for calculating lengths of line segments.

🎙️ Now that we know how to locate points, what if we want to know the straight-line distance between two of them? The Distance Formula helps us calculate exactly that, using their coordinates.

Deriving the Distance Formula

  • Consider two points P(x1, y1) and Q(x2, y2).
  • Form a right-angled triangle with these points.
  • Apply Pythagoras: (distance)² = (change in x)² + (change in y)².

🎙️ Imagine connecting the two points and then drawing horizontal and vertical lines to form a right-angled triangle. The horizontal side is the difference in x-coordinates, and the vertical side is the difference in y-coordinates. Then, Pythagoras comes to our rescue!

The Distance Formula

  • Distance d = √[(x₂ - x₁)² + (y₂ - y₁)²]
  • Always results in a positive value.
  • Units are usually implied, e.g., units, cm, km.

🎙️ This is the formula you'll use! Make sure to correctly substitute the x and y coordinates of your two points. Remember, squaring the differences makes sure that negative signs don't cause issues, and the square root gives us the actual length.

Worked Example: Find the Distance

  • Find the distance between P(2, 3) and Q(5, 7).
  • x₁=2, y₁=3; x₂=5, y₂=7
  • d = √[(5-2)² + (7-3)²] = √[3² + 4²] = √[9+16] = √25 = 5 units.

🎙️ Let's walk through this example. First, identify your (x1, y1) and (x2, y2). Then, carefully plug them into the formula, perform the subtractions, square the results, add them, and finally take the square root. The distance here is 5 units.

Section Formula: Dividing a Line Segment

  • Used to find the coordinates of a point that divides a line segment.
  • Divides it in a given ratio (m₁:m₂).
  • Can be internal or external division (we focus on internal).

🎙️ Imagine you have a straight rope, and you want to cut it into two pieces in a specific ratio, say 1:2. The Section Formula helps us find the exact point where you should make that cut, given the endpoints of the rope.

Midpoint Formula: The Middle Point

  • A special case of the Section Formula (ratio 1:1).
  • Finds the exact middle point of a line segment.
  • Midpoint M = ((x₁+x₂)/2, (y₁+y₂)/2).

🎙️ When the ratio of division is 1:1, the point is exactly in the middle. This is so common that it has its own simpler formula. You just average the x-coordinates and average the y-coordinates.

Worked Example: Section Formula

  • Find the point that divides the line segment joining A(1, 7) and B(4, -3) in the ratio 2:3 internally.
  • x = (3*1 + 2*4)/(2+3) = 11/5
  • y = (3*7 + 2*(-3))/(2+3) = 15/5 = 3
  • Point is (11/5, 3).

🎙️ Here, m1 is 2 and m2 is 3. Carefully substitute the values into the formula for both x and y coordinates. Pay close attention to the order of operations and the signs. The resulting point is (11/5, 3).

Area of a Triangle using Coordinates

  • Find the area of a triangle given its vertices (x₁,y₁), (x₂,y₂), (x₃,y₃).
  • Area = ½ | x₁(y₂ - y₃) + x₂(y₃ - y₁) + x₃(y₁ - y₂) |
  • The absolute value ensures a positive area.

🎙️ This formula is incredibly useful! Instead of needing base and height, we can directly calculate the area of any triangle just by knowing the coordinates of its three corners. Remember the cyclic order of y-coordinates within the brackets.

Worked Example: Area of Triangle

  • Find the area of the triangle with vertices (1, -1), (-4, 6), and (-3, -5).
  • Area = ½ |1(6 - (-5)) + (-4)(-5 - (-1)) + (-3)(-1 - 6)|
  • = ½ |1(11) - 4(-4) - 3(-7)|
  • = ½ |11 + 16 + 21| = ½ |48| = 24 square units.

🎙️ Substitute the coordinates carefully into the formula. Pay extra attention to the negative signs, especially when subtracting negative numbers. The absolute value at the end makes sure our area is always positive.

Collinearity: Are Points on a Straight Line?

  • Three or more points are collinear if they lie on the same straight line.
  • If points are collinear, the area of the triangle formed by them is zero.
  • Can also be checked using distance formula (AB+BC=AC).

🎙️ Collinearity is an important concept. If three points can form a triangle, they are not collinear. But if they all lie on the same straight line, they can't form a triangle, so the area enclosed by them would be zero.

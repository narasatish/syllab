The Distance Formula: Using Pythagoras!

  • We can form a right-angled triangle between the two points.
  • The distance between the points becomes the hypotenuse.
  • Pythagoras Theorem (a² + b² = c²) helps us find this length.

🎙️ Believe it or not, the distance formula is a clever application of a theorem you already know: Pythagoras Theorem! By drawing a right-angled triangle between our two points, the distance we want to find becomes the hypotenuse.

📐
Right Triangle Magic

The Distance Formula (Formal)

  • For points A(x1, y1) and B(x2, y2):
  • Distance AB = √[(x2 - x1)² + (y2 - y1)²]
  • Remember to square the differences and then add them before taking the square root.

🎙️ Here it is, the famous Distance Formula! It might look a bit complex at first glance, but it's just Pythagoras in disguise. You find the difference in x-coordinates, square it, do the same for y, add them up, and then take the square root.

Worked Example 1: Calculating Distance

  • Find the distance between A(2, 3) and B(5, 7).

🎙️ Let's work through an example together. We have point A at (2,3) and point B at (5,7). First, identify your x1, y1, x2, y2 values. Then, carefully substitute them into the distance formula and simplify step-by-step.

1Identify (x1, y1) = (2, 3) and (x2, y2) = (5, 7)
2Substitute into formula: √[(5-2)² + (7-3)²]
3Simplify: √[(3)² + (4)²] = √[9 + 16] = √25
4Calculate: Distance AB = 5 units

Special Case: Distance from Origin

  • If one point is the Origin (0,0) and the other is P(x,y):
  • Distance OP = √[(x - 0)² + (y - 0)²]
  • Distance OP = √(x² + y²) - a simpler form!

🎙️ What if one of our points is the Origin itself? The formula simplifies beautifully! Since x1 and y1 are both zero, the formula becomes much easier to remember: just the square root of x-squared plus y-squared. It's like finding how far you are from home!

Section Formula Explained: Proportional Division

  • A point P(x,y) divides a line segment AB.
  • It divides it internally in a given ratio, m1:m2.
  • This formula is derived using the concept of similar triangles.

🎙️ The Section Formula is all about ratios. If a point divides a line segment into two parts, and those parts are in a specific ratio, say m1 to m2, then this formula helps us find the coordinates of that dividing point. It's built on the concept of similar triangles, which is quite clever!

⚖️
Balancing the Ratio
17 / 30

The Section Formula (Formal)

  • For points A(x1, y1) and B(x2, y2).
  • Point P(x,y) divides AB internally in ratio m1:m2.
  • P(x,y) = [ (m1x2 + m2x1) / (m1 + m2) , (m1y2 + m2y1) / (m1 + m2) ]

🎙️ Here is the Section Formula. It looks a bit long, but notice the pattern: m1 multiplies with the second point's coordinates, and m2 with the first point's coordinates. And the denominator is always the sum of the ratios. Practice writing it down a few times to get comfortable.

Worked Example 2: Using Section Formula

  • Find the coordinates of the point which divides the line segment joining (4, -3) and (8, 5) in the ratio 3:1 internally.

🎙️ Let's apply the Section Formula to a problem. We have two points and a ratio. Carefully identify all the values for x1, y1, x2, y2, m1, and m2. Then, substitute them into the formula for both x and y coordinates, and calculate the final point.

1Identify (x1, y1)=(4, -3), (x2, y2)=(8, 5), m1=3, m2=1
2Calculate x = (3*8 + 1*4) / (3+1) = (24+4)/4 = 28/4 = 7
3Calculate y = (3*5 + 1*-3) / (3+1) = (15-3)/4 = 12/4 = 3
4The point is (7, 3)

Midpoint Formula: The Exact Middle

  • A special, simpler case of the Section Formula.
  • Occurs when the ratio is 1:1 (m1=1, m2=1).
  • The midpoint coordinates are simply the average of the x and y coordinates.

🎙️ The Midpoint Formula is a very useful special case of the Section Formula. If a point divides a line segment into two *equal* halves, the ratio is 1:1. This simplifies our formula significantly, making it super easy to find the exact middle point.

Worked Example 3: Finding the Midpoint

  • Find the midpoint of the line segment joining (2, 5) and (6, 1).

🎙️ Let's quickly find the midpoint of a line segment. This is often used in geometry problems. Just add the x-coordinates and divide by two, and do the same for the y-coordinates. It's like finding the average position!

1Identify (x1, y1)=(2, 5) and (x2, y2)=(6, 1)
2Calculate x-coordinate: (2 + 6) / 2 = 8 / 2 = 4
3Calculate y-coordinate: (5 + 1) / 2 = 6 / 2 = 3
4The midpoint is (4, 3)

Area of a Triangle Using Coordinates

  • For vertices A(x1, y1), B(x2, y2), C(x3, y3):
  • Area = 1/2 |x1(y2 - y3) + x2(y3 - y1) + x3(y1 - y2)|
  • The absolute value ensures the area is always positive, as area cannot be negative.

🎙️ This is the formula for the area of a triangle when you have its vertices. It looks long, but there's a cyclic pattern to the x and y terms. Remember to take the absolute value at the end, as area can never be negative.

Worked Example 4: Area of a Triangle

  • Find the area of the triangle with vertices (1, -1), (-4, 6), and (-3, -5).

🎙️ Let's calculate the area of a triangle using this formula. Be very careful with the negative signs and the order of operations. It's a multi-step calculation, so take your time and double-check each step.

1Identify vertices: (x1, y1)=(1, -1), (x2, y2)=(-4, 6), (x3, y3)=(-3, -5)
2Substitute into formula: 1/2 |1(6 - (-5)) + (-4)(-5 - (-1)) + (-3)(-1 - 6)|
3Simplify: 1/2 |1(11) - 4(-4) - 3(-7)| = 1/2 |11 + 16 + 21|
4Calculate: 1/2 |48| = 24 square units

Collinearity: Points on a Line

  • If three points are collinear, they lie on the same straight line.
  • In this case, the 'triangle' formed by them would be flat.
  • Therefore, the area of the triangle formed by collinear points is zero.

🎙️ What if three points don't form a triangle, but instead lie on a single straight line? We call such points 'collinear'. If points are collinear, the 'triangle' they form would be flat, meaning its area would be zero. This gives us a powerful way to test if points are collinear!

Common Mistakes to Avoid!

  • **Sign Errors**: Be very careful with positive and negative numbers in calculations.
  • **Order of Operations**: Always follow BODMAS/PEMDAS (Brackets, Orders, Division, Multiplication, Addition, Subtraction).
  • **Formula Confusion**: Don't mix up distance, section, and area formulas.
  • **Calculation Errors**: Double-check your arithmetic, especially with squares and square roots.

🎙️ Even experienced mathematicians make mistakes! Here are some common pitfalls in coordinate geometry. Always pay close attention to signs, follow the correct order of operations, and be sure you're using the right formula for the right problem. A little care goes a long way!

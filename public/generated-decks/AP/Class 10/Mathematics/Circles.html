What is a Circle?

  • A circle is a set of all points in a plane.
  • These points are equidistant from a fixed point.
  • That fixed point is called the 'center'.

🎙️ Imagine drawing a perfect curve where every point on that curve is exactly the same distance from a central point. That's a circle! Think of drawing with a compass – the fixed point is the center, and the pencil traces the circle.

Key Term: Radius

  • A line segment from the center to any point on the circle.
  • Denoted by 'r'.
  • All radii of a circle are equal in length.

🎙️ The radius is like the 'arm' of the circle, extending from its heart to its boundary. It's a fundamental measure, telling us how 'big' the circle is from its center. Remember, no matter where you draw it, the radius length is always the same for a given circle.

Key Term: Diameter

  • A line segment passing through the center.
  • Connects two points on the circle.
  • Diameter (d) = 2 × Radius (r).

🎙️ The diameter is the longest possible straight line you can draw inside a circle, always passing through the center. It essentially cuts the circle in half. Knowing the radius allows us to easily find the diameter, and vice-versa.

Key Term: Chord

  • A line segment connecting any two points on the circle.
  • The diameter is the longest chord of a circle.
  • It does not necessarily pass through the center.

🎙️ Think of a chord as a 'bridge' connecting two points on the circle's edge. It can be short or long, but it doesn't have to go through the center. The special case where it does pass through the center is what we call the diameter.

Key Term: Arc

  • A continuous piece or segment of a circle's circumference.
  • Can be a minor arc (smaller) or major arc (larger).
  • Formed by two points on the circle.

🎙️ An arc is simply a curved portion of the circle's boundary. Imagine cutting a piece of string that follows the curve of the circle; that's an arc. We classify them as minor or major depending on whether they are less than or greater than half the circle.

Key Term: Sector

  • The region bounded by two radii and an arc.
  • Looks like a 'slice of pizza' or a 'pie piece'.
  • Can be a minor sector or a major sector.

🎙️ A sector is a region inside the circle that's enclosed by two radii and the arc between them. It's a very common shape you see, especially when we talk about dividing circles into parts. Think of dividing a cake or a pizza.

Key Term: Segment

  • The region bounded by a chord and an arc.
  • Also known as a 'circular segment'.
  • Can be a minor segment or a major segment.

🎙️ Unlike a sector, a segment is formed by a straight chord and the arc it cuts off. Imagine cutting off a piece of a circular cookie with a straight knife; the part you cut off is a segment. It resembles a crescent moon shape.

Tangent to a Circle

  • A line that touches the circle at exactly one point.
  • This point is called the 'point of contact'.
  • The line does not enter the interior of the circle.

🎙️ Imagine a line just grazing the edge of a circle without crossing into its space. That's a tangent! It's like a car wheel touching the road at a single point. This concept is very important in higher geometry.

Secant to a Circle

  • A line that intersects the circle at two distinct points.
  • It passes through the interior of the circle.
  • Contrast with a tangent which touches at only one point.

🎙️ While a tangent just 'kisses' the circle, a secant 'cuts through' it, intersecting at two different places. This distinction is crucial for understanding how lines interact with circles. Think of a secant as an extended chord.

Property 1: Tangent and Radius

  • The radius drawn to the point of contact is perpendicular to the tangent.
  • This means they form a 90-degree angle.
  • A fundamental theorem in circle geometry.

🎙️ This is a very powerful property! It tells us that if you draw a line from the center to where the tangent touches the circle, that line will always be at a right angle to the tangent. This property is key for solving many problems.

Real-Life Example: Bicycle Wheel

  • The road is a tangent to the bicycle wheel.
  • The spoke from the hub (center) to the road contact point is a radius.
  • This radius is perpendicular to the road surface at that point.

🎙️ Think about how a bicycle wheel touches the ground. The ground acts as a tangent line. The spoke that goes from the center of the wheel down to the point where it touches the ground represents the radius, and it forms a perfect right angle with the road.

Worked Example 1: Finding Radius

  • A tangent PQ at a point P of a circle of radius 5 cm meets a line through the center O at a point Q so that OQ = 12 cm. Find the length PQ.
  • Solution: OP is radius (5 cm) and perpendicular to PQ.
  • In right triangle OPQ, by Pythagoras theorem: OP² + PQ² = OQ².
  • 5² + PQ² = 12² => 25 + PQ² = 144 => PQ² = 119 => PQ = √119 cm.

🎙️ Here's how we apply the perpendicularity property. We form a right-angled triangle using the radius, the tangent, and the line from the center to the external point. Then, we can use the Pythagorean theorem to find the unknown length. Make sure to draw a clear diagram for such problems.

Property 2: Tangents from an External Point

  • The lengths of tangents drawn from an external point to a circle are equal.
  • If PA and PB are tangents from point P, then PA = PB.
  • This property is useful in many geometric proofs.

🎙️ This is another crucial property. If you pick a point outside a circle and draw two tangents from it to the circle, those two tangent segments will always have the same length. It's like having two equal 'arms' reaching out from the point to touch the circle.

Worked Example 2: Equal Tangents

  • From a point Q, the length of the tangent to a circle is 24 cm and the distance of Q from the center is 25 cm. Find the radius of the circle.
  • Solution: Let the radius be 'r' (OP). OQ = 25 cm, PQ = 24 cm.
  • Angle OPQ = 90° (Radius is perpendicular to tangent).
  • In right triangle OPQ: OP² + PQ² = OQ² => r² + 24² = 25².
  • r² + 576 = 625 => r² = 49 => r = 7 cm.

🎙️ Let's apply our knowledge again. We use the property that the radius is perpendicular to the tangent at the point of contact to form a right triangle. Then, the Pythagorean theorem helps us calculate the unknown radius. These steps are common in tangent-related problems.

Number of Tangents from a Point

  • Point inside the circle: Zero tangents.
  • Point on the circle: Exactly one tangent.
  • Point outside the circle: Exactly two tangents.

🎙️ The position of a point relative to a circle determines how many tangents can be drawn from it. If the point is inside, no tangent can be drawn. If it's on the circle, only one. If it's outside, exactly two tangents can be drawn, and their lengths will be equal.

Circles and their Relationships

  • Intersecting circles: Two points of common intersection.
  • Touching circles: One point of common contact (internally or externally).
  • Non-intersecting circles: No common points.

🎙️ Just like people, circles can interact in different ways. They can overlap, touch at a single point, or remain completely separate. Understanding these relationships is important when dealing with multiple circles in a problem.

Concentric Circles

  • Circles that share the same center point.
  • They have different radii.
  • Appear as 'rings' or 'targets'.

🎙️ Concentric circles are like Russian nesting dolls or the rings you see when you throw a stone into water. They all originate from the same central point but have different sizes. They are very common in design and engineering.

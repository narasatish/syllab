  • The constant distance is the 'radius'.

Lines and Circles: Three Possibilities

  • A line can completely miss the circle.
  • A line can cut the circle at two distinct points.
  • A line can touch the circle at exactly one point.

🎙️ When we draw a straight line and a circle in the same plane, there are only three ways they can interact. Let's explore each one in detail.

Possibility 1: Non-Intersecting Line

  • The line and the circle have no common point.
  • They are completely separate from each other.
  • The shortest distance from the centre to the line is greater than the radius.

Formal Definition of a Tangent

  • A tangent to a circle is a line that touches the circle at only one point.
  • This point is known as the point of contact.
  • It's a special case of a secant where the two endpoints coincide.

🎙️ So, remember, the defining characteristic of a tangent is that it's a line that kisses the circle at just one unique point, its point of contact.

Theorem 1: Tangent and Radius Perpendicularity

  • The tangent at any point of a circle is perpendicular to the radius through the point of contact.
  • This means they form a 90-degree angle.
  • It's a fundamental property of tangents.

🎙️ This is a very important theorem! It tells us that if you draw a radius to the point where a tangent touches the circle, these two lines will always meet at a perfect right angle.

Analogy for Tangent-Radius Theorem: A Turning Car

  • Imagine a car on a circular race track.
  • At any point, the car's direction (tangent) is perpendicular to the line from the center of the track (radius) to the car's position.
  • This ensures the smoothest turn without skidding off.

🎙️ Think about how a race car navigates a curve. For the most efficient turn, its instantaneous path (tangent) is always at 90 degrees to the imaginary line connecting it to the center of the curve (radius).

Proof Idea for Theorem 1

  • Consider a point P on the circle, and a tangent XY at P.
  • Draw radius OP. Take any other point Q on XY.
  • OQ must be longer than OP, because Q is outside the circle.
  • Since OP is the shortest distance from O to XY, OP ⊥ XY.

🎙️ The proof relies on the fact that the shortest distance from a point to a line is the perpendicular distance. Any other point on the tangent will be further away from the center than the point of contact.

1Draw radius OP
2Take point Q on tangent
3OQ > OP
4OP is shortest, so perpendicular

Worked Example 1: Applying Perpendicularity

  • A tangent PQ at a point P of a circle of radius 5 cm meets a line through the centre O at a point Q so that OQ = 13 cm. Find the length of PQ.
  • Solution: OP is radius = 5 cm. OP ⊥ PQ (Theorem 1).
  • In right ΔOPQ, by Pythagoras theorem: OQ² = OP² + PQ².
  • 13² = 5² + PQ² => 169 = 25 + PQ² => PQ² = 144 => PQ = 12 cm.

🎙️ This is a classic application of the theorem. Once you know the radius is perpendicular to the tangent, you can often use the Pythagorean theorem to find unknown lengths in the resulting right-angled triangle.

Case A: Point Inside the Circle

  • If a point P lies inside the circle, no tangent can be drawn through it.
  • Any line passing through P will necessarily cut the circle at two points (it will be a secant).
  • It's impossible to touch the circle at just one point from within.

🎙️ Imagine trying to touch the edge of a plate with a straight stick while holding the stick inside the plate. You can only poke through, never just touch the edge from the inside.

Case B: Point On the Circle

Case C: Point Outside the Circle

Theorem 2: Lengths of Tangents from an External Point

  • The lengths of tangents drawn from an external point to a circle are equal.
  • If PA and PB are two tangents from point P to a circle, then PA = PB.
  • This is another crucial property for solving problems.

🎙️ This theorem simplifies many problems! It states that if you draw those two tangents from an outside point, the distance from the external point to each point of contact will always be the same.

  • You want to touch the roundabout's edge (circle) at two points, A and B.
  • The distance you walk from P to A will be exactly the same as P to B, assuming you take the 'tangential' path.

🎙️ Think of two roads leading from a common point to just touch the edge of a circular park. The length of these two 'touching' roads will be identical from your starting point.

Proof Idea for Theorem 2

  • Let O be the centre, P be the external point, and A, B be points of contact.
  • Draw radii OA and OB, and join OP.
  • ΔOAP and ΔOBP are right-angled triangles (OA ⊥ PA, OB ⊥ PB).
  • By RHS congruence (OA=OB, OP=OP, ∠A=∠B=90°), ΔOAP ≅ ΔOBP.
  • Therefore, PA = PB (CPCTC).

🎙️ The proof for this theorem is quite elegant and uses congruent triangles. By showing that the two triangles formed are identical, we can prove that their corresponding sides, the tangent segments, are equal.

1Draw radii, join OP
2Identify right triangles
3Use RHS congruence
4Conclude PA = PB

Worked Example 2: Applying Tangent Lengths

  • Two tangents TP and TQ are drawn to a circle with centre O from an external point T. Prove that ∠PTQ = 2∠OPQ.
  • Solution: TP = TQ (Theorem 2). So, ΔTPQ is isosceles.
  • Let ∠TPQ = ∠TQP = x. Then ∠PTQ = 180° - 2x.
  • Also, OP ⊥ TP, so ∠OPT = 90°. Thus ∠OPQ = ∠OPT - ∠TPQ = 90° - x.
  • Therefore, ∠PTQ = 180° - 2x = 2(90° - x) = 2∠OPQ. Hence proved.

🎙️ This example combines both theorems! We use the equality of tangent lengths to deduce an isosceles triangle, and the perpendicularity of radius and tangent to set up angle relationships. This helps prove the given relation.

Common Mistakes to Avoid!

  • Confusing secants with tangents.
  • Forgetting that radius is perpendicular to tangent at point of contact.
  • Incorrectly applying Pythagoras theorem.
  • Assuming chords are tangents or vice-versa.

🎙️ Students often make these mistakes. Always double-check your definitions and theorems. Drawing clear diagrams can help you avoid misinterpretations, especially with perpendicularity.

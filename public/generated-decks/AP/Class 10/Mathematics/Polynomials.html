Introducing Polynomials: Special Expressions

  • A polynomial is a special type of algebraic expression.
  • Variables must have non-negative integer exponents.
  • No variables in the denominator or under a radical sign.

🎙️ Not all algebraic expressions are polynomials. The key rule is that the powers of the variables must be whole numbers (0, 1, 2, ...). Expressions like '1/x' or '√x' are not polynomials.

Anatomy of a Polynomial: Terms, Coefficients, Variables

  • Terms: Parts separated by + or - signs (e.g., in 3x² + 2x - 5, terms are 3x², 2x, -5).
  • Coefficients: The numerical part multiplying the variable (e.g., 3 in 3x²).
  • Variables: The letters representing unknown values (e.g., x).

🎙️ Let's break down a polynomial like '3x² + 2x - 5'. Each part has a specific name. Understanding these terms is crucial for correctly working with polynomials.

Degree of a Polynomial: The 'Power' Ranking

  • The highest power of the variable in a polynomial.
  • It determines the polynomial's 'rank' or type.
  • Example: In 5x³ + 2x² - 7, the highest power is 3.

🎙️ The degree is one of the most important characteristics of a polynomial. It tells us a lot about its behaviour and how many solutions it might have. It's simply the largest exponent you see on any variable.

Finding the Degree: Examples

  • 2x + 5: Degree is 1 (power of x is 1).
  • 4y² - 3y + 1: Degree is 2 (highest power of y is 2).
  • 7: Degree is 0 (can be written as 7x⁰, where x⁰=1).

Zeros of a Polynomial: The 'Solution' Points

  • A real number 'k' is a zero of a polynomial P(x) if P(k) = 0.
  • It's the value of 'x' that makes the polynomial equal to zero.
  • Also known as 'roots' of the polynomial.

🎙️ Think of zeros as the special 'input' values that make the polynomial 'output' zero. Finding these zeros is a fundamental problem in algebra and has many practical applications.

Finding Zeros: For Linear Polynomials

  • For P(x) = ax + b, set P(x) = 0.
  • So, ax + b = 0.
  • Solving for x gives x = -b/a.

🎙️ Finding zeros for linear polynomials is straightforward. It's essentially solving a simple linear equation. Let's try an example to see how it works.

1Set P(x) = 0
2ax + b = 0
3Solve for x

Worked Example: Finding Zero of a Linear Polynomial

  • Find the zero of P(x) = 2x - 6.
  • Step 1: Set P(x) = 0 => 2x - 6 = 0.
  • Step 2: Add 6 to both sides => 2x = 6.
  • Step 3: Divide by 2 => x = 3. So, 3 is the zero.

🎙️ Here, when x is 3, the polynomial 2x - 6 becomes 2(3) - 6 = 6 - 6 = 0. This confirms that 3 is indeed the zero of the polynomial. Simple, right?

Relationship Between Zeros and Coefficients (Quadratic)

  • For a quadratic polynomial ax² + bx + c, let α and β be its zeros.
  • Sum of zeros (α + β) = -b/a.
  • Product of zeros (α ⋅ β) = c/a.

🎙️ This is a very powerful relationship! Without even finding the zeros, we can determine their sum and product directly from the coefficients of the quadratic polynomial. This saves a lot of time in calculations.

🔗
Connecting Parts

Worked Example: Zeros & Coefficients (Quadratic)

  • For x² + 5x + 6, find the sum and product of zeros.
  • Here, a=1, b=5, c=6.
  • Sum of zeros = -b/a = -5/1 = -5.
  • Product of zeros = c/a = 6/1 = 6.

🎙️ If you were to find the zeros of x² + 5x + 6, you'd find they are -2 and -3. Notice that (-2) + (-3) = -5 and (-2) × (-3) = 6. The formulas hold true!

Forming Quadratic Polynomials Given Zeros

  • If α and β are zeros, the polynomial is k[x² - (α + β)x + αβ].
  • Where k is any non-zero real number.
  • This formula uses the sum and product of zeros.

🎙️ This is the reverse process! If you know the zeros, you can construct the quadratic polynomial. The 'k' factor means there can be infinitely many such polynomials, all scalar multiples of each other.

Division Algorithm for Polynomials

  • If P(x) and G(x) are two polynomials with G(x) ≠ 0,
  • Then we can find Q(x) (quotient) and R(x) (remainder) such that:
  • P(x) = G(x) ⋅ Q(x) + R(x), where R(x) = 0 or degree of R(x) < degree of G(x).

🎙️ This is similar to the division algorithm for integers (Dividend = Divisor × Quotient + Remainder). It allows us to divide one polynomial by another, which is a key skill for finding factors and zeros.

Worked Example: Polynomial Long Division

  • Divide P(x) = x² + 3x + 2 by G(x) = x + 1.
  • Step 1: Divide first terms (x²/x = x).
  • Step 2: Multiply quotient (x) by divisor (x+1) = x² + x.
  • Step 3: Subtract (x² + 3x + 2) - (x² + x) = 2x + 2.
  • Step 4: Bring down next term (if any), repeat. (2x/x = 2).
  • Result: Quotient Q(x) = x + 2, Remainder R(x) = 0.

🎙️ Polynomial long division can seem daunting at first, but it follows a systematic process. Practice is key to mastering it. Notice that if the remainder is zero, the divisor is a factor of the dividend.

1Divide leading terms
2Multiply quotient by divisor
3Subtract
4Repeat

Common Mistakes in Polynomials

  • Sign Errors: Forgetting to change signs during subtraction in division.
  • Incorrect Degree: Misidentifying the highest power of the variable.
  • Confusing Terms: Mixing up coefficients, variables, and constants.
  • Calculation Errors: Simple arithmetic mistakes in formulas.

🎙️ Beware of these common pitfalls! A small error in a sign or a miscalculation can lead to a completely wrong answer. Always double-check your steps, especially during polynomial division and when applying formulas.

Measures of Central Tendency

  • Single values that describe the 'center' of a dataset.
  • Mean, Median, and Mode are the main measures.
  • Each measure has a different way of defining the 'center'.

🎙️ These measures help us summarize a whole dataset with just one number. They give us an idea of what a 'typical' value in the dataset might be, but each does it in its own unique way.

Concept: Mean (Average)

  • The most common measure of central tendency.
  • Calculated by summing all values and dividing by the count.
  • Formula for ungrouped data: Σx / n.

🎙️ The mean is what you usually think of as the 'average'. It's like sharing everything equally among all members. It's sensitive to extreme values, also known as outliers.

Real-Life Example: Mean

  • Finding the average daily temperature for a week.
  • Calculating the average score in a cricket match.
  • Determining the average height of students in a class.

🎙️ Imagine you want to know the average temperature in your city over the last week. You'd sum up all seven daily temperatures and divide by seven. This gives you a single representative value for the week.

Worked Example: Mean (Ungrouped Data)

  • Scores of 5 students: 10, 12, 8, 15, 10.
  • Sum of scores (Σx) = 10 + 12 + 8 + 15 + 10 = 55.
  • Number of students (n) = 5.
  • Mean = 55 / 5 = 11.

🎙️ Here, we simply add up all the individual scores and divide by the total number of scores. It's a straightforward calculation for ungrouped data, giving us the average score of 11.

1List all observations
2Calculate their sum (Σx)
3Count total observations (n)
4Divide Σx by n

Mean for Grouped Data: Direct Method

  • For grouped frequency distribution, we use class marks (midpoints).
  • Class Mark (xᵢ) = (Lower Limit + Upper Limit) / 2.
  • Formula: Mean (x̄) = Σ(fᵢxᵢ) / Σfᵢ.

🎙️ When data is grouped, we can't use individual values. Instead, we assume each value in a class interval is represented by its midpoint, called the class mark. We then take a weighted average using the frequencies.

➗
Weighted Average

Worked Example: Mean (Grouped - Direct Method)

  • Given classes and frequencies (fᵢ).
  • Calculate class marks (xᵢ) for each class.
  • Calculate fᵢxᵢ for each class.
  • Sum fᵢxᵢ and sum fᵢ, then divide.

🎙️ Let's say we have marks 0-10, 10-20. The class marks would be 5 and 15. We multiply these by their respective frequencies, sum them up, and then divide by the total frequency to get the mean.

1Find Class Marks (xᵢ)
2Calculate fᵢxᵢ
3Sum fᵢxᵢ and Σfᵢ
4Apply formula Σ(fᵢxᵢ) / Σfᵢ

Concept: Median

  • The middle value of a dataset when arranged in order.
  • Divides the data into two equal halves.
  • Less affected by extreme values than the mean.

🎙️ The median is literally the 'middle ground'. If you line up all your data points from smallest to largest, the median is the one right in the center. It's a good measure when there are very high or very low outliers.

↔️
Middle Value
16 / 25

Finding Median (Ungrouped Data)

  • Step 1: Arrange data in ascending or descending order.
  • Step 2: If 'n' (number of observations) is odd, Median = ((n+1)/2)th observation.
  • Step 3: If 'n' is even, Median = Average of (n/2)th and ((n/2)+1)th observations.

🎙️ The first and most crucial step for finding the median is to sort your data. Then, depending on whether you have an odd or even number of values, you'll either pick the exact middle value or average the two middle values.

1Order the data
2Count 'n'
3Apply odd/even rule
4Find the value(s)

Worked Example: Median (Ungrouped Data)

  • Data: 15, 12, 18, 10, 20. Ordered: 10, 12, 15, 18, 20. n=5 (odd).
  • Median = ((5+1)/2)th = 3rd observation = 15.
  • Data: 15, 12, 18, 10, 20, 16. Ordered: 10, 12, 15, 16, 18, 20. n=6 (even).
  • Median = Average of (6/2)th (3rd) and ((6/2)+1)th (4th) observations.
  • Median = (15 + 16) / 2 = 15.5.

🎙️ Here, we see two examples: one with an odd number of data points and one with an even number. Notice how crucial sorting the data is before applying the median formula.

1Sort the given data
2Identify 'n' (odd/even)
3Locate middle position(s)
4Find the Median value

Median for Grouped Data

  • Formula: Median = L + [((n/2) - cf) / f] × h.
  • L = lower limit of median class.
  • n = total frequency.
  • cf = cumulative frequency of class preceding median class.
  • f = frequency of median class.
  • h = class size.

🎙️ For grouped data, finding the median is a bit more involved. We first identify the 'median class' using cumulative frequency, and then use this formula to interpolate the exact median value within that class.

Interpolation

Worked Example: Median (Grouped Data)

  • Find n/2. Locate the median class where cf is just greater than n/2.
  • Identify L, cf, f, h for the median class.
  • Substitute values into the formula to calculate the median.

🎙️ This step-by-step process is crucial for grouped data. The cumulative frequency helps us pinpoint the class where the 'middle' value lies, and then the formula refines that location.

Concept: Mode

  • The value that appears most frequently in a dataset.
  • A dataset can have one mode (unimodal), multiple modes (multimodal), or no mode.
  • Useful for categorical data or identifying popular items.

🎙️ The mode is simply the 'most common' value. If you're looking for the most popular shoe size or the most frequently occurring mark in a test, the mode is your answer. It's the easiest to understand intuitively.

Finding Mode (Ungrouped Data)

  • Simply count the frequency of each observation.
  • The observation with the highest frequency is the mode.
  • Example: 2, 3, 4, 3, 5, 3, 2. The number 3 appears 3 times, which is the most. So, Mode = 3.

🎙️ For ungrouped data, finding the mode is straightforward. Just look for the value that repeats the most. If two values repeat with the same highest frequency, then you have two modes.

Mode for Grouped Data

  • Formula: Mode = L + [(f₁ - f₀) / (2f₁ - f₀ - f₂)] × h.
  • L = lower limit of the modal class.
  • h = size of the class interval.
  • f₁ = frequency of the modal class.
  • f₀ = frequency of the class preceding the modal class.
  • f₂ = frequency of the class succeeding the modal class.

🎙️ Similar to the median, for grouped data, we first identify the 'modal class' – the class with the highest frequency. Then, we use this specific formula to pinpoint the mode within that class, considering the frequencies of adjacent classes.

🏆
Peak Frequency

Worked Example: Mode (Grouped Data)

  • Determine L, h, f₁, f₀, f₂ from the modal class and surrounding classes.
  • Substitute these values into the mode formula.
  • Calculate the mode.

Recap: Mean, Median, Mode

  • Mean: The average, sum of values divided by count.
  • Median: The middle value when data is ordered.
  • Mode: The most frequently occurring value.
  • Each measure provides a unique perspective on the 'center' of data.

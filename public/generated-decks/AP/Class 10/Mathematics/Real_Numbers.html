Euclid's Division Lemma

  • Given positive integers a and b, there exist unique integers q and r.
  • Such that a = bq + r, where 0 ≤ r < b.
  • Helps in finding HCF of two positive integers.

🎙️ Euclid's Division Lemma is a fundamental concept. It simply states that when you divide one positive integer 'a' by another positive integer 'b', you'll always get a unique quotient 'q' and a remainder 'r'. The remainder will always be less than the divisor 'b'. This lemma is the basis for the Euclidean Algorithm.

Example: Using Euclid's Division Lemma

  • Find HCF of 135 and 225.
  • Step 1: 225 = 135 × 1 + 90
  • Step 2: 135 = 90 × 1 + 45
  • Step 3: 90 = 45 × 2 + 0. HCF is 45.

🎙️ Let's see this in action to find the HCF of 135 and 225. We repeatedly apply the lemma. We divide 225 by 135, get a remainder of 90. Then we divide 135 by 90, get 45. Finally, we divide 90 by 45, getting a remainder of 0. The divisor at this stage, 45, is our HCF.

Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic

  • Every composite number can be expressed as a product of primes.
  • This factorization is unique, apart from the order of factors.
  • Helps in finding HCF and LCM of numbers.

🎙️ This is a very powerful theorem! It says that any composite number can be broken down into a unique set of prime numbers multiplied together. Think of it like a prime 'fingerprint' for every number. The order in which you write the prime factors doesn't matter, but the factors themselves are always the same.

Example: Prime Factorization using FTA

  • Factorize 360 into prime factors.
  • Step 1: 360 = 2 × 180
  • Step 2: 180 = 2 × 90
  • Step 3: 90 = 2 × 45
  • Step 4: 45 = 3 × 15
  • Step 5: 15 = 3 × 5. So, 360 = 2³ × 3² × 5.

🎙️ Let's apply the FTA to factorize 360. We keep dividing by the smallest prime factors until we can't divide anymore. We find that 360 is made up of three 2s, two 3s, and one 5 multiplied together. This unique combination gives us 360.

HCF & LCM using Prime Factorization

  • HCF: Product of the smallest power of each common prime factor.
  • LCM: Product of the greatest power of each prime factor (common or not).
  • FTA simplifies finding HCF and LCM for larger numbers.

🎙️ The Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic makes finding HCF and LCM much easier. For HCF, we look for prime factors that are common to both numbers and take the lowest power. For LCM, we take all prime factors from both numbers, using their highest powers.

Example: HCF & LCM of 12 and 18

  • Prime factors of 12 = 2² × 3¹
  • Prime factors of 18 = 2¹ × 3²
  • HCF(12, 18) = 2¹ × 3¹ = 6 (common, lowest power)
  • LCM(12, 18) = 2² × 3² = 4 × 9 = 36 (all, highest power)

🎙️ Let's find the HCF and LCM of 12 and 18. First, we find their prime factorizations. Then, for HCF, we pick the common primes (2 and 3) with their smallest powers. For LCM, we pick all primes (2 and 3) with their largest powers. This method is systematic and always works.

HCF, LCM, and Product of Numbers

  • For any two positive integers a and b:
  • HCF(a, b) × LCM(a, b) = a × b
  • This relationship is useful for verifying calculations or finding one value if others are known.

🎙️ There's a beautiful relationship between the HCF, LCM, and the numbers themselves. For any two positive integers, if you multiply their HCF and LCM, you will get the same result as multiplying the two numbers themselves. This is a handy formula to remember and use.

Proving Irrationality (Brief)

  • Method of 'Proof by Contradiction'.
  • Assume the number is rational (p/q form).
  • Derive a contradiction to the initial assumption.
  • Commonly used for √2, √3, √5, etc.

🎙️ How do we *know* a number is irrational? We use a method called 'proof by contradiction'. We start by assuming the opposite – that the number *is* rational. Then, through logical steps, we show that this assumption leads to something impossible or false. This means our initial assumption must have been wrong, and the number is indeed irrational.

Example: Proving √2 is Irrational

  • Assume √2 = a/b (a, b are coprime integers).
  • Squaring both sides: 2 = a²/b² => a² = 2b².
  • This means a² is even, so a must be even (a=2c).
  • Substitute a=2c: (2c)² = 2b² => 4c² = 2b² => 2c² = b².
  • This means b² is even, so b must be even. Contradiction! (a and b are coprime).
  • Therefore, √2 is irrational.

🎙️ This is a classic proof. If √2 were rational, we could write it as a fraction a/b where a and b have no common factors. But through algebraic manipulation, we find that both 'a' and 'b' must be even, meaning they *do* have a common factor of 2. This contradicts our initial assumption, proving √2 is irrational.

2a² = 2b² (a is even)
3b² = 2c² (b is even)
4Contradiction (a,b coprime)

Terminating & Non-terminating Decimals

  • Terminating: Decimal expansion ends (e.g., 1/4 = 0.25).
  • Non-terminating: Decimal expansion goes on forever.
  • Repeating (Rational): Non-terminating with a repeating block (e.g., 1/3 = 0.333...).
  • Non-repeating (Irrational): Non-terminating with no repeating pattern (e.g., √2, π).

🎙️ Rational numbers can have two types of decimal expansions: terminating (they end) or non-terminating but repeating (a block of digits repeats). Irrational numbers, on the other hand, have non-terminating and non-repeating decimal expansions. This is a key way to distinguish them.

Identifying Decimal Expansions of Rational Numbers

  • A rational number p/q (in simplest form) has a terminating decimal expansion if the prime factorization of q is of the form 2ⁿ5ᵐ, where n, m are non-negative integers.
  • Otherwise, it has a non-terminating repeating decimal expansion.
  • Example: 3/8 = 3/(2³) (terminating). 1/7 (non-terminating repeating).

🎙️ There's a neat trick to tell if a rational number's decimal expansion will terminate without actually dividing! Just look at the prime factors of its denominator (after simplifying the fraction). If the denominator only has prime factors of 2 or 5, it will terminate. Otherwise, it will repeat.

Real Numbers: Key Takeaways

  • Real Numbers = Rational + Irrational.
  • Rational numbers can be p/q, decimals terminate or repeat.
  • Irrational numbers are non-terminating, non-repeating decimals.
  • Euclid's Lemma: a = bq + r (for HCF).

What are Rational Numbers?

  • Numbers that can be expressed in the form p/q.
  • Here, 'p' and 'q' are integers, and 'q' is not zero (q ≠ 0).
  • Examples: 1/2, -3/4, 5 (which is 5/1), 0.75 (which is 3/4).

🎙️ The key is the p/q form. Any number you can write as a simple fraction, where both the top and bottom are whole numbers (and the bottom isn't zero), is a rational number. This includes all integers too, since 5 can be written as 5/1.

Examples of Rational Numbers

🎙️ These examples show the wide variety of numbers that fall under the 'rational' umbrella. From simple counting numbers to complex repeating decimals, if it can be a fraction, it's rational.

Rational Numbers & Decimals

  • Rational numbers have two types of decimal expansions:
  • 1. Terminating: The decimal expansion ends after a finite number of digits (e.g., 0.5, 0.25).
  • 2. Non-terminating Repeating: The decimal expansion goes on forever, but a block of digits repeats periodically (e.g., 0.333..., 0.141414...).

🎙️ This is a key property to identify rational numbers from their decimal form. If it stops or repeats, it's rational. You can always convert these decimals back into a p/q fraction.

Sharing Sweets: Euclid's Division Lemma

  • Imagine you have 17 sweets and want to share them among 3 friends.
  • Each friend gets 5 sweets (3 × 5 = 15).
  • You are left with 2 sweets (17 - 15 = 2).
  • So, 17 = 3 × 5 + 2.

🎙️ This simple act of sharing sweets perfectly illustrates Euclid's Division Lemma. It's something you've been doing since childhood, just now we're putting a formal name to it in mathematics.

What is Euclid's Division Lemma?

  • Given two positive integers 'a' and 'b', there exist unique integers 'q' and 'r'.
  • Such that a = bq + r, where 0 ≤ r < b.
  • 'a' is the dividend, 'b' is the divisor, 'q' is the quotient, and 'r' is the remainder.

🎙️ This lemma is a fundamental result. It basically states that when you divide one positive integer by another, you'll always get a unique quotient and a unique remainder, and the remainder will always be smaller than the divisor.

➗
Dividend = Divisor × Quotient + Remainder

Finding HCF using Euclid's Division Algorithm (EDA)

  • Euclid's Division Algorithm is a technique to compute the HCF (Highest Common Factor) of two positive integers.
  • It's based on Euclid's Division Lemma.
  • The HCF of two numbers is the largest number that divides both of them exactly.

🎙️ The Euclidean Algorithm is one of the oldest algorithms still in common use! It's an efficient way to find the HCF of two numbers without needing to list all their factors. It's a series of steps using the lemma.

🔍
Searching for the highest common factor
21 / 29

Steps for Euclid's Division Algorithm

  • Step 1: Apply EDL to 'c' and 'd' (where c > d) to find q and r, such that c = dq + r, 0 ≤ r < d.
  • Step 2: If r = 0, then 'd' is the HCF of 'c' and 'd'.
  • Step 3: If r ≠ 0, then apply the Division Lemma to 'd' and 'r'.
  • Step 4: Continue the process until the remainder is zero. The divisor at this stage will be the HCF.

🎙️ This sequence of steps ensures we systematically reduce the numbers until we find their HCF. Each step uses the remainder from the previous step as the new divisor, and the old divisor as the new dividend.

1Apply EDL (a=bq+r)
2If r=0, b is HCF
3If r≠0, apply EDL to b and r
4Repeat until r=0
22 / 29

EDA in Action: Finding HCF of 135 and 225

  • Step 1: 225 = 135 × 1 + 90 (r ≠ 0)
  • Step 2: 135 = 90 × 1 + 45 (r ≠ 0)
  • Step 3: 90 = 45 × 2 + 0 (r = 0)
  • Since the remainder is 0, the divisor at this stage, 45, is the HCF.

🎙️ Here's a worked example. Notice how the remainder (90) becomes the new divisor, and the previous divisor (135) becomes the new dividend. We continue this until we get a zero remainder. The last non-zero divisor is our HCF.

Building Blocks of Numbers: Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic (FTA)

  • Think of prime numbers (2, 3, 5, 7...) as the 'atoms' of all other numbers.
  • Every composite number can be uniquely built from these prime atoms.
  • Just like a building is made of unique bricks.

🎙️ The Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic is like a super-power for understanding numbers. It tells us that every number can be broken down into a unique set of prime numbers, like a unique genetic code for each number.

🧱

The Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic

  • Every composite number can be expressed (factorised) as a product of primes.
  • This factorization is unique, apart from the order in which the prime factors occur.
  • Example: 12 = 2 × 2 × 3 = 2² × 3.

🎙️ This theorem is incredibly powerful. It means that no matter how you factorise a composite number, you'll always end up with the same set of prime factors. The order might change, but the primes themselves won't.

⭐
Unique prime factorization

Using FTA for HCF and LCM

  • HCF: Product of the smallest power of each common prime factor.
  • LCM: Product of the highest power of each prime factor (common or not).
  • Remember the relationship: HCF(a, b) × LCM(a, b) = a × b.

🎙️ The FTA makes finding HCF and LCM much easier, especially for larger numbers. By breaking numbers down into their prime factors, you can systematically identify the common and unique factors to calculate HCF and LCM. The relationship between HCF, LCM, and the product of the numbers is also very useful!

Watch Out! Common Mistakes

  • Confusing rational and irrational numbers (e.g., thinking √4 is irrational).
  • Incorrectly applying Euclid's Division Lemma (e.g., remainder greater than divisor).
  • Not finding all prime factors when using FTA for HCF/LCM.
  • Forgetting that 0 is a whole number, but not a natural number.

🎙️ Many students make these errors, so be extra careful! Always double-check your definitions and calculations. A small mistake in identifying a number type or applying a rule can lead to a completely wrong answer.

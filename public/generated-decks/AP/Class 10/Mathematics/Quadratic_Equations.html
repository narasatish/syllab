Recap: What are Polynomials?

  • An expression with variables and coefficients, involving only non-negative integer powers of variables.
  • Linear Polynomial: Highest power of variable is 1 (e.g., 2x + 3).
  • Quadratic Polynomial: Highest power of variable is 2 (e.g., 2x² + 3x + 5).

🎙️ Before we jump into quadratic equations, let's quickly refresh our memory on polynomials. Remember, a polynomial is an expression with terms like x, x², x³, etc. When the highest power of 'x' is 1, it's a linear polynomial, like a straight line graph. But when the highest power is 2, it's a quadratic polynomial, which gives us those beautiful curves!

What is a Quadratic Equation?

  • An equation of the form ax² + bx + c = 0.
  • Here, 'a', 'b', and 'c' are real numbers.
  • The crucial condition: 'a' cannot be zero (a ≠ 0).

🎙️ A quadratic equation is simply a quadratic polynomial set equal to zero. The standard form is ax² + bx + c = 0. The most important thing to remember is that the coefficient 'a' can never be zero. If 'a' were zero, the x² term would vanish, and it would become a linear equation, not a quadratic one!

Identifying Quadratic Equations: Examples!

  • Example 1: x² + 5x + 6 = 0 (a=1, b=5, c=6)
  • Example 2: 2x² - 3x = 0 (a=2, b=-3, c=0)
  • Example 3: (x+1)² = x - 3 (Simplify first: x² + 2x + 1 = x - 3 => x² + x + 4 = 0)

🎙️ Let's look at some examples. In the first one, all terms are present. In the second, 'c' is zero, which is perfectly fine – it's still a quadratic. The third example shows that sometimes you need to expand and simplify the equation first to see if it fits the standard form. Always check for the x² term!

Real-World Example: The Rectangular Plot

  • A rectangular plot has an area of 528 m².
  • The length is one more than twice its breadth.
  • Let breadth = x meters. Then length = (2x + 1) meters.
  • Area = Length × Breadth => x(2x + 1) = 528 => 2x² + x - 528 = 0. This is a Quadratic Equation!

🎙️ Here's a practical problem: imagine you're planning to buy a rectangular plot of land. You know its area and a relationship between its length and breadth. By setting up an equation based on the given information, we end up with a quadratic equation. Solving this equation will tell us the exact dimensions of the plot!

What are the Roots of a Quadratic Equation?

  • The 'roots' or 'solutions' are the values of 'x' that satisfy the equation.
  • When you substitute a root into the equation, the Left Hand Side (LHS) equals the Right Hand Side (RHS), which is zero.
  • A quadratic equation can have at most two roots.

🎙️ Just like a linear equation has one solution, a quadratic equation can have up to two solutions, or 'roots'. These roots are the specific values of 'x' that make the entire equation true, meaning when you plug them in, the equation balances out to zero. Think of them as the 'targets' we are trying to find.

How to Check if a Value is a Root?

  • Simply substitute the given value of 'x' into the equation.
  • If the Left Hand Side (LHS) becomes zero, then it's a root.
  • If LHS is not zero, it's not a root.

🎙️ Checking if a value is a root is straightforward. Take the given value, substitute it wherever 'x' appears in your quadratic equation, and then calculate the result. If the result is zero, congratulations, you've found a root! If not, then that value is not a root of that particular equation.

Method 1: Solving by Factorization (Splitting the Middle Term)

  • This method involves breaking down the quadratic polynomial into a product of two linear factors.
  • Analogy: Like breaking a big number (e.g., 12) into its prime factors (2x2x3).
  • If (x - α)(x - β) = 0, then x = α or x = β are the roots.

🎙️ Our first method to find the roots is factorization, also known as splitting the middle term. It's like taking a complex puzzle and breaking it into smaller, easier pieces. We want to rewrite the quadratic expression as a product of two simpler linear expressions. Once we have these factors, finding the roots becomes very simple.

Linking Factors

Steps for Factorization Method

  • Step 1: Write the equation in standard form ax² + bx + c = 0.
  • Step 2: Find two numbers 'p' and 'q' such that p + q = b and p × q = ac.
  • Step 3: Split the middle term 'bx' as 'px + qx'.
  • Step 4: Group the terms and factor out common monomials.
  • Step 5: Set each linear factor equal to zero and solve for 'x'.

🎙️ Here are the step-by-step instructions for factorization. The trickiest part is usually finding those two numbers 'p' and 'q' that satisfy the conditions. Once you master that, the rest is just algebraic manipulation: grouping terms and then setting each resulting factor to zero to find your roots.

1Standard Form
2Find p, q
3Split middle term
4Group & Factor
5Solve factors

Worked Example: Factorization

  • Solve: x² - 5x + 6 = 0
  • Here, a=1, b=-5, c=6. We need p+q = -5 and pq = 1*6 = 6.
  • Numbers are -2 and -3. (-2 + -3 = -5, -2 * -3 = 6).
  • x² - 2x - 3x + 6 = 0
  • x(x - 2) - 3(x - 2) = 0
  • (x - 2)(x - 3) = 0
  • So, x - 2 = 0 or x - 3 = 0. Therefore, x = 2 or x = 3.

🎙️ Let's walk through an example. For x² - 5x + 6 = 0, we need two numbers that add up to -5 and multiply to 6. These numbers are -2 and -3. We then replace -5x with -2x - 3x, group the terms, and factor out common terms. This leads us to the factors (x-2) and (x-3), giving us the roots x=2 and x=3.

Method 2: Completing the Square

  • This method transforms the quadratic equation into a perfect square trinomial.
  • Analogy: Like trying to make a perfectly square garden plot from an irregular shape.
  • This allows us to take the square root on both sides to solve for 'x'.

🎙️ Completing the square is another powerful method. The core idea is to manipulate the equation algebraically so that one side becomes a perfect square, like (x+k)². This makes it easy to isolate 'x' by taking the square root of both sides. It's like carefully adding a piece to a shape to make it a perfect square.

Steps for Completing the Square

  • Step 1: Divide the entire equation by 'a' (if a ≠ 1).
  • Step 2: Move the constant term 'c' to the RHS.
  • Step 3: Add (b/2a)² to both sides to complete the square on the LHS.
  • Step 4: Write the LHS as a perfect square (x + b/2a)².
  • Step 5: Take the square root on both sides and solve for 'x'.

🎙️ These are the steps to complete the square. The crucial step is adding (b/2a)² to both sides. This specific term ensures that the left side becomes a perfect square trinomial. Remember, whatever you add to one side, you must add to the other to keep the equation balanced.

1Divide by 'a'
2Move 'c' to RHS
3Add (b/2a)²
4Form (x+k)²
5Take square root

Worked Example: Completing the Square

  • Solve: x² + 6x + 5 = 0
  • Move constant: x² + 6x = -5
  • Add (6/2)² = 3² = 9 to both sides: x² + 6x + 9 = -5 + 9
  • Factor LHS: (x + 3)² = 4
  • Take square root: x + 3 = ±√4
  • x + 3 = ±2
  • So, x + 3 = 2 (x = -1) or x + 3 = -2 (x = -5).

🎙️ Let's solve x² + 6x + 5 = 0 using this method. First, move the 5 to the right. Then, we calculate (b/2a)² which is (6/2)² = 9, and add it to both sides. This transforms the left side into (x+3)², allowing us to take the square root. Don't forget the ± sign when taking the square root, as it leads to two possible solutions!

Method 3: The Quadratic Formula

  • The most reliable method for solving any quadratic equation.
  • Derived directly from the 'completing the square' method.
  • Formula: x = [-b ± √(b² - 4ac)] / 2a

🎙️ The quadratic formula is a universal tool. It works for *any* quadratic equation, even when factorization is difficult or completing the square seems tedious. This formula is derived by applying the completing the square method to the general form ax² + bx + c = 0. It's a formula you must memorize!

Why the Quadratic Formula is Important

  • Always works, even when factorization is not straightforward.
  • Provides a direct way to find roots without intermediate steps.
  • Essential for understanding the 'nature of roots' (using the discriminant part).

🎙️ Why bother with the formula when we have other methods? Because not all quadratic equations can be easily factored, especially if the roots are irrational or complex. The quadratic formula is a guaranteed solution. Plus, a crucial part of the formula, the term under the square root, tells us a lot about the nature of the roots even before we calculate them.

Worked Example: Quadratic Formula

  • Solve: 2x² + 3x - 2 = 0
  • Here, a=2, b=3, c=-2.
  • Substitute into formula: x = [-3 ± √(3² - 4*2*(-2))] / (2*2)
  • x = [-3 ± √(9 + 16)] / 4
  • x = [-3 ± √25] / 4
  • x = [-3 ± 5] / 4
  • So, x = (-3 + 5) / 4 = 2/4 = 1/2 or x = (-3 - 5) / 4 = -8/4 = -2.

🎙️ Let's apply the formula to 2x² + 3x - 2 = 0. Carefully identify a, b, and c, then substitute them into the formula. Pay close attention to the signs, especially when calculating b² - 4ac. Simplify step by step. This example shows how quickly you can get the roots using the formula.

Nature of Roots: The Discriminant (D)

  • The term (b² - 4ac) inside the square root in the quadratic formula is called the 'Discriminant' (D).
  • D = b² - 4ac
  • The value of D tells us about the type of roots a quadratic equation has, without actually solving it completely.

🎙️ Now, let's talk about the 'nature of roots'. The discriminant, D = b² - 4ac, is like a fortune teller for quadratic equations! Its value immediately tells us whether the roots are real, equal, or not real at all, without having to complete the entire quadratic formula calculation. It's a very powerful concept.

Cases for the Nature of Roots

  • Case 1: If D > 0 (positive), the equation has two distinct real roots.
  • Case 2: If D = 0, the equation has two equal real roots (or one repeated root).
  • Case 3: If D < 0 (negative), the equation has no real roots (roots are complex).

🎙️ Here are the three main cases for the discriminant. If D is positive, you'll get two different real numbers as roots. If D is zero, both roots will be the same real number. And if D is negative, then taking the square root of a negative number means there are no real solutions, only complex ones, which you'll explore in higher classes.

D > 0 (Distinct Real)
D = 0 (Equal Real)
D < 0 (No Real)

Real-World Example: Is it Possible?

  • Is it possible to design a rectangular park of perimeter 80m and area 400m²?
  • Let length = l, breadth = b. 2(l+b) = 80 => l+b = 40 => l = 40-b.
  • Area = lb = 400 => (40-b)b = 400 => 40b - b² = 400 => b² - 40b + 400 = 0.
  • Calculate D = b² - 4ac = (-40)² - 4(1)(400) = 1600 - 1600 = 0.
  • Since D = 0, yes, it's possible! (b=20, l=20, it's a square park).

🎙️ Sometimes, before finding the exact solution, we just need to know if a solution even exists. For this park problem, we form a quadratic equation. Calculating the discriminant D, we find it's zero. This means it's indeed possible to design such a park, and it will have unique dimensions, in this case, a square park.

Common Mistakes in Quadratic Equations

  • Sign Errors: Often made when substituting values into the quadratic formula or while expanding expressions.
  • Forgetting 'a ≠ 0': Not checking if the coefficient of x² is zero, which would make it a linear equation.
  • Calculation Errors: Especially with squares and products under the square root in the discriminant.
  • Ignoring ±: Forgetting the plus/minus sign when taking the square root, leading to only one root instead of two.

🎙️ It's easy to make small errors, but being aware of common pitfalls helps. Always double-check your signs, especially when 'b' or 'c' are negative. Remember that 'a' cannot be zero. And when you take a square root, always consider both the positive and negative possibilities, as this gives you both roots.

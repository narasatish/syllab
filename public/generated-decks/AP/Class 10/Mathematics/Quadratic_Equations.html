Standard Form of a Quadratic Equation

  • The general form is ax² + bx + c = 0.
  • 'a', 'b', and 'c' are real numbers.
  • 'a' cannot be zero (a ≠ 0).

🎙️ This is the most important form to remember! Every quadratic equation can be written in this standard form. The condition 'a ≠ 0' is crucial, because if 'a' were zero, it would no longer be a quadratic equation.

1a (coefficient of x²)
2b (coefficient of x)
3c (constant term)

Why is it called 'Quadratic'?

  • Comes from the Latin word 'quadratus'.
  • 'Quadratus' means square.
  • Refers to the 'x²' term (x squared).

🎙️ The name 'quadratic' might seem a bit odd at first, as 'quad' often means four. However, it actually comes from the idea of squaring, as in finding the area of a square. So, 'x-squared' is the key!

Is it a Quadratic Equation?

  • Check if the highest power of 'x' is 2 after simplification.
  • Example: (x+1)² = x - 3
  • Expand: x² + 2x + 1 = x - 3 => x² + x + 4 = 0.

🎙️ To determine if an equation is quadratic, you must first simplify it completely. Expand any brackets and move all terms to one side. Then, look for the highest power of the variable. If it's 2, then it's quadratic!

Method 1: Factorisation

  • Also called 'Splitting the Middle Term'.
  • Break 'bx' into two terms whose sum is 'bx' and product is 'acx²'.
  • Group terms and factor out common factors.

🎙️ Factorisation is a very elegant method, especially for equations with integer roots. The trick is to split the middle term 'bx' into two parts that help us factor the entire expression into two linear factors.

✂️
Splitting Apart
16 / 25

Worked Example 2: Factorisation

  • Solve: x² - 5x + 6 = 0
  • Split -5x into -2x and -3x.
  • x(x-2) - 3(x-2) = 0 => (x-2)(x-3) = 0.
  • Roots are x = 2 and x = 3.

🎙️ Here’s a step-by-step example of factorisation. Notice how splitting the middle term allows us to group terms and find common factors, ultimately leading to two simple linear equations whose solutions are our roots.

1x² - 2x - 3x + 6 = 0
2x(x-2) - 3(x-2) = 0
3(x-2)(x-3) = 0
4x = 2 or x = 3

Method 2: Completing the Square

  • Transform the equation into a perfect square trinomial.
  • Allows us to take the square root of both sides.
  • Useful when factorisation is difficult.

🎙️ Completing the square is a powerful algebraic technique. The goal is to manipulate the equation so that one side becomes a perfect square, like (x+k)². This makes it much easier to solve for 'x' by taking square roots.

Worked Example 3: Completing the Square

  • Solve: x² + 4x - 5 = 0
  • Move constant: x² + 4x = 5.
  • Add (4/2)² = 4 to both sides: x² + 4x + 4 = 5 + 4.
  • (x+2)² = 9 => x+2 = ±3.
  • Roots are x = 1 and x = -5.

🎙️ Here's how we apply completing the square. The key step is adding (b/2)² to both sides to create that perfect square. After that, it's a matter of taking the square root and solving two linear equations.

1x² + 4x = 5
2x² + 4x + 4 = 9
3(x+2)² = 9
4x+2 = ±3
5x = 1, x = -5

Method 3: The Quadratic Formula!

  • A universal formula to find roots.
  • Works for ALL quadratic equations.
  • x = [-b ± √(b² - 4ac)] / 2a.

🎙️ This is perhaps the most famous and useful formula in quadratic equations! The quadratic formula always works, no matter how complex the numbers are. You just need to correctly identify 'a', 'b', and 'c' and plug them in.

✨
Magic Formula

Worked Example 4: Quadratic Formula

  • Solve: 2x² + 5x - 3 = 0
  • Identify a=2, b=5, c=-3.
  • Substitute into formula: x = [-5 ± √(5² - 4(2)(-3))] / 2(2).
  • Simplify: x = [-5 ± √(25 + 24)] / 4 = [-5 ± √49] / 4.
  • x = [-5 ± 7] / 4. Roots are x = 1/2 and x = -3.

🎙️ Let's apply the quadratic formula step-by-step. Careful substitution and simplification are key here, especially with the square root part. The '±' sign means we'll get two potential roots.

1a=2, b=5, c=-3
2x = [-5 ± √(25 - 4(2)(-3))] / 4
3x = [-5 ± √49] / 4
4x = [-5 ± 7] / 4
5x = 1/2 or x = -3

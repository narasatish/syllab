Line of Sight: Looking at a Kite

  • Imagine you are flying a kite.
  • The line from your eyes to the kite is an imaginary line.
  • This is our 'Line of Sight'.

🎙️ When you look at an object, your eyes follow a straight path to that object. This imaginary straight line connecting your eye to the object you are observing is what we call the Line of Sight.

Defining the Line of Sight

  • The line drawn from the eye of an observer to the object observed.
  • It's always a straight line.
  • Forms the hypotenuse of our right-angled triangle.

🎙️ So, whether you're looking up, down, or straight ahead, the path your vision takes to reach an object is the Line of Sight. This concept is fundamental to understanding angles of elevation and depression.

Angle of Elevation: Looking Up!

  • Imagine looking up at a mountain peak.
  • Your head tilts upwards from a horizontal position.
  • This upward tilt forms an angle.

🎙️ When you observe an object that is above your horizontal eye level, you naturally have to look upwards. The angle formed by your line of sight with the horizontal line in this scenario is called the Angle of Elevation.

⛰️
Looking at a mountain

Defining Angle of Elevation

  • The angle formed by the Line of Sight with the horizontal line.
  • When the object is ABOVE the horizontal level.
  • Always measured from the observer's eye level.

🎙️ Crucially, the horizontal line is an imaginary line parallel to the ground, passing through the observer's eye. The angle of elevation is always measured upwards from this horizontal line to the line of sight.

Worked Example 2: Lighthouse - Step 2

The Horizontal Line: Your Reference

  • It's an imaginary line parallel to the ground.
  • Always passes through the observer's eye.
  • The starting point for measuring both elevation and depression angles.

🎙️ The horizontal line is the most important reference line in these problems. Always draw it first from the observer's eye. It helps you correctly identify and measure both angles of elevation and depression.

Elevation vs. Depression: A Key Relation

  • Angle of Elevation from A to B is equal to the Angle of Depression from B to A.
  • These are Alternate Interior Angles.
  • This property simplifies many problems.

🎙️ When two horizontal lines are parallel (ground and eye-level horizontal), and the line of sight acts as a transversal, the angle of elevation from one point to another is equal to the angle of depression from the second point back to the first. This is a powerful shortcut!

Worked Example 3: Observer - Step 2

  • For the first triangle (angle 30°): Tan 30° = h / (x + 20).
  • For the second triangle (angle 60°): Tan 60° = h / x.
  • We now have two equations with two unknowns (h and x).

🎙️ Using the tangent ratio for both triangles, we get two separate equations. Remember your standard trigonometric values for 30 and 60 degrees. This system of equations will help us find both 'h' and 'x'.

Worked Example 3: Observer - Step 3

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Not drawing a clear diagram.
  • Confusing Angle of Elevation with Angle of Depression.
  • Incorrectly identifying the horizontal line.
  • Using the wrong trigonometric ratio or value.

🎙️ Many students make these common errors. Always take your time to draw and label the diagram correctly, and double-check which angle you're working with. Practice helps in avoiding these pitfalls.

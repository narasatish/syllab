Rational Numbers (Q): Fractions and Decimals

  • Numbers that can be expressed in the form p/q
  • Where p and q are integers, and q is not zero
  • Includes terminating and repeating decimals

🎙️ Rational numbers are a big step! They include all integers, but also fractions and decimals that either end (terminate) or repeat a pattern. The key is that they can always be written as a ratio of two integers.

Locating Rational Numbers on the Number Line

  • Divide the unit length between integers into equal parts
  • The number of parts is determined by the denominator
  • Mark the point corresponding to the numerator

🎙️ Placing rational numbers on a number line requires a simple method. First, you identify the two integers between which the fraction lies. Then, you divide that segment into equal parts based on the denominator of your fraction.

🎙️ Let's try an example. To represent 3/4, we know it's between 0 and 1. We divide the space between 0 and 1 into four equal segments. Then, we count three segments from zero and mark that point as 3/4.

Irrational Numbers: Never-ending, Non-repeating

  • Numbers that CANNOT be expressed in the form p/q
  • Their decimal expansion is non-terminating and non-repeating
  • Examples: √2, √3, π (Pi)

🎙️ Now we come to irrational numbers, which are quite fascinating. Unlike rational numbers, they cannot be written as a simple fraction. Their decimal forms go on forever without any repeating pattern, making them truly unique.

Infinite Decimals

🎙️ The most famous irrational number is Pi (π), which is essential for calculating the circumference or area of any circle. You can never write down the exact value of Pi as a simple fraction, it's always an approximation in decimal form.

Every Point on the Number Line is a Real Number

Laws of Exponents for Real Numbers

  • Rules to simplify expressions with powers
  • Example: a^m * a^n = a^(m+n)
  • Helps in handling very large or very small numbers

🎙️ Exponents are a powerful tool in mathematics, allowing us to express repeated multiplication concisely. There are specific laws that govern how we combine or simplify terms with exponents, which are very useful in algebra and beyond.

📈
Powers and Growth

Worked Example: Exponents

  • Simplify: (2^3) * (2^4)
  • Using the law a^m * a^n = a^(m+n)
  • Result: 2^(3+4) = 2^7 = 128

🎙️ Let's apply one of the exponent laws. When multiplying powers with the same base, we add their exponents. So, 2 to the power of 3 multiplied by 2 to the power of 4 becomes 2 to the power of (3+4), which is 2 to the power of 7, or 128.

Rationalizing the Denominator

  • Process of converting an irrational denominator to a rational number
  • Achieved by multiplying the numerator and denominator by a suitable factor
  • Makes expressions easier to work with

🎙️ Rationalizing the denominator is a common technique used to simplify expressions involving square roots or other irrational numbers in the denominator. It makes calculations cleaner and easier to manage, as we prefer to have rational numbers in the denominator.

↔️
Making it Rational
22 / 25

Worked Example: Rationalize 1/√2

  • Multiply numerator and denominator by √2
  • (1 * √2) / (√2 * √2)
  • Result: √2 / 2

🎙️ To rationalize 1/√2, we multiply both the numerator and the denominator by √2. This makes the denominator (√2 * √2) equal to 2, which is a rational number. The expression then simplifies to √2/2, which is much easier to use.

What is a Point?

  • A point is a location in space.
  • It has no size, no width, no depth.
  • Represented by a dot and named with a capital letter (e.g., Point A).

🎙️ Imagine the tip of a sharpened pencil or a tiny speck of dust. That's how we visualize a point. It's just a position, a mark that shows 'here'.

📍
A specific location

What is a Line Segment?

  • A part of a line with two distinct endpoints.
  • It has a definite length.
  • Named by its two endpoints (e.g., Segment AB).

🎙️ Think of a stretched thread between two pins. That's a line segment. It starts at one point and ends at another, and you can measure its length.

➖
Part of a line with ends

What is a Ray?

  • A part of a line that has one endpoint and extends infinitely in one direction.
  • It does not have a definite length.
  • Named by its endpoint first, then another point on the ray (e.g., Ray AB).

🎙️ A ray is like a beam of light from a flashlight. It starts from the flashlight (endpoint) and travels endlessly in one direction. We cannot measure its length.

➡️
One start, goes forever

What is a Line?

  • A collection of points extending infinitely in both directions.
  • It has no endpoints and no definite length.
  • Represented by arrows on both ends and named by two points on it (e.g., Line AB) or a lowercase letter (e.g., Line 'l').

🎙️ Imagine extending a line segment endlessly in both directions. That's a line. It has no beginning and no end, so we can't measure its length.

↔️
Goes on forever both ways

Collinear Points

  • Three or more points that lie on the same straight line.
  • If points are not on the same line, they are non-collinear.
  • Example: Points A, B, C are collinear if they all lie on Line 'l'.

🎙️ If you can connect several points with a single straight ruler, then those points are collinear. Think of beads arranged perfectly straight on a string.

📏
Points on a straight line

Introduction to Angles

  • An angle is formed when two rays originate from the same endpoint.
  • The rays forming the angle are called arms or sides.
  • The common endpoint is called the vertex.

🎙️ Look at the corner of a room or the hands of a clock. These are examples of angles. They are formed when two lines or rays meet at a common point.

📐
Two rays meeting

Types of Angles: Acute Angle

  • An angle whose measure is between 0° and 90°.
  • It looks like a sharp corner.
  • Example: The angle formed by the hands of a clock at 1 o'clock (30°).

🎙️ Think of a small slice of pizza or the tip of a knife. Acute angles are 'cute' and small, less than 90 degrees.

🤏
Smaller than a right angle

Types of Angles: Right Angle

  • An angle whose measure is exactly 90°.
  • It forms a perfect square corner.
  • Example: The corner of a book or a wall.

🎙️ A right angle is very common in our daily lives. Look at the corner of your screen, or where two walls meet. It's a fundamental angle in construction and design.

🔲
A perfect square corner

Types of Angles: Obtuse Angle

  • An angle whose measure is between 90° and 180°.
  • It looks wider than a right angle.
  • Example: The angle formed by the hands of a clock at 4 o'clock (120°).

🎙️ If an angle is 'obtuse,' it means it's 'fat' or wide. Imagine opening a door more than 90 degrees; the angle formed is obtuse.

👐
Wider than a right angle

Types of Angles: Straight Angle

  • An angle whose measure is exactly 180°.
  • It forms a straight line.
  • Example: A perfectly flat horizon line.

🎙️ When the two rays of an angle point in exactly opposite directions, they form a straight line, and the angle is 180 degrees. Think of a ruler laid flat.

↔️
Forms a straight line

Types of Angles: Reflex Angle

  • An angle whose measure is between 180° and 360°.
  • It's the 'outside' angle, larger than a straight angle.
  • Example: The larger angle formed when a door is opened slightly.

🎙️ A reflex angle is like looking at the angle 'around' a point. If you turn more than half a circle, you've turned a reflex angle. It's the angle 'behind' the acute or obtuse angle.

↩️
Larger than a straight angle

Pairs of Angles: Complementary Angles

  • Two angles whose sum is 90°.
  • They 'complement' each other to form a right angle.
  • Example: A 30° angle and a 60° angle are complementary.

🎙️ When two angles perfectly fit together to make a right angle, they are complementary. Think of two puzzle pieces forming a square corner.

➕
Add up to 90°
15 / 25

Pairs of Angles: Supplementary Angles

  • Two angles whose sum is 180°.
  • They 'supplement' each other to form a straight angle.
  • Example: A 70° angle and a 110° angle are supplementary.

🎙️ If two angles combine to form a straight line, they are supplementary. Imagine two adjacent rooms sharing a wall, and together they make a flat layout.

➕
Add up to 180°

Pairs of Angles: Adjacent Angles

  • Two angles that share a common vertex and a common arm.
  • They do not overlap.
  • Example: Angles on either side of a shared wall in a house.

🎙️ Adjacent means 'next to'. So, adjacent angles are simply angles that are next to each other, sharing a common corner and a common side, without overlapping.

🤝
Sharing a side and vertex

Pairs of Angles: Linear Pair of Angles

  • A pair of adjacent angles whose non-common arms form a straight line.
  • Their sum is always 180° (supplementary).
  • Example: Angles formed when a ray stands on a line.

🎙️ A linear pair is a special case of adjacent angles. If the outer boundaries of two adjacent angles form a straight line, then they are a linear pair, and their sum is always 180 degrees.

📏
Adjacent and form a line

Pairs of Angles: Vertically Opposite Angles

  • Angles formed when two lines intersect.
  • They are opposite to each other at the point of intersection.
  • Vertically opposite angles are always equal.

🎙️ When two straight lines cross each other, they create four angles. The angles that are directly across from each other are called vertically opposite angles, and a key property is that they are always equal.

✖️
Across from each other

Worked Example: Finding Unknown Angles

  • Given two intersecting lines forming angles: 50°, x, y, z.
  • Angle x and 50° form a linear pair, so x + 50° = 180°.
  • Angle y and 50° are vertically opposite, so y = 50°.
  • Angle z and 50° form a linear pair, so z + 50° = 180°.

🎙️ Let's apply what we've learned. If one angle is 50 degrees, we can find the others. x and 50 form a linear pair, so x is 130. y is vertically opposite to 50, so y is 50. And z also forms a linear pair with 50, making it 130 degrees.

1x = 130°
2y = 50°
3z = 130°

Intersecting and Non-Intersecting Lines

  • Intersecting lines meet at a single common point.
  • Non-intersecting lines (or parallel lines) never meet, no matter how far they are extended.
  • Example: Roads crossing versus railway tracks.

🎙️ Just like roads, lines can either cross each other at a point, or they can run side-by-side forever without ever touching. These are intersecting and non-intersecting lines, respectively.

Parallel Lines

  • Two lines in a plane that never intersect.
  • The perpendicular distance between them is always constant.
  • Represented by arrows on the lines and notation 'l || m'.

🎙️ Parallel lines are a special type of non-intersecting lines. Think of the opposite edges of a ruler, or the rails of a train track. They maintain the same distance apart and will never meet.

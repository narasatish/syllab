Recap: The Number Line (One Dimension)

  • A straight line representing numbers.
  • Positive numbers to the right of zero.
  • Negative numbers to the left of zero.
  • Each point has a unique number.

🎙️ You're already familiar with the number line. It's a one-dimensional system where you can locate any number by moving left or right from zero. For example, '3' is three steps to the right, and '-2' is two steps to the left.

The Cartesian Plane (or Coordinate Plane)

  • A flat surface where points are located.
  • Formed by two perpendicular number lines.
  • These lines are called 'axes'.

🎙️ The Cartesian Plane is our canvas for coordinate geometry. It's essentially two number lines crossing each other at a perfect right angle. This setup allows us to describe any point on this plane using a pair of numbers.

Introducing the Axes: X and Y

  • The horizontal line is the X-axis (or abscissa axis).
  • The vertical line is the Y-axis (or ordinate axis).
  • These are our reference lines.

🎙️ Just like a map has a north-south and an east-west line, our Cartesian plane has an X-axis and a Y-axis. The X-axis runs horizontally, and the Y-axis runs vertically. They are the foundation for locating any point.

The Origin: Where It All Begins

  • The point where the X-axis and Y-axis intersect.
  • Its coordinates are always (0, 0).
  • It's our starting point for all measurements.

🎙️ The Origin is the central hub of our coordinate system. It's like the 'zero' on both number lines. Every time we want to locate a point, we imagine starting from this 'Origin' at (0,0) and moving from there.

🎯
Starting Point

Ordered Pairs: The Address of a Point (x, y)

  • Every point on the plane has a unique 'address'.
  • This address is written as an 'ordered pair' (x, y).
  • The first number (x) is the horizontal distance.
  • The second number (y) is the vertical distance.

🎙️ An ordered pair is like a postal address for a point. The 'x' always comes first, telling us how far to move horizontally, and the 'y' comes second, telling us how far to move vertically. The order matters!

🏷️
(x, y) Label

Abscissa and Ordinate: Fancy Names for Coordinates

  • The x-coordinate is also called the 'Abscissa'.
  • The y-coordinate is also called the 'Ordinate'.
  • Together, they form the coordinates of a point.

🎙️ These are important terms to remember. The abscissa tells you the horizontal position relative to the y-axis, and the ordinate tells you the vertical position relative to the x-axis. Don't get them confused!

Dividing the Plane: Quadrants

  • The X and Y axes divide the plane into four regions.
  • Each region is called a 'Quadrant'.
  • They are numbered I, II, III, IV in an anti-clockwise direction.

🎙️ When the two axes cross, they create four distinct sections, like four rooms in a house. These are the quadrants, and knowing which quadrant a point lies in tells us about the signs of its coordinates.

➕
Four Sections
15 / 30

Quadrant I: The 'All Positive' Zone

  • Located in the upper-right section.
  • Both x-coordinate and y-coordinate are positive.
  • Example: Point A (3, 2)

🎙️ In Quadrant I, you move right on the X-axis (positive x) and up on the Y-axis (positive y). So, any point here will have both its x and y coordinates as positive numbers. It's the 'sunny' quadrant!

Quadrant II: Left and Up

  • Located in the upper-left section.
  • x-coordinate is negative, y-coordinate is positive.
  • Example: Point B (-4, 1)

🎙️ To reach Quadrant II, you move left on the X-axis (negative x) and then up on the Y-axis (positive y). So, points in this quadrant will always have a negative x-value and a positive y-value.

Negative, Positive

Quadrant III: The 'All Negative' Zone

  • Located in the lower-left section.
  • Both x-coordinate and y-coordinate are negative.
  • Example: Point C (-2, -5)

🎙️ Quadrant III is where both coordinates take a dip. You move left on the X-axis (negative x) and then down on the Y-axis (negative y). So, points here will have both their x and y coordinates as negative numbers.

Quadrant IV: Right and Down

  • Located in the lower-right section.
  • x-coordinate is positive, y-coordinate is negative.

🎙️ Finally, Quadrant IV. Here, you move right on the X-axis (positive x) and then down on the Y-axis (negative y). Points in this quadrant will always have a positive x-value and a negative y-value.

Plotting Points: Step-by-Step

  • Start at the Origin (0,0).
  • Move horizontally along the X-axis by 'x' units.
  • From there, move vertically parallel to the Y-axis by 'y' units.
  • Mark the final position as your point.

🎙️ Plotting points is like following directions on a treasure map! Always start at the origin, move left or right for 'x', and then up or down for 'y'. Let's try an example together.

Points on the X-axis

  • Any point on the X-axis has its y-coordinate as 0.
  • It takes the form (x, 0).
  • Example: (5, 0) or (-3, 0).

🎙️ If a point lies directly on the X-axis, it means it hasn't moved up or down from the axis. Therefore, its vertical distance (y-coordinate) is zero. You just move left or right.

Points on the Y-axis

  • Any point on the Y-axis has its x-coordinate as 0.
  • It takes the form (0, y).
  • Example: (0, 4) or (0, -2).

🎙️ Similarly, if a point lies directly on the Y-axis, it means it hasn't moved left or right from the axis. Its horizontal distance (x-coordinate) is zero. You just move up or down.

Common Mistakes to Avoid!

  • Confusing (x, y) with (y, x) - order matters!
  • Incorrectly identifying signs in quadrants.
  • Mixing up X-axis and Y-axis when plotting.
  • Not starting from the Origin (0,0).

🎙️ It's easy to make small errors in coordinate geometry. Always remember that the x-coordinate comes first. Double-check your signs for quadrants and make sure you move correctly along the axes from the origin.

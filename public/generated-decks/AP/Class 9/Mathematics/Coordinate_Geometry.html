What is a Coordinate System?

  • A system to describe the position of a point.
  • Uses a pair of numbers called coordinates.
  • Needs reference lines or axes.

🎙️ Imagine you're trying to tell someone exactly where a treasure is hidden. You can't just say 'over there'! A coordinate system provides a clear, unambiguous way to specify a point's location using a set of numbers relative to a fixed starting point.

The Cartesian System

  • Developed by René Descartes.
  • Uses two perpendicular lines as reference.
  • Also known as the Rectangular Coordinate System.

🎙️ The most common coordinate system we use is called the Cartesian system, named after the brilliant French mathematician René Descartes. He came up with this idea while watching a fly on his ceiling, realizing he could describe its position with two numbers!

The X-axis and Y-axis

  • Horizontal line is the X-axis.
  • Vertical line is the Y-axis.
  • They intersect at a point called the Origin.

🎙️ Our two main reference lines are the X-axis, which runs horizontally like the horizon, and the Y-axis, which runs vertically like a tall building. These two lines are always perpendicular to each other, forming a perfect cross.

The Origin (0,0)

  • The point where the X-axis and Y-axis intersect.
  • Its coordinates are always (0,0).
  • It is the starting point for measuring all other points.

🎙️ The Origin is like the home base of our coordinate system. It's where both axes meet, and it's always represented by the coordinates (0,0). Every other point's position is measured relative to this central starting point.

Quadrants: Dividing the Plane

  • The axes divide the plane into four regions.
  • These regions are called Quadrants.
  • Numbered I, II, III, and IV in an anti-clockwise direction.

🎙️ When the X and Y axes cross, they divide the entire flat surface, or plane, into four distinct sections. We call these sections 'Quadrants'. They are numbered counter-clockwise, starting from the top-right section.

Locating a Point (x,y)

  • First number (x) tells horizontal movement.
  • Second number (y) tells vertical movement.
  • Always start from the Origin (0,0).

🎙️ To locate any point (x,y), you always start at the Origin. The 'x' value tells you how many steps to take horizontally (right if positive, left if negative). Then, the 'y' value tells you how many steps to take vertically (up if positive, down if negative).

Coordinates of a Point

  • An ordered pair (x, y) that specifies a point's location.
  • 'x' is the distance from the Y-axis.
  • 'y' is the distance from the X-axis.

🎙️ The coordinates are always written as an ordered pair, (x, y), with the x-value first and the y-value second. Remember, (2,3) is different from (3,2)! The x-coordinate tells you how far right or left, and the y-coordinate tells you how far up or down from the origin.

Abscissa and Ordinate

  • The x-coordinate is called the Abscissa.
  • The y-coordinate is called the Ordinate.
  • Together, they form the coordinates (Abscissa, Ordinate).

🎙️ These are just fancy names for the x and y coordinates! The abscissa tells you the horizontal position, and the ordinate tells you the vertical position. So, when you see a point like (5, 7), 5 is the abscissa and 7 is the ordinate.

Example 1: Locating Point (3,2)

  • Start at the Origin (0,0).
  • Move 3 units to the right along the X-axis.
  • From there, move 2 units up parallel to the Y-axis.
  • This is the point (3,2) in Quadrant I.

🎙️ Let's plot our first point! For (3,2), we begin at (0,0). First, we move 3 units to the right because the x-coordinate is positive 3. Then, from that position, we move 2 units upwards because the y-coordinate is positive 2. That's our point!

Example 2: Locating Point (-2,4)

  • Start at the Origin (0,0).
  • Move 2 units to the left along the X-axis (because -2).
  • From there, move 4 units up parallel to the Y-axis (because +4).
  • This is the point (-2,4) in Quadrant II.

🎙️ Now let's try a point with a negative x-coordinate. For (-2,4), we start at the Origin. Since the x-coordinate is -2, we move 2 units to the *left*. Then, because the y-coordinate is positive 4, we move 4 units *up*. This point lies in the second quadrant.

Points on the X-axis

  • Any point on the X-axis has its y-coordinate as 0.
  • It will be of the form (x, 0).
  • Example: (5,0), (-3,0).

🎙️ If a point lies directly on the X-axis, it means it hasn't moved up or down from the axis. Therefore, its vertical distance from the X-axis, which is the y-coordinate, must be zero. So, any point on the X-axis will always look like (x, 0).

Points on the Y-axis

  • Any point on the Y-axis has its x-coordinate as 0.
  • It will be of the form (0, y).
  • Example: (0,4), (0,-2).

🎙️ Similarly, if a point lies directly on the Y-axis, it means it hasn't moved left or right from the Y-axis. This means its horizontal distance from the Y-axis, the x-coordinate, is zero. So, points on the Y-axis always have the form (0, y).

Distance Formula (Introduction)

  • Used to find the distance between any two points.
  • A fundamental concept in coordinate geometry.
  • Helps calculate lengths of line segments.

🎙️ Now that we can locate points, what if we want to know the distance between two of them? The Distance Formula is a powerful tool that allows us to do just that. It's like having a virtual ruler to measure distances on our coordinate plane.

📏
Measuring Length

Distance Formula (Concept)

  • For two points (x₁, y₁) and (x₂, y₂).
  • Distance d = √[(x₂ - x₁)² + (y₂ - y₁)²].
  • Based on the Pythagorean theorem.

🎙️ The distance formula might look a bit complex, but it's actually a direct application of the Pythagorean theorem. You find the difference in x-coordinates, square it, do the same for y-coordinates, add them up, and then take the square root. Don't worry, we'll practice this!

➕
Pythagorean Link

Example 3: Calculating Distance

  • Find distance between A(1,2) and B(4,6).
  • x₁=1, y₁=2, x₂=4, y₂=6.
  • d = √[(4-1)² + (6-2)²]
  • d = √[3² + 4²] = √[9+16] = √25 = 5 units.

🎙️ Let's apply the distance formula. For points (1,2) and (4,6), we substitute the values into the formula. We calculate the difference in x's (3) and y's (4), square them, add them, and find the square root. The distance turns out to be 5 units.

Midpoint Formula (Introduction)

  • Used to find the exact middle point of a line segment.
  • Divides a line segment into two equal parts.
  • Useful for symmetry and geometric constructions.

🎙️ Another useful tool is the Midpoint Formula. If you have a line segment connecting two points, this formula helps you find the coordinates of the point that lies exactly in the middle of that segment. It's like finding the exact center of a road between two cities.

Midpoint Formula (Concept)

  • For two points (x₁, y₁) and (x₂, y₂).
  • Midpoint M = ( (x₁+x₂)/2 , (y₁+y₂)/2 ).
  • It's simply the average of the x-coordinates and y-coordinates.

🎙️ The midpoint formula is quite straightforward. You just need to add the x-coordinates of the two points and divide by 2, and do the same for the y-coordinates. This gives you the average x and y values, which is exactly where the middle point will be.

⚖️

Example 4: Calculating Midpoint

  • Find the midpoint of A(1,2) and B(5,8).
  • x₁=1, y₁=2, x₂=5, y₂=8.
  • Midpoint x = (1+5)/2 = 6/2 = 3.
  • Midpoint y = (2+8)/2 = 10/2 = 5.
  • Midpoint M = (3,5).

🎙️ Let's calculate a midpoint. For points (1,2) and (5,8), we add the x-coordinates (1+5=6) and divide by 2 to get 3. We do the same for y-coordinates (2+8=10) and divide by 2 to get 5. So, the midpoint is (3,5).

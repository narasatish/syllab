What are Polynomials?

  • An expression with variables, constants, and exponents.
  • Variables are usually letters like x, y, z.
  • Constants are fixed numbers like 5, -2, 1/3.
  • Exponents must be non-negative integers (0, 1, 2, 3...).

🎙️ A polynomial is essentially a mathematical expression built from variables, constants, and exponents, combined using addition, subtraction, and multiplication. The key rule is that the exponents on the variables must always be whole numbers, like 0, 1, 2, and so on.

Terms of a Polynomial

  • Parts of a polynomial separated by '+' or '-' signs.
  • Each term consists of a coefficient and a variable part.
  • Example: In 5x² + 3x - 7, the terms are 5x², 3x, and -7.

🎙️ Just like words make up a sentence, terms make up a polynomial. Each term is a product of a number and one or more variables raised to a power. We separate these terms using addition or subtraction signs.

Coefficients: The Numerical Part

  • The numerical factor multiplying the variable part of a term.
  • Example: In 4x³, the coefficient is 4.
  • If a term is just 'x', the coefficient is 1 (1x).
  • A constant term like '5' can be thought of as 5x⁰, with coefficient 5.

🎙️ The coefficient is simply the number that stands in front of the variable in each term. It tells us how many times the variable part is being multiplied. Understanding coefficients is crucial for performing operations on polynomials.

Degree of a Polynomial

  • The highest power of the variable in a polynomial.
  • For a single variable polynomial, find the largest exponent.
  • Example: In 7x³ - 2x⁵ + x, the degree is 5.

🎙️ The degree of a polynomial is a very important characteristic. It's determined by looking at all the terms and finding the highest exponent attached to any variable. If there are multiple variables in a term, you sum their exponents for that term.

Types of Polynomials (by Number of Terms)

  • Monomial: One term (e.g., 5x²)
  • Binomial: Two terms (e.g., 2x + 3)
  • Trinomial: Three terms (e.g., x² - 4x + 7)

🎙️ We classify polynomials based on how many terms they have. A monomial is a single term, a binomial has two terms, and a trinomial has three terms. These names are derived from Greek prefixes, 'mono' for one, 'bi' for two, and 'tri' for three.

Real-Life Example: Area of a Rectangle

  • If length is 'x' and width is 'y', area is 'xy' (Monomial).
  • If length is 'x+2' and width is 'x', area is 'x(x+2) = x² + 2x' (Binomial).

🎙️ Consider finding the area of a rectangle. If the length is 'x' and width is 'y', the area 'xy' is a monomial. If the length is 'x+2' and width is 'x', the area becomes 'x² + 2x', which is a binomial. This shows how polynomials naturally appear in geometry.

Types of Polynomials (by Degree)

  • Linear Polynomial: Degree 1 (e.g., 3x + 5)
  • Quadratic Polynomial: Degree 2 (e.g., 2x² - x + 1)
  • Cubic Polynomial: Degree 3 (e.g., x³ + 4x - 2)
  • Constant Polynomial: Degree 0 (e.g., 7)

🎙️ Besides the number of terms, we also classify polynomials by their degree. Linear polynomials have a degree of 1, quadratic polynomials have a degree of 2, and cubic polynomials have a degree of 3. Constant numbers are also polynomials with a degree of 0, as they can be written as 7x⁰.

Zero of a Polynomial

  • A value 'k' such that when substituted into P(x), the result is 0.
  • If P(k) = 0, then 'k' is a zero of the polynomial P(x).
  • It's also called a root of the polynomial equation P(x) = 0.

🎙️ The 'zero' of a polynomial is a very significant concept. It's the specific value of the variable that makes the entire polynomial expression equal to zero. Finding these zeros is often a key objective in solving polynomial equations.

Worked Example 3: Evaluating a Polynomial

  • Problem: Find P(2) for P(x) = x² + 3x - 5.
  • Step 1: Substitute x = 2 into the polynomial.
  • Step 2: P(2) = (2)² + 3(2) - 5.
  • Step 3: Calculate the value: 4 + 6 - 5 = 5.
  • Step 4: So, P(2) = 5.

🎙️ To evaluate a polynomial at a specific value, you simply replace every 'x' with that value and then perform the arithmetic. For P(x) = x² + 3x - 5, when x is 2, we get 2 squared plus 3 times 2, minus 5, which simplifies to 4 plus 6 minus 5, giving us 5.

Worked Example 4: Finding a Zero of a Linear Polynomial

  • Problem: Find the zero of P(x) = 2x - 6.
  • Step 1: Set the polynomial equal to zero: P(x) = 0.
  • Step 2: So, 2x - 6 = 0.
  • Step 3: Solve for x: 2x = 6.
  • Step 4: x = 6/2 = 3. The zero is 3.

🎙️ For a linear polynomial, finding the zero is straightforward. We set the expression equal to zero and solve the resulting linear equation. In this case, 2x minus 6 equals zero, which means 2x equals 6, and therefore x equals 3. So, 3 is the zero.

  • We divide a polynomial (dividend) by another polynomial (divisor).
  • The result is a quotient and a remainder.
  • This process is similar to long division of numbers.

🎙️ Now, let's briefly touch upon dividing polynomials. It's a fundamental operation, much like dividing numbers. When you divide one polynomial by another, you get a quotient and sometimes a remainder, just like in arithmetic.

Remainder Theorem

  • If a polynomial P(x) is divided by (x - a), the remainder is P(a).
  • This theorem provides a shortcut to find the remainder without long division.
  • Example: Remainder when P(x) = x² + 1 is divided by (x - 1) is P(1) = 1² + 1 = 2.

🎙️ The Remainder Theorem is a powerful tool! It tells us that if you want to find the remainder when a polynomial P(x) is divided by a linear factor (x - a), you simply need to evaluate the polynomial at 'a', i.e., find P(a). This saves a lot of time compared to long division.

Factor Theorem

  • A special case of the Remainder Theorem.
  • If P(a) = 0, then (x - a) is a factor of P(x).
  • Conversely, if (x - a) is a factor, then P(a) = 0.
  • This helps us find factors and zeros of polynomials.

🎙️ The Factor Theorem is directly linked to the Remainder Theorem. It states that if evaluating a polynomial P(x) at 'a' gives zero, then (x - a) must be a factor of that polynomial. This theorem is extremely useful for factoring polynomials and finding their roots.

Real-Life Application: Designing a Box

  • The volume of a box can be expressed as a polynomial.
  • If length = x, width = x-2, height = x+1, Volume V(x) = x(x-2)(x+1).
  • This is a cubic polynomial: V(x) = x³ - x² - 2x.
  • Polynomials help engineers and designers model shapes and quantities.

🎙️ Polynomials are not just abstract math; they have real-world uses. For example, when designing a box, its volume can be represented by a polynomial. If the dimensions are given in terms of 'x', multiplying them will give you a polynomial expression for the volume, which helps in optimizing design.

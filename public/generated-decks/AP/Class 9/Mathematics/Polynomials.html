Constants & Variables: The Building Blocks

  • Constants: Fixed numerical values (e.g., 5, -7, 1/2).
  • Variables: Symbols that can take different values (e.g., x, y, a).
  • Example: In 3x + 4, '3' and '4' are constants, 'x' is a variable.

Defining a Polynomial: The Special Rule

  • An algebraic expression where variables have only non-negative integer powers.
  • No negative powers, no fractional powers.
  • Example: 3x² + 2x - 1 is a polynomial.

🎙️ Not all algebraic expressions are polynomials! There's a special rule: the power of the variable must always be a whole number, like 0, 1, 2, 3... You can't have powers like -1 or 1/2. This is what makes a polynomial unique.

Is it a Polynomial? Let's See!

  • Polynomial: 5x³ + 2x - 7 (Powers: 3, 1, 0)
  • Not a Polynomial: 4x⁻² + 3 (Power: -2 is negative)
  • Not a Polynomial: √x + 1 (Power: √x = x^(1/2) is fractional)

🎙️ Look closely at the powers of the variables. If you see any negative numbers or fractions in the exponent, then it's not a polynomial. This is the crucial test to distinguish them from other algebraic expressions.

1Check Powers
2Must be Non-Negative
3Must be Integers

Terms: The Parts of a Polynomial

  • Individual components separated by '+' or '-' signs.
  • Each term has a coefficient and a variable part.
  • Example: In 4x² + 3x - 5, the terms are 4x², 3x, and -5.

🎙️ Imagine a polynomial as a train. Each carriage on the train is a 'term'. They are linked together by plus or minus signs. Each carriage, or term, has its own unique features.

Breaking Down Terms

  • Polynomial: 7x³ - 2x² + x - 10
  • Term 1: 7x³
  • Term 2: -2x²
  • Term 3: x (which is 1x)
  • Term 4: -10 (constant term)

🎙️ When you see a polynomial, mentally draw lines where the plus or minus signs are. Each section you create is a term. Don't forget to include the sign in front of the term when you identify it!

1Identify '+' or '-'
2Each part is a term
3Include the sign

Coefficients: The Number Partner

  • The numerical factor of a term.
  • It tells us how many times the variable part is present.
  • Example: In 5x², '5' is the coefficient of x².

🎙️ Think of a coefficient as the 'number friend' that stands right next to the variable. It tells you 'how many' of that variable part you have. If there's no number, like in 'x', the coefficient is 1.

Finding the Coefficients

  • Polynomial: -3y⁴ + 8y - 12
  • Coefficient of y⁴: -3
  • Coefficient of y: 8
  • Coefficient of y⁰ (constant term): -12

🎙️ It's important to include the sign with the coefficient. So, if a term is '-3y⁴', the coefficient is '-3', not just '3'. The constant term also has a coefficient, which is itself, as it's multiplied by y to the power of zero.

Degree: The Highest Power

  • The highest exponent of the variable in a polynomial.
  • For a constant polynomial (e.g., 7), the degree is 0.
  • Example: In 6x³ + 2x⁵ - 4, the degree is 5.

🎙️ The degree of a polynomial is like finding the tallest person in a group. You look at all the powers of the variable in each term and pick the largest one. This highest power tells us a lot about the polynomial's behavior.

How to Find the Degree

  • Polynomial: 9x⁴ - 2x⁷ + 5x² - 1
  • Powers of x are: 4, 7, 2, 0.
  • The highest power is 7.
  • So, the degree of the polynomial is 7.

🎙️ To find the degree, simply scan through all the terms and note down the exponent of the variable in each term. Once you have all the exponents, pick the largest one. That's your degree!

1List all powers
2Find the largest
3That's the degree!

Zeroes of a Polynomial: The Solution

  • A value of the variable that makes the polynomial equal to zero.
  • It's where the polynomial 'crosses' the x-axis on a graph.
  • Example: For P(x) = x - 2, the zero is x = 2 (because 2 - 2 = 0).

🎙️ The 'zero' of a polynomial is a very important concept. It's the specific number you can substitute for the variable that makes the entire polynomial expression equal to zero. Think of it as finding the specific input that gives you a zero output.

🎯
Target Zero

How to Find a Zero

  • To find the zero of P(x) = 3x - 6:
  • 1. Set P(x) = 0: 3x - 6 = 0
  • 2. Solve for x: 3x = 6
  • 3. x = 6/3 = 2
  • So, x = 2 is the zero of P(x).

🎙️ Finding a zero is like solving a simple equation. You just set the polynomial equal to zero and then use your algebraic skills to find the value of the variable. This value is the zero you're looking for.

1Set P(x) = 0
2Solve for x
3The value is the zero

Evaluating Polynomials: Plugging in Values

  • To find the value of P(x) at x = a, substitute 'a' for 'x' in the expression.
  • Example: If P(x) = x² + 3x - 1, find P(2).
  • P(2) = (2)² + 3(2) - 1 = 4 + 6 - 1 = 9.

🎙️ Evaluating a polynomial is like using a formula. You're given a specific number, and you just 'plug' that number into the polynomial wherever you see the variable. Then, you simplify the expression to get a single numerical value.

Watch Out! Common Mistakes

  • Confusing algebraic expressions with polynomials (e.g., x⁻¹ is not a polynomial).
  • Incorrectly identifying the degree (e.g., picking a coefficient instead of an exponent).
  • Errors in signs when finding coefficients or evaluating.

🎙️ Many students make small errors. Remember, powers must be non-negative integers for a polynomial. Always look for the highest *exponent* for the degree, not the largest number. And be super careful with positive and negative signs!

Quick Recap: What We Learned

  • Polynomials are expressions with non-negative integer powers.
  • Terms are parts separated by +/-, coefficients are numerical factors.
  • Degree is the highest power of the variable.
  • Classified by terms (monomial, binomial, trinomial) and degree (linear, quadratic, cubic, constant).
  • Zeroes make the polynomial equal to zero.

What is a Circle?

  • A collection of points.
  • All points are equidistant from a fixed point.
  • The fixed point is the 'centre'.

🎙️ Imagine drawing a perfect curve where every point on that curve is exactly the same distance from a central point. That's a circle! Think of it like a bangle, all its parts are equally far from its middle.

⭕
The Perfect Round Shape

The Centre and Radius

  • The 'centre' is the fixed point.
  • The 'radius' is the fixed distance.
  • Connects the centre to any point on the circle.

🎙️ The centre is like the hub of a wheel, and the radius is like one of its spokes. All spokes from the hub to the rim are of the same length, just like all radii of a circle are equal.

📍
Centre (O) and Radius (r)

Diameter: The Longest Chord

  • A line segment passing through the centre.
  • Connects two points on the circle.
  • It's twice the length of the radius (D = 2r).

🎙️ If you walk straight across a circular park, passing through its very middle, that path is the diameter. It's the longest possible straight line you can draw inside a circle, connecting two points on its boundary.

Diameter (D)

Chord: Connecting Two Points

  • A line segment joining any two points on a circle.
  • Does not necessarily pass through the centre.
  • The diameter is the longest chord.

🎙️ Imagine a bridge connecting two points on the edge of a circular lake. That bridge is like a chord. It connects two points on the circle's boundary, but it doesn't always have to go through the centre of the lake.

🌉
A Chord

Arc: A Piece of the Circle

  • A continuous piece of a circle.
  • Formed by two points on the circle.
  • Can be a 'minor arc' (smaller) or 'major arc' (larger).

🎙️ If you take a bite out of a circular pizza crust, that curved piece is an arc. A small bite is a minor arc, while the rest of the crust is the major arc. Together, they make up the whole circle.

🍕
Minor and Major Arc

Segment: Area Bounded by Chord and Arc

  • Region between a chord and its corresponding arc.
  • Can be a 'minor segment' or 'major segment'.
  • Think of a slice of pizza with a straight cut.

🎙️ Imagine you cut a circular cake with a straight knife, but not through the centre. The piece you cut out, which has a straight edge and a curved edge, is a segment. There will be a smaller piece (minor segment) and a larger piece (major segment).

🍰
Minor and Major Segment

Sector: Area Bounded by Two Radii and an Arc

  • Region between two radii and the arc between them.
  • Like a slice of pizza cut from the centre.
  • Can be a 'minor sector' or 'major sector'.

🎙️ Now, if you cut a circular pizza from the very centre outwards along two radii, the piece you get is a sector. It's that classic 'pizza slice' shape, defined by two straight lines and a curved arc.

🥧
Minor and Major Sector

Theorem 1: Perpendicular from Centre to Chord

  • A perpendicular from the centre to a chord.
  • It bisects the chord.
  • Divides the chord into two equal parts.

🎙️ This is a very important property! If you drop a perpendicular line from the centre of a circle straight onto any chord, it will always cut that chord exactly in half. Think of it like a perfect cut.

✂️
Bisecting the Chord

Worked Example 1: Using the Chord Theorem

  • Problem: A chord of length 16 cm is 6 cm from the centre. Find the radius.
  • Step 1: Draw a diagram. Form a right-angled triangle.
  • Step 2: Apply the theorem: Perpendicular bisects the chord (8 cm).
  • Step 3: Use Pythagoras theorem: Radius² = 6² + 8² = 36 + 64 = 100. Radius = 10 cm.

🎙️ Let's see this theorem in action. By drawing a diagram and forming a right-angled triangle, we can use the Pythagorean theorem to find the unknown radius. Remember, the perpendicular bisects the chord!

Theorem 2: Converse of Perpendicular from Centre

  • A line drawn through the centre.
  • If it bisects a chord, it is perpendicular to the chord.
  • The opposite of Theorem 1.

🎙️ This theorem is the converse of the previous one. It says that if a line from the centre cuts a chord into two equal halves, then that line *must* be perpendicular to the chord. It's like proving the original statement backwards.

🔄
Reversed Logic

Theorem 3 & 4: Equal Chords and Distances

  • Equal chords are equidistant from the centre.
  • Chords equidistant from the centre are equal.
  • These are converses of each other.

🎙️ Imagine two identical swings hanging from the same point. If their ropes are of equal length, they will hang at the same distance from the centre. Similarly, equal chords in a circle are always the same distance from the centre.

⚖️
Balanced Chords

Worked Example 2: Equal Chords

  • Problem: Two chords AB and CD are equal. If AB is 5 cm from the centre, what is the distance of CD?
  • Step 1: Identify given information: AB = CD, distance of AB from centre = 5 cm.
  • Step 2: Apply Theorem 3: Equal chords are equidistant from the centre.
  • Step 3: Therefore, the distance of chord CD from the centre is also 5 cm.

🎙️ This example directly uses the theorem we just learned. Since the chords are equal, their distances from the centre must also be equal. It's a straightforward application of the property.

Theorem 5: Angle Subtended by an Arc

  • The angle subtended by an arc at the centre.
  • It's double the angle subtended by it at any point on the remaining part of the circle.
  • This is a crucial angle property.

🎙️ Imagine an arc of a circle. The angle it makes at the very centre is twice as big as the angle it makes at any other point on the circle's boundary. It's like looking at a stage from the front row versus from the back of the hall.

👁️
Centre vs. Circumference

Theorem 6: Angles in the Same Segment

  • Angles in the same segment of a circle are equal.
  • If multiple points on the circumference 'look' at the same arc.
  • All these angles will be identical.

🎙️ Think of two friends sitting at different spots on a circular bench, both looking at the same section of a cricket match. The angle of their view of that section will be the same. This theorem states that angles formed by the same segment are equal.

👯
Equal Angles

Worked Example 3: Angles in a Circle

  • Problem: If ∠AOB = 80° (O is centre), find ∠ACB.
  • Step 1: Identify that arc AB subtends ∠AOB at the centre.
  • Step 2: Arc AB also subtends ∠ACB at the remaining part of the circle.
  • Step 3: Apply Theorem 5: ∠AOB = 2 * ∠ACB. So, 80° = 2 * ∠ACB. ∠ACB = 40°.

🎙️ Here's how we use the angle subtended theorem. By knowing the angle at the centre, we can easily find the angle at the circumference. This is a common type of question in exams.

Cyclic Quadrilateral: All Vertices on Circle

  • A quadrilateral whose all four vertices lie on a circle.
  • All corners 'touch' the circumference.
  • Special properties apply to its angles.

🎙️ Imagine four friends standing on the edge of a circular stage, forming a four-sided shape. If all four friends are exactly on the edge, they form a cyclic quadrilateral. This shape has unique angle properties.

Theorem 7: Properties of a Cyclic Quadrilateral

  • The sum of either pair of opposite angles is 180°.
  • If ∠A + ∠C = 180°, and ∠B + ∠D = 180°.
  • This is a defining characteristic.

🎙️ This is the most important property of a cyclic quadrilateral. If you add up any two angles that are opposite to each other in a cyclic quadrilateral, their sum will always be 180 degrees. This is very useful for solving problems!

➕
Opposite Angles Sum

Theorem 8: Exterior Angle of a Cyclic Quadrilateral

  • If one side of a cyclic quadrilateral is produced.
  • The exterior angle formed is equal to the interior opposite angle.
  • Another useful angle property.

🎙️ If you extend one side of a cyclic quadrilateral, the angle formed outside is equal to the angle inside, directly opposite to it. This is a quick way to find angles without calculating the adjacent interior angle first.

📐
Exterior = Interior Opposite

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Confusing segment and sector.
  • Forgetting diameter is the longest chord.
  • Not identifying the correct angle for theorems (centre vs. circumference).

🎙️ Many students get confused between segments and sectors, or forget that the diameter is a special type of chord. Always draw diagrams and label points clearly to avoid these common errors. Practice helps a lot!

Exam Tips for Circles

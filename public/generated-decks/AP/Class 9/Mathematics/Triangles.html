Congruent Figures: Same Shape, Same Size

  • Two figures are congruent if they have the same shape and the same size.
  • They can be placed one over the other to perfectly cover each other.
  • Symbol: ≅

🎙️ Formally, congruent figures are those that are exact copies of each other. If you cut them out, you could place one directly on top of the other, and they would match perfectly. The symbol '≅' means 'is congruent to'.

Congruent Triangles: Perfect Match!

  • Two triangles are congruent if their corresponding parts are equal.
  • Corresponding sides are equal.
  • Corresponding angles are equal.
  • CPCTC: Corresponding Parts of Congruent Triangles are Congruent.

🎙️ For triangles, congruence means all three corresponding sides are equal, AND all three corresponding angles are equal. This is a very powerful concept. Once you prove triangles are congruent, you know all their matching parts are also equal due to CPCTC.

Congruence Rule: SSS (Side-Side-Side)

  • If three sides of one triangle are equal to three sides of another triangle, then the two triangles are congruent.
  • Analogy: Building a stable structure with fixed lengths.

🎙️ Our first rule! Imagine you have three sticks of specific lengths. You can only form one unique triangle with those sticks. So, if two triangles have all three corresponding sides equal, they must be congruent. This is SSS.

SSS Worked Example

  • Given: In ΔABC and ΔDEF,
  • AB = DE (4 cm)
  • BC = EF (5 cm)
  • AC = DF (6 cm)
  • Conclusion: ΔABC ≅ ΔDEF (by SSS rule)

🎙️ Here, we clearly see that all three corresponding sides are equal in length. Therefore, we can confidently say that triangle ABC is congruent to triangle DEF using the SSS congruence rule. Simple, right?

Congruence Rule: SAS (Side-Angle-Side)

  • If two sides and the *included* angle of one triangle are equal to two sides and the *included* angle of another triangle, then the triangles are congruent.
  • Analogy: A door hinge – the angle is between the two sides.

🎙️ This rule is like a door hinge. The angle must be *between* the two sides you are comparing. If you have two sides and the angle formed by them, the triangle is uniquely determined. This is the SAS rule.

🚪

SAS Worked Example

  • Given: In ΔPQR and ΔXYZ,
  • PQ = XY (3 cm)
  • ∠Q = ∠Y (60°)
  • QR = YZ (4 cm)
  • Conclusion: ΔPQR ≅ ΔXYZ (by SAS rule)

🎙️ Notice how angle Q is *between* sides PQ and QR. Similarly, angle Y is *between* sides XY and YZ. Since these corresponding parts are equal, we can apply the SAS rule and conclude that the triangles are congruent.

Congruence Rule: ASA (Angle-Side-Angle)

  • If two angles and the *included* side of one triangle are equal to two angles and the *included* side of another triangle, then the triangles are congruent.
  • Analogy: A road segment with turns at both ends.

🎙️ Here, the side must be *between* the two angles. Imagine you're drawing a triangle. If you fix two angles and the side connecting their vertices, the rest of the triangle is fixed. This is the ASA rule.

🛣️

ASA Worked Example

  • Given: In ΔLMN and ΔOPQ,
  • ∠M = ∠P (70°)
  • MN = PQ (5 cm)
  • ∠N = ∠Q (50°)
  • Conclusion: ΔLMN ≅ ΔOPQ (by ASA rule)

🎙️ Observe that side MN is included between angles M and N. Similarly, side PQ is included between angles P and Q. Since these corresponding parts are equal, we can apply the ASA rule and declare congruence.

Congruence Rule: AAS (Angle-Angle-Side)

  • If two angles and a *non-included* side of one triangle are equal to two angles and a *non-included* side of another triangle, then the triangles are congruent.
  • Relation to ASA: If two angles are known, the third angle is also known (180° rule).

🎙️ AAS is very similar to ASA. If you know two angles, you automatically know the third angle because the sum of angles in a triangle is 180°. So, AAS is essentially a variation of ASA, just with a non-included side.

AAS Worked Example

  • Given: In ΔRST and ΔUVW,
  • ∠R = ∠U (80°)
  • ∠S = ∠V (40°)
  • ST = VW (6 cm) (non-included side)
  • Conclusion: ΔRST ≅ ΔUVW (by AAS rule)

🎙️ Here, ST is not between angles R and S. It's opposite angle R. Since two angles and a non-included side are equal, the AAS rule applies. This rule is very useful when the side given is not the one connecting the two known angles.

Properties of Isosceles Triangles

  • A triangle with two equal sides.
  • The angles opposite to the equal sides are also equal.
  • Analogy: A symmetrical mountain peak.

🎙️ An isosceles triangle is a special type of triangle. If two of its sides are equal in length, then the angles opposite to those sides will also be equal. This creates a beautiful symmetry within the triangle.

Theorem: Angles Opposite Equal Sides

  • If two sides of a triangle are equal, then the angles opposite to these sides are equal.
  • In ΔABC, if AB = AC, then ∠B = ∠C.

🎙️ This is a key theorem for isosceles triangles. It means that if you know two sides are equal, you automatically know the two angles opposite them are also equal. This property is very useful in solving problems.

⚖️
Balance of angles

Isosceles Triangle Worked Example

  • Given: In ΔABC, AB = AC. ∠A = 40°.
  • Find: ∠B and ∠C.
  • Solution: Since AB = AC, ∠B = ∠C (angles opposite equal sides).
  • ∠A + ∠B + ∠C = 180°
  • 40° + 2∠B = 180°
  • 2∠B = 140° => ∠B = 70°.
  • So, ∠C = 70°.

🎙️ Here's how you apply the property. First, identify the equal sides and their opposite angles. Then, use the angle sum property of a triangle to find the unknown angles. It's a straightforward application.

Converse Theorem: Sides Opposite Equal Angles

  • If two angles of a triangle are equal, then the sides opposite to these angles are equal.
  • In ΔABC, if ∠B = ∠C, then AB = AC.

🎙️ This is the converse of the previous theorem. It works both ways! If you find two angles in a triangle are equal, you can immediately conclude that the sides opposite those angles are also equal. This helps prove side equality.

Congruence Rule: RHS (Right Angle-Hypotenuse-Side)

  • If in two right-angled triangles, the hypotenuse and one side of one triangle are equal to the hypotenuse and one side of the other triangle, then the two triangles are congruent.
  • Only for right-angled triangles!

🎙️ RHS is a special congruence rule *only* for right-angled triangles. You need a right angle, the hypotenuse (the side opposite the right angle), and one other corresponding side to be equal. It's a very common rule in geometry.

RHS Worked Example

  • ∠B = ∠Q = 90°
  • AC = PR (hypotenuse, 5 cm)
  • BC = QR (side, 3 cm)
  • Conclusion: ΔABC ≅ ΔPQR (by RHS rule)

🎙️ Here, we have the right angles, the hypotenuses are equal, and one pair of corresponding legs are equal. This perfectly fits the RHS congruence criterion. Remember, this rule is specific to right triangles.

Inequalities in a Triangle: Comparing Sides and Angles

  • The side opposite the larger angle is longer.
  • The angle opposite the longer side is larger.
  • Triangle Inequality: Sum of any two sides > third side.

🎙️ Beyond congruence, triangles also have interesting inequalities. The largest angle is always opposite the longest side, and vice versa. Also, for a triangle to exist, the sum of any two sides must be greater than the third side.

Triangle Inequality Theorem

  • For any triangle, the sum of the lengths of any two sides must be greater than the length of the third side.
  • Example: Sides 3, 4, 5 form a triangle (3+4>5, 4+5>3, 3+5>4).
  • Example: Sides 2, 3, 6 do NOT form a triangle (2+3 is NOT > 6).

🎙️ This theorem is crucial to check if a given set of side lengths can actually form a triangle. If this condition isn't met, you simply cannot construct a triangle with those side lengths. It's a fundamental property!

Common Mistakes to Avoid!

  • Confusing SSA with AAS (SSA is NOT a valid rule!)
  • Not identifying *included* angle/side correctly for SAS/ASA.
  • Assuming triangles are congruent just because they 'look' similar.
  • Forgetting CPCTC only applies AFTER congruence is proven.

🎙️ Many students make these common errors. Remember, SSA is NOT a congruence rule. Always double-check if the angle or side is truly 'included' for SAS and ASA. Don't rely on visual appearance; always prove congruence using the rules.

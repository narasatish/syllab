Carbon: The Ultimate Connector

  • Carbon is found in Group 14 of the Periodic Table
  • Atomic number is 6
  • Electronic configuration: 2, 4

🎙️ Imagine carbon as a super-friendly person who loves to make four new friends. Its electronic configuration tells us it has four electrons in its outermost shell, making it very eager to bond.

How Carbon Forms Bonds: Sharing is Caring!

  • To achieve stability, carbon needs 8 electrons in its outer shell
  • It cannot gain 4 electrons (too much energy)
  • It cannot lose 4 electrons (too much energy)

🎙️ So, what's the solution? Carbon shares its four valence electrons with other atoms. This type of bond, formed by the sharing of electrons, is called a covalent bond.

Valency of Carbon: Four Hands to Connect

  • Carbon's valency is 4
  • It forms four covalent bonds
  • This is known as tetravalency

🎙️ Think of carbon as having four hands, each capable of holding onto another atom. This ability to form four bonds is what makes carbon incredibly versatile and able to create a vast number of compounds.

Carbon's Superpower: Catenation!

  • Ability of carbon atoms to link with each other
  • Forms long chains, branched chains, and rings
  • Leads to a huge number of carbon compounds

🎙️ This is truly carbon's unique superpower! Imagine building with LEGO blocks – carbon atoms can connect endlessly with other carbon atoms, forming complex structures. This property is called catenation.

Carbon's Versatility: Four Bonds, Many Possibilities

  • Carbon can bond with other carbon atoms (Catenation)
  • It can also bond with hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, sulfur, halogens, etc.

🎙️ Not only can carbon bond with itself, but it can also bond strongly with many other elements. This, combined with its ability to form single, double, or triple bonds, allows for an incredible diversity of compounds.

  • They have different physical properties
  • But similar chemical properties

🎙️ Just like a person can wear different clothes and look different, carbon can exist in various forms, each with unique physical characteristics, even though they are all made of only carbon atoms. These are called allotropes.

Allotropes: Diamond - Hardest Known Substance

  • Each carbon atom is bonded to four other carbon atoms
  • Forms a rigid, 3D tetrahedral structure
  • Extremely hard, high melting point, non-conductor of electricity

🎙️ Diamond is a giant covalent structure where every carbon atom is strongly bonded to four others. This makes it incredibly strong and the hardest natural substance known. It's used in jewellery and cutting tools.

💍
Sparkling diamond
12 / 30

Allotropes: Graphite - Smooth and Conductive

  • Carbon atoms arranged in hexagonal layers
  • Layers held by weak forces, can slide over each other
  • Soft, slippery, good conductor of electricity

🎙️ Graphite is what you find in your pencil lead. Unlike diamond, carbon atoms in graphite are arranged in flat layers. These layers can slide past each other, making graphite soft and a good lubricant. It also conducts electricity due to free electrons.

✏️
Pencil lead

Allotropes: Fullerene - The Buckyball!

  • First identified as C-60 (Buckminsterfullerene)
  • Carbon atoms arranged in a spherical shape
  • Resembles a football (soccer ball)

🎙️ Fullerenes are a more recently discovered allotrope, with C-60 being the most famous. Its structure is like a hollow sphere, resembling a football. They have potential applications in medicine and nanotechnology.

Saturated Hydrocarbons: Alkanes - The 'Single Bond' Family

  • Contain only carbon-carbon single bonds
  • All valencies of carbon are satisfied by single bonds
  • General formula: CnH2n+2

🎙️ Alkanes are like a happy family where every carbon atom is holding hands with its neighbors using only one hand each. They are called 'saturated' because they contain the maximum possible number of hydrogen atoms.

➖➖➖
Single bonds

Unsaturated Hydrocarbons: Alkenes - The 'Double Bond' Family

  • Contain at least one carbon-carbon double bond
  • General formula: CnH2n
  • More reactive than alkanes

🎙️ Alkenes are a bit more adventurous! They have at least one carbon atom sharing two pairs of electrons with another carbon atom, forming a double bond. This makes them 'unsaturated' and more reactive.

🟰🟰
Double bonds

Unsaturated Hydrocarbons: Alkynes - The 'Triple Bond' Family

  • Contain at least one carbon-carbon triple bond
  • General formula: CnH2n-2
  • Even more reactive than alkenes

🎙️ And then there are alkynes, the most 'bonded' of the lot! They feature a carbon-carbon triple bond, meaning three pairs of electrons are shared. This makes them highly unsaturated and very reactive.

Common Functional Groups: Haloalkanes (-X)

🎙️ Haloalkanes are formed when one or more hydrogen atoms in an alkane are substituted by halogen atoms like chlorine or bromine. They are important industrial chemicals, but some, like CFCs, have environmental concerns.

Chemical Properties: Burning Carbon (Combustion)

  • Carbon compounds burn in oxygen to produce CO2 and water
  • Releases a large amount of heat and light
  • Example: Burning methane (natural gas)

🎙️ One of the most common chemical properties of carbon compounds is combustion, or burning. When hydrocarbons burn completely in sufficient oxygen, they produce carbon dioxide and water, releasing energy in the form of heat and light, which we use as fuel.

Soaps & Detergents: How They Clean!

  • Soaps are sodium or potassium salts of long-chain carboxylic acids
  • Detergents are synthetic cleansing agents
  • Both have hydrophobic (water-repelling) and hydrophilic (water-attracting) parts

🎙️ Have you ever wondered how soap cleans your clothes? Soaps and detergents work by forming micelles around dirt particles. The hydrophobic part traps the oil/grease, while the hydrophilic part dissolves in water, washing the dirt away.

Common Mistakes to Avoid!

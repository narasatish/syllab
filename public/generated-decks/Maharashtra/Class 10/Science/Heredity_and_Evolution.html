What is Heredity?

  • Transfer of characteristics from one generation to the next.
  • Also known as 'inheritance'.
  • Explains why children resemble their parents but are not identical.

🎙️ Heredity is the process by which traits and characteristics are passed down from parents to their children. Think about how you might have your mother's eyes or your father's height. This transfer of genetic information is what we call heredity.

The Blueprint of Life: DNA

  • Deoxyribonucleic Acid (DNA) is the genetic material.
  • Carries all hereditary information in living organisms.
  • Found primarily in the nucleus of cells.

🎙️ DNA is like the instruction manual for every living being. It contains all the codes that determine our characteristics, from hair colour to blood type. This incredible molecule is the fundamental unit of heredity.

Genes: The Units of Heredity

  • Specific segments of DNA.
  • Each gene carries instructions for a particular trait.
  • Passed from parents to offspring.

🎙️ Imagine DNA as a long book. Each chapter in that book, which gives instructions for a specific feature, is a gene. These genes are the actual units that are inherited, deciding what traits we express.

Central Dogma of Life

  • Describes the flow of genetic information.
  • DNA makes RNA, and RNA makes protein.
  • Fundamental concept in molecular biology.

🎙️ The Central Dogma explains how information stored in our DNA is ultimately used to build proteins, which are the workhorses of our cells. It's a three-step process: DNA to RNA, and then RNA to Protein. Let's look at each step.

Step 1: Transcription

  • Process of synthesizing RNA from a DNA template.
  • Occurs in the nucleus of eukaryotic cells.
  • Messenger RNA (mRNA) carries the genetic message.

🎙️ Transcription is like making a copy of a specific recipe from a large cookbook. Here, a segment of DNA is copied into an mRNA molecule. This mRNA then carries the genetic instructions out of the nucleus.

Step 2: Translation

  • Process of synthesizing proteins from mRNA.
  • Occurs on ribosomes in the cytoplasm.
  • mRNA codons are read to assemble amino acids.

🎙️ Translation is where the mRNA message is 'translated' into a sequence of amino acids, forming a protein. Ribosomes act as factories, reading the mRNA code and linking amino acids together to create the specific protein needed.

Step 3: Translocation

  • Movement of the ribosome along the mRNA strand.
  • Each movement exposes a new codon for translation.
  • Ensures continuous protein synthesis.

🎙️ Translocation is the final part of protein synthesis, where the ribosome literally shifts along the mRNA molecule. This movement is crucial as it allows new codons to be exposed, ensuring that the protein chain continues to grow correctly.

What are Mutations?

  • Sudden, heritable changes in the genetic material.
  • Can occur spontaneously or be induced by mutagens.
  • Can be beneficial, harmful, or neutral.

🎙️ Sometimes, there are accidental changes in the DNA sequence, like a typo in our instruction manual. These changes are called mutations. They can lead to new traits or, in some cases, genetic disorders.

💥
Genetic Change

Types of Mutations: An Example

  • Point mutations: Change in a single nucleotide base.
  • Chromosomal mutations: Changes in chromosome structure or number.
  • Example: Sickle cell anemia is caused by a point mutation.

🎙️ Mutations can range from very small changes, like a single letter alteration in the DNA code (point mutation), to large-scale changes involving entire chromosomes. Sickle cell anemia is a classic example of a disease caused by a single point mutation.

Genetic Disorders

  • Diseases caused by abnormalities in an individual's DNA.
  • Can be inherited from parents or arise from new mutations.
  • Examples: Sickle cell anemia, Down syndrome, Haemophilia.

🎙️ When mutations lead to health problems, they are called genetic disorders. These conditions can be passed down through families or can appear spontaneously. Understanding them helps in diagnosis and management.

Introduction to Evolution

  • The gradual process of change in living organisms over successive generations.
  • Leads to diversity of life forms on Earth.
  • Driven by natural selection and other factors.

🎙️ Now, let's shift our focus to evolution. Evolution is about how life has changed and diversified over millions of years, leading to the incredible variety of plants and animals we see today. It's a slow, continuous process.

📈

Evidences of Evolution: Morphological

  • Similarities in external features among different organisms.
  • Suggests a common ancestry.
  • Example: Similar body plans of a dog, cat, and fox.

🎙️ One way we know evolution happens is by looking at the outward appearance of different animals. For instance, the paws of a dog, a cat, and a fox, while slightly different, share a fundamental structure, indicating they might have evolved from a common ancestor.

🐾🐾🐾

Evidences of Evolution: Anatomical

  • Similarities in internal structures (bones, organs).
  • Homologous structures: same basic structure, different functions.
  • Example: Forelimbs of humans, bats, and whales have similar bone patterns.

🎙️ Beyond external looks, the internal anatomy also provides strong evidence. The bone structure in the forelimbs of a human, a bat's wing, and a whale's flipper are surprisingly similar, even though they are used for very different purposes. This points to a shared evolutionary past.

🦴
Homologous Bones

Evidences of Evolution: Vestigial Organs

  • Organs that are reduced or non-functional in an organism.
  • Were functional in ancestors but lost use over time.
  • Example: Appendix in humans, wisdom teeth.

🎙️ Vestigial organs are like evolutionary leftovers. They are structures that once had a purpose in our distant ancestors but are now greatly reduced or have no clear function in modern organisms. The human appendix is a classic example.

📉
Reduced Function

Evidences of Evolution: Palaeontological

  • Study of fossils provides direct evidence of past life.
  • Fossils show evolutionary changes over geological time.
  • Example: Fossil record of horses shows gradual changes in size and hoof structure.

🎙️ Fossils are incredible snapshots of life from millions of years ago. By studying them, palaeontologists can reconstruct the history of life and see how species have changed and evolved over vast periods of time. The fossil record of horses is a well-studied example.

Evidences of Evolution: Connecting Links

  • Organisms that possess characteristics of two different groups.
  • Show evolutionary relationships between groups.
  • Example: Duck-billed platypus (mammal and reptile traits).

🎙️ Connecting links are amazing creatures that act as bridges between different groups of animals. They have features of both groups, suggesting an evolutionary link. The duck-billed platypus, with its mammalian and reptilian characteristics, is a prime example.

Evidences of Evolution: Embryological

  • Similarities in early embryonic development among different vertebrates.
  • Suggests a common evolutionary origin.
  • Example: Early embryos of fish, birds, and mammals look very similar.

🎙️ If you look at the early embryos of different vertebrates, like a fish, a bird, and a human, you'll find striking similarities. This suggests that these diverse animals share a common ancestor and their developmental pathways have been conserved over evolutionary time.

Darwin's Theory of Natural Selection

  • Proposed by Charles Darwin.
  • Organisms with advantageous traits survive and reproduce more.
  • Survival of the Fittest: best-adapted individuals pass on their genes.

🎙️ Charles Darwin's theory of Natural Selection is a cornerstone of evolutionary biology. It states that individuals with traits best suited to their environment are more likely to survive, reproduce, and pass on those beneficial traits to their offspring. This leads to gradual changes in populations over generations.

Lamarckism: Theory of Acquired Characters

  • Proposed by Jean-Baptiste Lamarck.
  • Believed acquired traits during an organism's lifetime are inherited.
  • Example: Giraffes stretching necks to reach leaves leads to longer-necked offspring (disproven).

🎙️ Before Darwin, Lamarck proposed a different theory. He thought that traits an organism acquired during its lifetime, like a giraffe stretching its neck, could be passed on to its children. While his theory was later disproven, it was an important early idea in evolutionary thought.

Speciation: Formation of New Species

  • The evolutionary process by which new biological species arise.
  • Occurs due to genetic isolation and adaptation to different environments.
  • Can happen through geographic isolation, reproductive isolation.

🎙️ Speciation is the ultimate outcome of evolution – the creation of entirely new species. When populations become isolated and adapt to different conditions over long periods, they can become so distinct that they can no longer interbreed, forming new species.

Human Evolution

  • The evolutionary process leading to the emergence of modern humans.
  • Involves a series of changes in physical and behavioral traits.
  • From ape-like ancestors to Homo sapiens.

🎙️ Our own species, Homo sapiens, is also a product of evolution. Human evolution describes the long journey from our common ancestors with apes, through various stages like Australopithecus and Homo erectus, to the modern humans we are today.

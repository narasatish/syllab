What is a Lens?

  • A transparent material.
  • Bound by two spherical surfaces or one spherical and one plane surface.
  • Refracts light to form images.

🎙️ Imagine a piece of clear glass or plastic shaped in a special way. That's essentially a lens! It's designed to bend or refract light in a predictable manner, which allows us to create images.

🎙️ Just like we have different types of mirrors, lenses also come in two main categories: convex and concave. Each type has unique properties in how it handles light.

Introducing the Convex Lens

  • Thicker in the middle, thinner at the edges.
  • Converges parallel rays of light to a single point.
  • Also called a 'Converging Lens'.

🎙️ A convex lens is like a magnifying glass. If you feel its surface, you'll notice it bulges outwards in the center. Its main job is to bring parallel light rays together at a point.

Introducing the Concave Lens

  • Thinner in the middle, thicker at the edges.
  • Diverges parallel rays of light away from each other.
  • Also called a 'Diverging Lens'.

🎙️ On the other hand, a concave lens curves inwards in the middle, like a cave. Instead of bringing light together, it spreads it out, making it a 'diverging' lens.

Lens Terminology: Optical Centre (O) & Principal Axis

  • Optical Centre (O): Central point of the lens.
  • Principal Axis: Imaginary line passing through O and perpendicular to the lens.

🎙️ To understand how lenses work, we need to know some key terms. The optical centre is the very middle of the lens. The principal axis is like an imaginary straight line that goes right through this centre.

🎯

Lens Terminology: Principal Focus (F) & Focal Length (f)

  • Principal Focus (F): Point where parallel rays converge (convex) or appear to diverge from (concave).
  • Focal Length (f): Distance between the optical centre (O) and the principal focus (F).

🎙️ When parallel light rays hit a lens, they either meet at a point or appear to spread out from a point. This special point is called the principal focus. The distance from the lens's center to this point is the focal length.

Convex Lens Ray Rule 1

  • A ray parallel to the principal axis,
  • after refraction, passes through the principal focus (F) on the other side.

🎙️ Now, let's learn how to draw ray diagrams for convex lenses. The first rule is simple: any light ray that comes in parallel to the main axis will bend and go through the focal point on the other side of the lens.

Convex Lens Ray Rule 2

  • A ray passing through the principal focus (F),
  • after refraction, emerges parallel to the principal axis.

🎙️ The second rule is the opposite of the first. If a light ray goes through the focal point before hitting the lens, it will bend and come out parallel to the main axis after passing through the lens.

Convex Lens Ray Rule 3

  • A ray passing through the optical centre (O),

🎙️ This is the easiest rule! Any light ray that passes directly through the optical centre of the lens will not bend at all. It just goes straight through without changing direction.

🎙️ When an object is very far away, like the sun, the light rays coming from it are almost parallel. A convex lens brings these parallel rays to a sharp focus at its focal point, forming a tiny, inverted image.

Concave Lens Ray Rule 1

  • A ray parallel to the principal axis,
  • after refraction, appears to diverge from the principal focus (F) on the same side.

🎙️ Now for concave lenses. The first rule states that a ray of light coming parallel to the principal axis will bend outwards after hitting the lens. However, if you trace it backward, it seems to come from the focal point on the same side.

Concave Lens Ray Rule 2

  • A ray directed towards the principal focus (F) on the other side,
  • after refraction, emerges parallel to the principal axis.

🎙️ For the second rule, if a ray is heading towards the focal point on the far side of the lens, it will bend and exit the lens parallel to the principal axis. It's like the reverse of the first rule for a convex lens.

Concave Lens: Object at Infinity

  • Image formed at F1 (first principal focus).
  • Virtual, erect, and highly diminished (point-sized).

🎙️ When parallel rays from a very distant object hit a concave lens, they diverge. But if we trace them backward, they appear to originate from the principal focus on the same side as the object, forming a tiny, upright image.

The Lens Formula

  • Relates object distance (u), image distance (v), and focal length (f).
  • 1/v - 1/u = 1/f
  • Remember the sign convention for u, v, and f!

🎙️ Just like with mirrors, we have a formula to calculate image and object distances for lenses. This 'lens formula' helps us predict where an image will form if we know the object's position and the lens's focal length. Always be careful with the signs!

Understanding Magnification (m)

  • Ratio of image height (h') to object height (h).
  • m = h'/h = v/u
  • Indicates how much larger or smaller the image is.

🎙️ Magnification tells us how much an image is enlarged or reduced compared to the actual object. If 'm' is greater than 1, the image is magnified; if less than 1, it's diminished. A positive 'm' means an erect image, negative means inverted.

Image Size

Power of a Lens (P)

  • The ability of a lens to converge or diverge light rays.
  • P = 1/f (where f is in meters).
  • Unit is Dioptre (D).

🎙️ The 'power' of a lens describes how strongly it can bend light. A lens with a shorter focal length bends light more, so it has higher power. We measure power in 'Dioptres', which is commonly seen in spectacle prescriptions.

How Rockets Work: Action-Reaction

  • Based on Newton's Third Law of Motion.
  • Hot gases are expelled downwards (action force).
  • Rocket is pushed upwards (reaction force).
  • Often multi-stage to shed weight as fuel is consumed.

🎙️ A rocket's operation is a brilliant application of Newton's Third Law: 'For every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction.' The rocket expels hot gases downwards at high velocity, and in return, an equal and opposite force pushes the rocket upwards. Multi-stage rockets make this process more efficient by dropping empty fuel tanks.

Low Earth Orbit (LEO)

  • Altitude: 160 km to 2,000 km above Earth.
  • Satellites travel very fast (around 27,000 km/h).
  • Used by the International Space Station (ISS) and remote sensing satellites.
  • Example: ISS is in LEO, visible from Earth.

🎙️ LEO is the closest orbit to Earth, meaning satellites here experience less signal delay and can capture high-resolution images. However, they complete an orbit very quickly, often in about 90 minutes, so they don't stay over one spot for long. The ISS is a prime example of a LEO inhabitant.

🌍
Close to Earth
15 / 30

Medium Earth Orbit (MEO)

  • Altitude: 2,000 km to 35,786 km above Earth.
  • Slower than LEO, but still covers large areas.
  • Primarily used by navigation satellites.
  • Example: GPS satellites operate in MEO.

🎙️ MEO is higher than LEO and offers a balance between global coverage and signal strength. This orbit is ideal for navigation systems like GPS, as a constellation of MEO satellites can provide continuous coverage across the globe, allowing your phone to pinpoint your location.

🗺️
Navigation Signal
16 / 30

Geostationary Orbit (GEO)

  • Altitude: Exactly 35,786 km above the Equator.
  • Satellite matches Earth's rotation period (24 hours).
  • Appears stationary from Earth's surface.
  • Example: Communication and weather satellites.

🎙️ GEO is a special type of high Earth orbit where a satellite's speed perfectly matches Earth's rotation. This means it appears to hover over the same spot on the equator, making it perfect for communication and broadcasting. Your satellite TV dish points to a GEO satellite.

Space Debris: A Growing Concern

  • Non-functional human-made objects in Earth's orbit.
  • Includes spent rocket stages, defunct satellites, and fragments.
  • Poses a collision risk to active satellites and spacecraft.
  • Example: A tiny paint fleck can cause significant damage at high speeds.

🎙️ While space exploration is exciting, it comes with a challenge: space debris. These are pieces of old equipment, broken satellites, or rocket parts that continue to orbit Earth. Even small pieces can be extremely dangerous due to their high velocities, threatening operational satellites and future missions.

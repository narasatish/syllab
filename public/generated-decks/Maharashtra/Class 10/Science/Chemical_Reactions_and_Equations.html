What is a Chemical Reaction?

  • Process where new substances form.
  • Original substances change their identity.
  • Often involves energy change.

🎙️ A chemical reaction is like a magic show where old ingredients disappear and completely new ones appear! It's a fundamental process where atoms rearrange to form different molecules, resulting in new properties.

Physical vs. Chemical Changes

  • Physical: No new substance, easily reversible.
  • Chemical: New substance formed, often irreversible.
  • Look for signs like gas, heat, color change.

🎙️ It's important to distinguish between a physical change, like melting ice, and a chemical change, like burning wood. Only chemical changes result in the formation of entirely new substances with different properties.

Chemical Equations

  • Uses chemical formulas and symbols.

🎙️ Instead of writing long sentences, chemists use a concise language called chemical equations. These equations are like a recipe, telling us exactly what goes in and what comes out of a chemical reaction, using standard symbols.

Reactants and Products

Writing Chemical Equations

  • Write reactants on the left, products on the right.
  • Use '+' sign for multiple reactants/products.
  • Indicate physical states (s, l, g, aq).

🎙️ When writing an equation, we start by identifying what substances are reacting and what they are forming. Then, we use their chemical formulas and symbols for their physical states, like 's' for solid or 'g' for gas.

Balancing Chemical Equations - Why?

  • Follows Law of Conservation of Mass.
  • Mass cannot be created or destroyed.
  • Same number of atoms of each element on both sides.

🎙️ Just like in a balanced scale, a chemical equation must also be balanced. This is crucial because of the Law of Conservation of Mass, which states that atoms are merely rearranged, not lost or gained, during a chemical reaction.

⚖️
Law of Mass Conservation

Balancing Chemical Equations - Step-by-Step

  • Write unbalanced equation.
  • Count atoms of each element on both sides.
  • Balance elements one by one using coefficients.
  • Check final balanced equation.

🎙️ We use a method called 'Hit and Trial' for balancing. It involves systematically adjusting coefficients in front of the chemical formulas until the number of atoms for each element is equal on both the reactant and product sides.

Worked Example: Balancing H₂ + O₂ → H₂O

  • Unbalanced: H₂ + O₂ → H₂O
  • Oxygen: 2 on left, 1 on right. Add '2' before H₂O.
  • Now H: 2 on left, 4 on right. Add '2' before H₂.
  • Balanced: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O

🎙️ Let's balance the formation of water. We start with H₂ + O₂ → H₂O. By adding a '2' in front of H₂O and then a '2' in front of H₂, we ensure that we have 4 hydrogen atoms and 2 oxygen atoms on both sides, thus balancing the equation.

Types of Chemical Reactions

  • Reactions are categorized based on how atoms rearrange.
  • Helps predict products and understand mechanisms.
  • Four main types: Combination, Decomposition, Displacement, Double Displacement.

🎙️ Just like there are different types of sports, there are different types of chemical reactions. Classifying them helps us understand their general patterns and predict what might happen when certain chemicals mix.

Type 1: Combination Reaction

  • Two or more reactants combine.
  • Form a single product.
  • General form: A + B → AB

🎙️ In a combination reaction, two or more simpler substances come together to form one more complex substance. Think of it as putting two puzzle pieces together to form a single, larger picture.

➕
Joining together

Real-life Example: Combination Reaction

  • Burning of coal: C(s) + O₂(g) → CO₂(g)
  • Formation of water: 2H₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2H₂O(l)
  • Quicklime reacting with water: CaO(s) + H₂O(l) → Ca(OH)₂(aq)

🎙️ A common example is when quicklime, calcium oxide, reacts with water to form slaked lime, calcium hydroxide. This reaction releases a lot of heat and is used in whitewashing walls.

Type 2: Decomposition Reaction

  • Single reactant breaks down.
  • Forms two or more simpler products.
  • General form: AB → A + B
  • Often requires energy (heat, light, electricity).

🎙️ Decomposition reactions are the opposite of combination reactions. Here, one compound breaks down into two or more simpler substances, usually requiring energy in the form of heat, light, or electricity to initiate the breakdown.

➖
Breaking apart

Real-life Example: Decomposition Reaction

  • Decomposition of calcium carbonate (limestone): CaCO₃(s) → CaO(s) + CO₂(g)
  • Electrolysis of water: 2H₂O(l) → 2H₂(g) + O₂(g)
  • Silver chloride decomposition by light: 2AgCl(s) → 2Ag(s) + Cl₂(g)

🎙️ A great example is the electrolysis of water, where electricity is passed through water to break it down into hydrogen and oxygen gases. This shows how energy can be used to split compounds.

Type 3: Displacement Reaction

  • More reactive element displaces less reactive element.
  • From its compound in solution.
  • General form: A + BC → AC + B

🎙️ Imagine a stronger person taking the seat of a weaker person. In chemistry, a more reactive element will 'displace' or push out a less reactive element from its compound, forming a new compound and leaving the displaced element free.

➡️
One takes another's place

Real-life Example: Displacement Reaction

  • Iron nail in copper sulphate solution: Fe(s) + CuSO₄(aq) → FeSO₄(aq) + Cu(s)
  • Zinc granules in dilute sulphuric acid: Zn(s) + H₂SO₄(aq) → ZnSO₄(aq) + H₂(g)

🎙️ When you put an iron nail into a blue copper sulphate solution, the iron, being more reactive than copper, displaces the copper. The nail gets a reddish-brown coating of copper, and the solution turns light green due to iron sulphate.

Type 4: Double Displacement Reaction

  • Exchange of ions between two compounds.
  • Forms two new compounds.
  • General form: AB + CD → AD + CB

🎙️ In a double displacement reaction, it's like two couples swapping partners. The positive and negative ions of two different ionic compounds switch places, leading to the formation of two entirely new compounds.

↔️
Partners swapping

Real-life Example: Double Displacement Reaction

  • Sodium sulphate and barium chloride: Na₂SO₄(aq) + BaCl₂(aq) → BaSO₄(s) + 2NaCl(aq)
  • Lead nitrate and potassium iodide: Pb(NO₃)₂(aq) + 2KI(aq) → PbI₂(s) + 2KNO₃(aq)

🎙️ A classic example is the reaction between sodium sulphate and barium chloride. They exchange ions to form barium sulphate, which is an insoluble white precipitate, and sodium chloride. This often results in a cloudy mixture.

Oxidation and Reduction

  • Oxidation: Gain of oxygen or loss of hydrogen.
  • Reduction: Loss of oxygen or gain of hydrogen.
  • Can also be defined by electron transfer (loss/gain).

🎙️ These are two very important concepts in chemistry. Simply put, oxidation is when a substance gains oxygen or loses hydrogen, while reduction is the opposite. They often occur together.

⚡
Electron movement

Redox Reactions

  • Reactions where oxidation and reduction occur simultaneously.
  • One substance gets oxidized, another gets reduced.
  • Example: CuO + H₂ → Cu + H₂O (CuO is reduced, H₂ is oxidized).

🎙️ Redox reactions are everywhere, from the rusting of iron to the functioning of batteries. It's a coupled process; you can't have one without the other, as electrons are always transferred from one substance to another.

Redox reactions involve electron transfer between reactants. Oxidation = loss of electrons

The Gravitational Force Formula

  • F = G(m₁m₂)/r²
  • F: Gravitational Force
  • m₁, m₂: Masses of objects
  • r: Distance between centers

🎙️ This formula helps us calculate the gravitational force. Here, 'F' is the gravitational force, 'm₁' and 'm₂' are the masses of the two objects, 'r' is the distance between their centers, and 'G' is the universal gravitational constant.

➗
F = Gm₁m₂/r²

Understanding the Gravitational Constant (G)

  • G is a universal constant.
  • Value: 6.674 × 10⁻¹¹ Nm²/kg².
  • Makes the gravitational equation work.

🎙️ The universal gravitational constant 'G' is a fixed value that makes the equation work. It ensures that the calculated force matches the observed force. Its value is very small, indicating that gravitational force is weak unless at least one of the masses is very large.

🔢
G = 6.674 × 10⁻¹¹ Nm²/kg²

What Affects Gravitational Force?

  • Greater distance means weaker force (inverse square law).

🎙️ The gravitational force increases with the increase in the masses of the objects. Conversely, it decreases rapidly as the distance between the objects increases. This inverse square relationship with distance is very important.

What is Free Fall?

  • Object falling under gravity's influence only.
  • No air resistance or other forces.
  • All objects accelerate at the same rate in a vacuum.

🎙️ When an object falls solely under the influence of gravity, with no other forces like air resistance acting on it, it is said to be in free fall. In a vacuum, all objects in free fall accelerate at the same rate, regardless of their mass.

The Value of 'g' on Earth

  • Average value: approximately 9.8 m/s².
  • Velocity increases by 9.8 m/s every second.
  • Can vary slightly by location.

🎙️ On the surface of the Earth, the average value of 'g' is approximately 9.8 m/s². This means that for every second an object is in free fall, its velocity increases by 9.8 meters per second. This value can vary slightly depending on location.

🌍
g ≈ 9.8 m/s²
13 / 25

Factors Affecting the Value of 'g'

  • Earth's shape: greater at poles, less at equator.
  • Height: decreases with increasing altitude.
  • Depth: decreases with increasing depth below surface.

🎙️ The value of 'g' is not constant everywhere on Earth. It is slightly greater at the poles than at the equator due to the Earth's slightly flattened shape. It also decreases with increasing height above the Earth's surface and with increasing depth below it.

Mass vs. Weight: What's the Difference?

  • Mass: amount of matter, constant everywhere (kg).
  • Weight: force of gravity on mass (W=mg), changes with 'g' (N).

🎙️ Mass is the amount of matter in an object and remains constant everywhere. Weight, however, is the force of gravity acting on an object's mass (W = mg), and it changes depending on the value of 'g' at a particular location.

Kepler's First Law: Orbits are Elliptical

  • Planets orbit the Sun in ellipses.
  • Sun is at one focus of the ellipse.
  • Not perfect circles.

🎙️ Kepler's First Law states that the orbit of a planet is an ellipse with the Sun at one of the two foci. This means that planetary orbits are not perfect circles, but slightly elongated ovals. This was a revolutionary idea at the time.

Kepler's Second Law: Equal Areas

  • A line from planet to Sun sweeps equal areas.
  • Happens in equal time intervals.
  • Planet moves faster when closer to Sun.

🎙️ Kepler's Second Law states that a line segment joining a planet and the Sun sweeps out equal areas during equal intervals of time. This implies that a planet moves faster when it is closer to the Sun and slower when it is farther away.

Kepler's Third Law: Period and Radius

  • T² ∝ R³ (T = orbital period, R = semi-major axis).
  • Square of period is proportional to cube of radius.
  • Relates orbital time to distance from Sun.

🎙️ Kepler's Third Law states that the square of the orbital period (T) of a planet is directly proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis (R) of its orbit. This law provides a mathematical relationship between a planet's orbital time and its distance from the Sun.

📏
T² ∝ R³

Escape Velocity: Breaking Free

  • Minimum speed to escape a celestial body's gravity.
  • Object will not fall back.
  • Important for space travel.

🎙️ Escape velocity is the minimum speed an object needs to attain to escape the gravitational pull of a celestial body, like Earth, without further propulsion. If an object reaches this speed, it will continue to move away indefinitely, never falling back.

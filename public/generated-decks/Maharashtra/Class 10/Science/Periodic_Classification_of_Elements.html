Dobereiner's Triads (1817)

  • Proposed by Johann Dobereiner.
  • Grouped elements in sets of three, called 'triads'.
  • The atomic mass of the middle element was roughly the average of the other two.

🎙️ Dobereiner was one of the first to notice a numerical pattern. He found that if you took three elements with similar properties, the one in the middle had an atomic mass that was almost the average of the first and third. This was a clever observation!

Example: Lithium, Sodium, Potassium Triad

  • Lithium (Li) - Atomic Mass ≈ 7
  • Sodium (Na) - Atomic Mass ≈ 23
  • Potassium (K) - Atomic Mass ≈ 39

🎙️ Let's look at an example. Lithium, Sodium, and Potassium all react similarly. If we average the atomic masses of Lithium (7) and Potassium (39), we get (7+39)/2 = 46/2 = 23. This is very close to Sodium's atomic mass of 23! This pattern was very exciting for scientists.

Limitations of Dobereiner's Triads

  • Could identify only a few triads.
  • Many known elements could not be grouped into triads.
  • Not all elements fit this pattern accurately.

🎙️ While Dobereiner's triads were a good start, they weren't comprehensive. He could only find a handful of these groups, leaving many elements unclassified. It was like having a few perfect shelves in a huge library, but most books were still on the floor.

Newlands' Law of Octaves (1866)

  • Proposed by John Newlands.
  • Arranged elements in increasing order of atomic mass.
  • Found that every eighth element had similar properties to the first.

🎙️ Newlands drew an interesting parallel between elements and musical notes. Just as in music, where the eighth note repeats the first, he observed a similar periodicity in chemical properties when elements were arranged by increasing atomic mass.

🎶
Musical Harmony

Example: Newlands' Octaves

  • Li, Be, B, C, N, O, F, Na
  • Properties of Sodium (Na) are similar to Lithium (Li).
  • Just like 'Do, Re, Mi, Fa, So, La, Ti, Do' repeats.

🎙️ If you start with Lithium, count eight elements, you land on Sodium. Both are highly reactive metals. This pattern was quite striking, and it showed a stronger sense of periodicity than Dobereiner's triads.

Limitations of Newlands' Law of Octaves

  • Only applicable up to Calcium.
  • New elements discovered later did not fit the octave rule.
  • Newlands assumed only 56 elements would ever exist.

🎙️ Unfortunately, Newlands' law had its own set of problems. It worked well for lighter elements but failed for heavier ones. Also, he made a big mistake by assuming no more elements would be found, which proved to be incorrect as many new elements were discovered.

Mendeleev's Periodic Table (1869)

  • Proposed by Dmitri Mendeleev.
  • Considered the 'Father of the Periodic Table'.
  • Arranged elements in increasing order of atomic mass.

🎙️ Mendeleev's work was a huge leap forward. He systematically organized all known elements, not just a few. His genius lay in not just arranging them, but also in leaving gaps and making bold predictions.

Mendeleev's Periodic Law

  • The properties of elements are a periodic function of their atomic masses.
  • Elements with similar properties were grouped together.
  • Vertical columns (groups), horizontal rows (periods).

🎙️ This law was the foundation of his table. It meant that as you go through the elements by increasing atomic mass, properties repeat after certain intervals. He was very careful to group elements with similar chemical behaviors, even if it meant adjusting the atomic mass order slightly.

Achievements of Mendeleev's Periodic Table

  • Systematic study of elements.

🎙️ Mendeleev's table was incredibly powerful. It provided a logical framework for studying chemistry. His most famous achievement was predicting elements that hadn't even been discovered yet, like 'Eka-Aluminium' and 'Eka-Silicon'.

Prediction of New Elements

  • Mendeleev left gaps for undiscovered elements.
  • Predicted properties of 'Eka-Aluminium' (Gallium).
  • When Gallium was discovered, its properties matched perfectly!

🎙️ Imagine finding a puzzle with missing pieces, and you can describe exactly what those missing pieces will look like! Mendeleev did just that. He left blank spaces in his table, predicting elements like Gallium (Eka-Aluminium) and Germanium (Eka-Silicon) based on their neighbors.

Limitations of Mendeleev's Periodic Table

  • Position of Hydrogen was not clear.
  • Isotopes posed a challenge (same element, different atomic mass).
  • Anomalous pairs of elements (e.g., Argon and Potassium).

🎙️ Despite its brilliance, Mendeleev's table wasn't perfect. Hydrogen, being unique, didn't fit neatly. The discovery of isotopes, which have different atomic masses but are the same element, also created confusion, as his table was based purely on atomic mass.

The Modern Periodic Table (Henry Moseley, 1913)

  • Based on Atomic Number, not Atomic Mass.
  • Proposed by Henry Moseley.
  • Atomic Number (Z) is the number of protons in an atom.

🎙️ The biggest breakthrough came with Henry Moseley's work. He discovered that a more fundamental property than atomic mass was the atomic number, which is unique to each element. This resolved many of the anomalies in Mendeleev's table.

⚛️
Protons are Key
18 / 25

Modern Periodic Law

  • The properties of elements are a periodic function of their atomic numbers.
  • This law forms the basis of the Modern Periodic Table.
  • Elements are arranged in increasing order of atomic number.

🎙️ This revised law is the cornerstone of our current understanding. By arranging elements according to their atomic number, the periodicity of chemical properties becomes much clearer and more consistent, resolving issues like the position of isotopes and anomalous pairs.

Structure: Periods (Horizontal Rows)

  • There are 7 periods in the Modern Periodic Table.
  • Elements in a period have the same number of electron shells.
  • Atomic number increases from left to right in a period.

🎙️ Imagine walking across a period from left to right. The number of electron shells remains the same for all elements in that row. However, the number of electrons in the outermost shell gradually increases, leading to changes in chemical properties.

Structure: Groups (Vertical Columns)

  • There are 18 groups in the Modern Periodic Table.
  • Elements in a group have similar chemical properties.
  • They have the same number of valence electrons.

🎙️ Now, imagine moving down a group. These elements share similar chemical properties because they have the same number of valence electrons, which are the electrons involved in chemical reactions. This is why Group 1 elements are all highly reactive metals!

Periodic Trends: Valency

  • Valency is the combining capacity of an element.
  • It depends on the number of valence electrons.
  • Trend: First increases, then decreases across a period.

🎙️ Valency tells us how many bonds an atom can form. It's related to how many electrons an atom needs to gain, lose, or share to achieve a stable electron configuration. Across a period, valency generally increases to 4 and then decreases to 0.

Periodic Trends: Atomic Size

  • Atomic size is the radius of an atom.
  • Trend: Decreases across a period (due to increased nuclear charge).
  • Trend: Increases down a group (due to new shells).

🎙️ Think of atomic size as the space an atom occupies. Across a period, atoms get smaller because the increased nuclear charge pulls the electrons closer. Down a group, atoms get larger because new electron shells are added, increasing the distance from the nucleus.

Periodic Trends: Metallic & Non-metallic Character

  • Metallic character: Tendency to lose electrons.
  • Non-metallic character: Tendency to gain electrons.
  • Trend: Metallic character decreases across a period, increases down a group.

🎙️ Metals are typically found on the left side of the periodic table and tend to lose electrons easily. Non-metals are on the right and tend to gain electrons. This trend explains why elements on the left are shiny, good conductors, and ductile, while those on the right are often brittle and poor conductors.

Recap: Journey of Classification

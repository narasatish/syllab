The Distance Formula: How Far Apart?

  • Calculates the distance between two points.
  • Essential for finding lengths of line segments.
  • Derived from the Pythagorean theorem.

🎙️ One of the first things we learn to do with coordinates is to find the distance between any two points. This is incredibly useful, like finding the length of a road between two cities on a map.

Understanding the Distance Formula

  • Consider two points P(x₁, y₁) and Q(x₂, y₂).
  • Imagine a right-angled triangle formed.
  • The distance is the hypotenuse.

🎙️ To understand how the formula works, imagine drawing a right-angled triangle with the line segment connecting our two points as its hypotenuse. The sides of this triangle are the differences in the x and y coordinates.

The Distance Formula Revealed!

  • Distance d = √[(x₂ - x₁)² + (y₂ - y₁)²]
  • Remember to square the differences.
  • Then add them and take the square root.

🎙️ And here it is! The famous distance formula. It might look a bit complex, but it's just an application of the Pythagorean theorem. Let's practice using it.

➕
Square Root Sum
9 / 24

Worked Example: Find the Distance

  • Find distance between A(2, 3) and B(5, 7).
  • d = √[(5-2)² + (7-3)²] = √[3² + 4²] = √[9+16] = √25 = 5 units.

🎙️ Let's apply the formula. Substitute the x and y values carefully into the formula. First, calculate the differences, then square them, add them up, and finally take the square root to get your answer.

Collinearity: Are They in a Line?

  • Three or more points are collinear if they lie on the same straight line.
  • Using distance formula: AB + BC = AC.
  • If true, points A, B, C are collinear.

🎙️ Collinearity is about checking if points are 'in a straight line'. We can use the distance formula for this. If three points A, B, and C are collinear, then the sum of the distances of two smaller segments must equal the distance of the longest segment.

➖
Straight Line
11 / 24

Worked Example: Check Collinearity

  • Are P(1, 2), Q(2, 4), R(3, 6) collinear?
  • PQ = √5, QR = √5, PR = 2√5.
  • Since PQ + QR = √5 + √5 = 2√5 = PR, points are collinear.

🎙️ To check collinearity, we calculate all three possible distances between the points. If the sum of any two distances equals the third distance, then the points lie on the same line.

Section Formula: Cutting a Line Segment

  • Finds the coordinates of a point dividing a line segment.
  • Divides it internally in a given ratio (m:n).
  • Used in many geometric proofs and problems.

🎙️ Sometimes, we need to find a point that lies *between* two other points and divides the line segment connecting them in a specific ratio. That's where the section formula comes in handy.

✂️
Divide and Conquer
13 / 24

Section Formula (Internal Division)

  • Point P(x, y) divides A(x₁, y₁) and B(x₂, y₂) in ratio m:n.
  • x = (mx₂ + nx₁) / (m + n)
  • y = (my₂ + ny₁) / (m + n)

🎙️ This formula helps us find the exact coordinates of that dividing point. Remember, 'm' and 'n' represent the ratio in which the line segment is divided. Pay close attention to which x and y values are multiplied by m and n.

➗
Ratio Division

Midpoint Formula: The Exact Middle

  • Special case of section formula when ratio is 1:1.
  • Point P(x, y) is the midpoint of A(x₁, y₁) and B(x₂, y₂).
  • x = (x₁ + x₂) / 2
  • y = (y₁ + y₂) / 2

🎙️ The midpoint formula is a very important special case of the section formula. If a point divides a line segment into two equal halves, the ratio is 1:1. This simplifies the section formula greatly.

↔️
Equal Halves

Worked Example: Using Section Formula

  • Find point P dividing A(1, 7) and B(4, -3) in ratio 2:3.
  • x = (2*4 + 3*1) / (2+3) = 11/5.
  • y = (2*-3 + 3*7) / (2+3) = 15/5 = 3.
  • Point P is (11/5, 3).

🎙️ Let's work through an example. Identify your points, the ratio, and then carefully substitute these values into the section formula for both x and y coordinates. Don't forget to simplify your fractions!

Worked Example: Finding the Midpoint

  • Find the midpoint of C(-1, 8) and D(5, -2).
  • x = (-1 + 5) / 2 = 2.
  • y = (8 + (-2)) / 2 = 3.
  • Midpoint is (2, 3).

🎙️ The midpoint calculation is much simpler. Just add the x-coordinates and divide by two, then do the same for the y-coordinates. This gives you the exact center of the line segment.

Area of a Triangle: Using Coordinates

  • Calculate the area of a triangle given its vertices.
  • No need for base and height directly.
  • A powerful application of coordinate geometry.

🎙️ You've learned to find the area of a triangle using base and height. But what if you only have the coordinates of its vertices? Coordinate geometry provides a neat formula for this!

Area of a Triangle Formula

  • Vertices A(x₁, y₁), B(x₂, y₂), C(x₃, y₃).
  • Area = ½ |x₁(y₂ - y₃) + x₂(y₃ - y₁) + x₃(y₁ - y₂)|
  • The absolute value ensures area is positive.

🎙️ This formula might look a bit intimidating at first, but it's just a systematic way of plugging in your x and y coordinates. Remember to follow the pattern carefully and take the absolute value at the end.

🔢
Complex Calculation
19 / 24

Worked Example: Area of Triangle

  • Find area of triangle with vertices (1, -1), (-4, 6), (-3, -5).
  • Area = ½ |1(6 - (-5)) + (-4)(-5 - (-1)) + (-3)(-1 - 6)|
  • Area = ½ |11 + 16 + 21| = ½ |48| = 24 sq. units.

🎙️ Let's substitute the coordinates into the formula. Be extra careful with the negative signs! Work step-by-step inside the brackets, then multiply, add, and finally take half to get the area.

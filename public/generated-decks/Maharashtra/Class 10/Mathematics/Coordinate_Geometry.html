Introducing the Cartesian System

  • A grid system to locate points.
  • Uses two perpendicular lines: X-axis (horizontal) and Y-axis (vertical).
  • Their intersection is called the Origin (0,0).

🎙️ Think of a graph paper. The horizontal line is the X-axis, and the vertical line is the Y-axis. This entire setup is called the Cartesian Coordinate System, named after René Descartes.

Quadrants: Dividing the Plane

  • The X and Y axes divide the plane into four regions.
  • These regions are called Quadrants (I, II, III, IV).
  • Signs of (x, y) coordinates change in each quadrant.

🎙️ Starting from the top-right and moving counter-clockwise, we label them Quadrant I, II, III, and IV. In Quadrant I, both x and y are positive.

The Distance Formula: Measuring Straight Lines

  • How far apart are two points?
  • We need a formula to calculate this distance.
  • It's like measuring the length of a segment connecting two points.

🎙️ Now that we know how to locate points, the next logical step is to find the distance between any two given points. This is where the powerful Distance Formula comes in handy.

📏

Distance on the X-axis (Horizontal)

  • For points like A(x1, 0) and B(x2, 0).
  • The distance is simply |x2 - x1|.
  • Example: Distance between (2,0) and (7,0) is |7-2| = 5 units.

🎙️ If two points lie on the X-axis, their y-coordinates are both zero. The distance is just the absolute difference between their x-coordinates. Simple, isn't it?

Horizontal distance

Distance on the Y-axis (Vertical)

  • For points like C(0, y1) and D(0, y2).
  • The distance is simply |y2 - y1|.
  • Example: Distance between (0,3) and (0,9) is |9-3| = 6 units.

🎙️ Similarly, if points are on the Y-axis, their x-coordinates are zero. We just find the absolute difference between their y-coordinates to get the distance.

The Pythagorean Connection

  • The line segment connecting the two points is the hypotenuse.
  • The legs are the horizontal and vertical distances.

🎙️ Remember Pythagoras' Theorem? It's a cornerstone here! We can always draw a right-angled triangle using the two points and their projections on the axes. The distance becomes the hypotenuse.

The Distance Formula Revealed!

  • For any two points P(x1, y1) and Q(x2, y2):
  • Distance PQ = √[(x2 - x1)² + (y2 - y1)²]
  • This is derived directly from the Pythagorean theorem.

🎙️ This is it! The famous Distance Formula. It looks a bit complex, but it's just squaring the differences in x and y coordinates, adding them, and taking the square root. Practice makes it perfect!

🧮
The formula!

Worked Example 1: Finding Distance

  • Find the distance between P(2, 3) and Q(5, 7).

🎙️ Let's apply the formula step-by-step. It's crucial to substitute the values carefully and follow the order of operations. The distance between P and Q is 5 units.

1Identify (x1, y1) and (x2, y2): P(2,3), Q(5,7)
2Calculate (x2 - x1): 5 - 2 = 3
3Calculate (y2 - y1): 7 - 3 = 4
4Substitute into formula: √[ (3)² + (4)² ]
5Simplify: √[9 + 16] = √25 = 5

Collinearity: Are Points in a Straight Line?

  • Three or more points are collinear if they lie on the same straight line.
  • Imagine three friends standing perfectly in a row.
  • How can we check this using coordinates?

🎙️ Collinearity is a fancy word for points being in a straight line. It's an important concept in geometry. We can use the distance formula to determine if points are collinear.

Straight line

Collinearity Test: Using Distance Formula

  • For three points A, B, C to be collinear:
  • The sum of the distances of two segments must equal the third segment.
  • i.e., AB + BC = AC (or similar combinations).

🎙️ If A, B, and C are on the same line, then walking from A to B and then B to C should be the same total distance as walking directly from A to C. This is the core idea behind the test.

🚶🚶🚶
Three friends in a line
18 / 30

Worked Example 2: Checking Collinearity

  • Check if points A(1,1), B(2,2), and C(3,3) are collinear.

🎙️ Here, we found the distance between each pair of points. Since AB + BC equals AC, the points A, B, and C are indeed collinear. They form a straight line.

1Calculate AB: √[(2-1)² + (2-1)²] = √[1+1] = √2
2Calculate BC: √[(3-2)² + (3-2)²] = √[1+1] = √2
3Calculate AC: √[(3-1)² + (3-1)²] = √[4+4] = √8 = 2√2
4Check AB + BC = AC: √2 + √2 = 2√2. Yes!

Special Case: The Midpoint Formula

  • When a point divides a segment into two equal halves (ratio 1:1).
  • The midpoint M(x, y) of A(x1, y1) and B(x2, y2) is:
  • M = ( (x1+x2)/2 , (y1+y2)/2 )

🎙️ Finding the midpoint is the simplest application of the section formula. You just average the x-coordinates and average the y-coordinates. It's like finding the exact center point.

🎯

Worked Example 3: Finding the Midpoint

  • Find the midpoint of the line segment joining P(2, 3) and Q(8, 9).

🎙️ This example clearly shows how straightforward the midpoint formula is. Just add the coordinates and divide by two for both x and y. The midpoint is (5,6).

1Identify (x1, y1) and (x2, y2): P(2,3), Q(8,9)
2Apply midpoint formula for x: (2 + 8) / 2 = 10 / 2 = 5
3Apply midpoint formula for y: (3 + 9) / 2 = 12 / 2 = 6
4The midpoint is (5, 6)

The General Section Formula

  • Point P(x, y) divides segment AB (A(x1, y1), B(x2, y2)) internally.
  • In the ratio m1 : m2.
  • P(x, y) = ( (m1x2 + m2x1)/(m1 + m2) , (m1y2 + m2y1)/(m1 + m2) )

🎙️ This is the full section formula. Notice how m1 is multiplied with x2 and y2, and m2 with x1 and y1. Remember to cross-multiply the ratios with the *opposite* point's coordinates.

Worked Example 4: Using Section Formula

  • Find the point that divides the segment joining A(1,2) and B(7,5) in the ratio 2:1.

🎙️ Careful substitution is key here. Multiply m1 with the second point's coordinates and m2 with the first point's. The point (5,4) lies on the segment AB and divides it in a 2:1 ratio.

1Identify (x1,y1)=(1,2), (x2,y2)=(7,5), m1=2, m2=1
2Calculate x: (2*7 + 1*1) / (2+1) = (14+1)/3 = 15/3 = 5
3Calculate y: (2*5 + 1*2) / (2+1) = (10+2)/3 = 12/3 = 4
4The point is (5, 4)

🎙️ Just like you learned to find the area of a triangle with base and height, coordinate geometry offers a way to find it using just the coordinates of its three vertices. This is very useful for any triangle.

Area of a Triangle Formula

  • For a triangle with vertices A(x1, y1), B(x2, y2), C(x3, y3):
  • Area = 1/2 |x1(y2 - y3) + x2(y3 - y1) + x3(y1 - y2)|
  • The absolute value ensures the area is always positive.

🎙️ This formula might look long, but there's a pattern! Notice the cyclic order of y-coordinates: (y2-y3), (y3-y1), (y1-y2). Remember to take the absolute value at the end, as area cannot be negative.

Worked Example 5: Finding Triangle Area

  • Find the area of the triangle with vertices (1,1), (4,5), and (7,2).

🎙️ Careful substitution and arithmetic are crucial here. Pay close attention to the signs within the parentheses. The area of this triangle is 10.5 square units.

1Assign (x1,y1)=(1,1), (x2,y2)=(4,5), (x3,y3)=(7,2)
2Substitute into formula: 1/2 |1(5-2) + 4(2-1) + 7(1-5)|
3Simplify: 1/2 |1(3) + 4(1) + 7(-4)|
4Continue: 1/2 |3 + 4 - 28|
5Final Calculation: 1/2 |-21| = 1/2 * 21 = 10.5 sq. units

Common Mistakes to Avoid!

  • Sign Errors: Be careful with negative numbers in formulas.
  • Mixing up Coordinates: Always (x, y) and (x1, y1) vs (x2, y2).
  • Formula Confusion: Double-check which formula to use for each problem.

🎙️ Many students lose marks due to simple errors. Always write down the formula first, substitute carefully, and double-check your calculations, especially with negative signs. Practice is the best way to avoid these.

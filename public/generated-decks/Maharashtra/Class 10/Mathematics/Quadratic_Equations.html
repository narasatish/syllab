Recap: What are Linear Equations?

  • An equation with one variable, highest power 1.
  • Example: 2x + 3 = 7.
  • It has only one solution.
  • Can be represented as a straight line on a graph.

🎙️ Before we jump into quadratics, let's quickly remember what linear equations are. You've studied these in earlier classes. They are like a simple balance, where both sides must be equal, and there's only one unknown value we need to find.

Introducing Quadratic Equations

  • An equation with one variable, highest power 2.
  • General form: ax² + bx + c = 0 (where a ≠ 0).
  • Here, 'a', 'b', and 'c' are real numbers.
  • It can have up to two solutions or 'roots'.

🎙️ Now, let's meet our main topic: Quadratic Equations! The key difference from linear equations is the highest power of the variable 'x' is 2. This 'x²' term is what makes them 'quadratic', and 'a' cannot be zero, otherwise it becomes a linear equation. Because of the power 2, they can have two solutions.

Key Terms in a Quadratic Equation

  • Variable: The unknown (usually 'x').
  • Coefficients: 'a', 'b' (numbers multiplying x² and x).
  • Constant Term: 'c' (the number without 'x').
  • Degree: The highest power of the variable (always 2).

🎙️ Let's break down the general form ax² + bx + c = 0 into its parts. 'x' is our variable, the value we want to find. 'a' is the coefficient of x², 'b' is the coefficient of x, and 'c' is the constant term. Understanding these terms is crucial for solving quadratic equations.

Real-Life Example: Area of a Garden

  • A rectangular garden has length (x+5) meters and width x meters.
  • Its total area is 150 square meters.
  • How do we find the dimensions of the garden?
  • This leads to a quadratic equation!

🎙️ Imagine you have a garden, and you know its area and how its length and width are related. If the length is 5 meters more than the width, and the area is 150 sq. meters, we can set up an equation. Multiplying (x+5) by x gives x² + 5x = 150, which is a quadratic equation.

What are the 'Roots' of a Quadratic Equation?

  • The values of 'x' that satisfy the quadratic equation.
  • Also called 'solutions' or 'zeros' of the equation.
  • When you substitute a root into the equation, it makes the equation true (LHS = RHS).
  • A quadratic equation can have at most two roots.

🎙️ The 'roots' are the answers we are looking for when we solve a quadratic equation. They are the specific values of 'x' that make the equation balance out to zero. Think of it like hitting a target; these are the precise points that satisfy the condition.

Method 1: Factorization

  • Split the middle term (bx) into two terms.
  • Find two numbers whose product is 'ac' and sum is 'b'.
  • Group terms and factor out common factors.
  • Set each factor to zero to find the roots.

🎙️ The factorization method relies on breaking down the quadratic expression into two linear factors. This is a very elegant method, especially when the factors are easy to spot. The key is finding the right pair of numbers that satisfy the product and sum conditions.

Worked Example: Factorization Method

  • Solve: x² - 5x + 6 = 0
  • Here, a=1, b=-5, c=6. Product = ac = 6, Sum = b = -5.
  • Numbers are -2 and -3. So, x² - 2x - 3x + 6 = 0.
  • Factor: x(x-2) - 3(x-2) = 0 => (x-2)(x-3) = 0.
  • Roots are x=2 or x=3.

🎙️ Let's walk through an example. For x² - 5x + 6 = 0, we need two numbers that multiply to 6 and add up to -5. These numbers are -2 and -3. Once we split the middle term, we can group and factor to easily find our two roots: 2 and 3.

Method 2: Completing the Square

  • Transform the quadratic equation into a perfect square trinomial.
  • Move the constant term to the RHS.
  • Add (b/2a)² to both sides of the equation.
  • Take the square root of both sides to solve for x.

🎙️ Completing the square is a powerful method because it works for all quadratic equations, even those that are difficult to factorize. The idea is to manipulate the equation so that one side becomes a perfect square, allowing us to easily take the square root and solve for 'x'.

Worked Example: Completing the Square

  • Solve: x² + 6x + 5 = 0
  • Move constant: x² + 6x = -5.
  • Add (6/2)² = 3² = 9 to both sides: x² + 6x + 9 = -5 + 9.
  • Factor LHS: (x+3)² = 4.
  • Take square root: x+3 = ±2. Roots are x = -1 or x = -5.

🎙️ Let's apply this method to x² + 6x + 5 = 0. First, isolate the x² and x terms. Then, we find the term needed to complete the square, which is (b/2)² or (6/2)² = 9. Adding 9 to both sides transforms the equation into a perfect square, making it easy to solve.

Method 3: The Quadratic Formula

  • A universal formula derived from completing the square.
  • For ax² + bx + c = 0, the roots are given by:
  • x = [-b ± √(b² - 4ac)] / 2a
  • This formula works for ALL quadratic equations.

🎙️ The quadratic formula is a fantastic tool because it guarantees a solution for any quadratic equation, regardless of how complex it is. You just need to identify the values of 'a', 'b', and 'c' from your equation, substitute them into the formula, and calculate the two possible values for 'x'.

Understanding the Discriminant (Δ)

  • The term b² - 4ac inside the square root is called the Discriminant (Δ).
  • It tells us about the 'nature' of the roots without solving the equation.
  • Δ > 0: Two distinct real roots.
  • Δ = 0: Two equal real roots (or one repeated root).
  • Δ < 0: No real roots (roots are complex/imaginary).

🎙️ The discriminant, Δ = b² - 4ac, is a very powerful part of the quadratic formula. It's like a crystal ball that tells us what kind of solutions we can expect. Knowing its value helps us understand if the roots will be real and different, real and same, or not real at all, right from the start.

Worked Example: Quadratic Formula

  • Solve: 2x² + 5x + 2 = 0
  • Here, a=2, b=5, c=2.
  • Calculate Discriminant: Δ = b² - 4ac = 5² - 4(2)(2) = 25 - 16 = 9.
  • Roots: x = [-5 ± √9] / (2*2) = [-5 ± 3] / 4.
  • Roots are x = -2/4 = -1/2 or x = -8/4 = -2.

🎙️ Let's use the formula for 2x² + 5x + 2 = 0. First, identify a, b, and c. Then, calculate the discriminant. Since Δ = 9 (which is > 0), we expect two distinct real roots. Substitute the values into the formula and simplify carefully to find the two roots, -1/2 and -2.

Relation Between Roots and Coefficients

  • For roots α and β of ax² + bx + c = 0:
  • Sum of roots: α + β = -b/a
  • Product of roots: αβ = c/a
  • These relations are very useful for checking answers or forming equations.

🎙️ There's a beautiful relationship between the roots of a quadratic equation and its coefficients. If 'α' and 'β' are the roots, their sum is always -b/a, and their product is always c/a. This is a powerful shortcut and a great way to verify your calculated roots.

Forming Quadratic Equations from Roots

  • If α and β are the roots, the equation is: x² - (α + β)x + αβ = 0.
  • First, find the sum of the roots.
  • Then, find the product of the roots.
  • Substitute these values into the formula to get the equation.

🎙️ What if you're given the roots and asked to find the quadratic equation? We can use the relationships we just learned! Calculate the sum and product of the given roots, then plug them directly into the equation x² - (sum of roots)x + (product of roots) = 0. It's like reverse engineering!

Worked Example: Age Problem

  • The product of Rohan's age (in years) five years ago and his age five years hence is 24.
  • Let Rohan's current age be x years.
  • Five years ago: (x-5). Five years hence: (x+5).
  • Equation: (x-5)(x+5) = 24 => x² - 25 = 24 => x² = 49.
  • So, x = 7 (since age cannot be negative).

🎙️ Let's solve an age-related problem. If Rohan's current age is 'x', then his age 5 years ago was (x-5) and 5 years from now will be (x+5). The product is 24, leading to x² - 25 = 24. Solving this gives x = ±7. Since age cannot be negative, Rohan's current age is 7 years.

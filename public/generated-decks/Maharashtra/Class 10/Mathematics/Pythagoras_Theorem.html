Parts of a Right Triangle

  • Hypotenuse: The side opposite the right angle (longest side)
  • Perpendicular (or Height): One of the sides forming the right angle
  • Base: The other side forming the right angle

🎙️ Every right-angled triangle has three distinct sides. The side directly opposite the 90-degree angle is always called the hypotenuse, and it's always the longest side. The other two sides, which form the right angle, are called the perpendicular and the base, or sometimes just 'legs'.

The Formula Revealed!

  • In a right-angled triangle:
  • (Perpendicular)² + (Base)² = (Hypotenuse)²
  • Often written as: a² + b² = c²

🎙️ Here it is, the famous formula! If 'a' and 'b' are the lengths of the two legs (perpendicular and base) and 'c' is the length of the hypotenuse, then a squared plus b squared will always equal c squared. This is the heart of the Pythagoras Theorem.

➕
a² + b² = c²

🎙️ A great way to understand this theorem visually is to imagine squares built upon each side of the triangle. The theorem means that if you add the area of the square built on the perpendicular to the area of the square built on the base, you will get exactly the area of the square built on the hypotenuse.

Pythagorean Triplets: A Special Group

  • A set of three positive integers (a, b, c)
  • That satisfies the Pythagoras Theorem: a² + b² = c²
  • Examples: (3, 4, 5), (5, 12, 13), (8, 15, 17)

🎙️ Some sets of whole numbers have this special property that they perfectly fit the Pythagoras Theorem. These are called Pythagorean Triplets. Knowing common triplets can save you time in calculations, as they often appear in problems.

Checking for Triplets

  • Step 1: Identify the largest number (this will be 'c')
  • Step 2: Square all three numbers
  • Step 3: Check if the sum of the squares of the two smaller numbers equals the square of the largest number

🎙️ To check if a given set of three numbers forms a Pythagorean Triplet, always start by identifying the largest number, as this will be your hypotenuse. Then, simply apply the formula a² + b² = c² and see if the equality holds true.

4Compare with largest square

Worked Example 1: Is (6, 8, 10) a Triplet?

  • Largest number c = 10
  • a = 6, b = 8
  • a² + b² = 6² + 8² = 36 + 64 = 100
  • c² = 10² = 100
  • Since 100 = 100, Yes, (6, 8, 10) is a Pythagorean Triplet.

🎙️ Let's work through an example. For the numbers 6, 8, and 10, the largest is 10. We square 6 and 8, add them up, and then square 10. As you can see, both sides equal 100, confirming that this is indeed a Pythagorean Triplet.

Converse of Pythagoras Theorem

  • If a² + b² = c² for a triangle with sides a, b, c
  • Then the triangle MUST be a right-angled triangle
  • The angle opposite the side 'c' (the longest side) will be 90 degrees

🎙️ The converse of the theorem is just as important. It tells us that if the sum of the squares of two sides of a triangle equals the square of the third side, then that triangle *must* be a right-angled triangle. This is a powerful tool for identifying right triangles.

🔄
Working Backwards

Worked Example 2: Is a triangle with sides 7, 9, 12 a right triangle?

  • Longest side c = 12
  • a = 7, b = 9
  • a² + b² = 7² + 9² = 49 + 81 = 130
  • c² = 12² = 144
  • Since 130 ≠ 144, No, it's not a right-angled triangle.

🎙️ Here, we check if a triangle with sides 7, 9, and 12 is a right triangle. We square the two shorter sides, add them, and compare with the square of the longest side. Since they don't match, this triangle does not have a right angle.

Worked Example 3: Finding the Hypotenuse

  • A right triangle has legs of 5 cm and 12 cm. Find the hypotenuse.
  • a = 5, b = 12, c = ?
  • a² + b² = c² => 5² + 12² = c²

🎙️ Let's find the hypotenuse. We have the two legs, 5 cm and 12 cm. We plug these values into our formula, calculate the squares, sum them up, and then take the square root to find the length of the hypotenuse, which is 13 cm.

Worked Example 4: Finding a Leg

Real-World Application 1: The Ladder Problem Solved!

  • Ladder (hypotenuse) = 5m, Distance from wall (base) = 3m
  • Height on wall (perpendicular) = ?
  • 3² + H² = 5² => 9 + H² = 25
  • H² = 25 - 9 = 16 => H = √16 = 4m
  • The ladder reaches 4 meters up the wall.

🎙️ Remember our painter's ladder dilemma? Now we can solve it! The ladder forms the hypotenuse, the distance from the wall is the base, and the height it reaches is the perpendicular. Applying the theorem, we find the ladder reaches 4 meters up the wall.

The 45°-45°-90° Theorem

  • An isosceles right-angled triangle (two equal angles, two equal sides)
  • The ratio of its sides is 1 : 1 : √2
  • If legs are 'x', then hypotenuse is 'x√2'

🎙️ This triangle has two 45-degree angles and one 90-degree angle, making it an isosceles right triangle. This means its two legs are equal in length. The hypotenuse is always √2 times the length of a leg. This ratio is 1:1:√2.

The 30°-60°-90° Theorem

  • A right-angled triangle with angles 30°, 60°, and 90°
  • The ratio of its sides is 1 : √3 : 2
  • Side opposite 30° is 'x', opposite 60° is 'x√3', hypotenuse is '2x'

🎙️ This is another very common special triangle. The side opposite the 30-degree angle is the shortest, let's call it 'x'. The side opposite the 60-degree angle is 'x√3', and the hypotenuse is always double the shortest side, '2x'. The ratio is 1:√3:2.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Using the theorem for non-right-angled triangles
  • Confusing hypotenuse with legs (always 'c' in a² + b² = c²)
  • Forgetting to take the square root at the end
  • Calculation errors with squares or square roots

🎙️ Students often make a few common errors. Always double-check that you're working with a right-angled triangle. Remember that the hypotenuse is always 'c' in the formula. And don't forget the final step of taking the square root to find the side length!

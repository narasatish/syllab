Defining Our Topic

  • An equation with two variables.
  • Each variable has a degree of 1.
  • Example: 2x + 3y = 7

🎙️ Formally, a linear equation in two variables is an equation that can be written in the form ax + by + c = 0, where 'a', 'b', and 'c' are real numbers, and 'a' and 'b' are not both zero. The variables 'x' and 'y' each have a power of one.

📝
Formal Definition
11 / 30

The Standard Form

  • ax + by + c = 0
  • a, b, c are real numbers.
  • a and b cannot both be zero.

🎙️ This form, ax + by + c = 0, is the standard way we write linear equations in two variables. Remember, 'a' and 'b' are the coefficients of 'x' and 'y' respectively, and 'c' is the constant term. It's crucial that 'a' and 'b' are not simultaneously zero, otherwise, it wouldn't be a two-variable equation!

Solving Pairs of Equations

  • We need two equations to find unique x, y.
  • Called 'simultaneous linear equations'.
  • Two main methods: Elimination and Substitution.

🎙️ Unlike one-variable equations, to find a *unique* solution for two variables, we usually need *two* linear equations working together. These are called simultaneous linear equations. We'll explore two powerful methods to solve them: Elimination and Substitution.

Elimination Method: Step 1

  • Make coefficients of one variable equal.
  • Multiply equations by suitable numbers.
  • Choose x or y to eliminate.

🎙️ In the elimination method, our first goal is to make the coefficients of either 'x' or 'y' the same in both equations. We do this by multiplying one or both equations by suitable non-zero numbers. Decide which variable is easier to eliminate!

✖️
Multiply to Match
17 / 30

Elimination Method: Step 2

  • Add or subtract the equations.
  • Eliminate the chosen variable.
  • Solve the resulting one-variable equation.

🎙️ Once the coefficients are equal, we either add or subtract the two equations. If the signs of the coefficients are opposite, we add. If they are the same, we subtract. This step eliminates one variable, leaving you with a simple linear equation in one variable, which you can easily solve.

➕
Add or Subtract

Elimination Example

  • Solve: x + y = 5 and 2x - y = 1
  • Notice 'y' coefficients are already opposite.
  • Add equations: (x+y) + (2x-y) = 5+1
  • 3x = 6 => x = 2
  • Substitute x=2 into x+y=5 => 2+y=5 => y=3
  • Solution: (2, 3)

🎙️ Let's see this in action. For x + y = 5 and 2x - y = 1, the 'y' coefficients are already +1 and -1. Adding them eliminates 'y', giving us 3x = 6, so x=2. Substitute x=2 back into the first equation: 2+y=5, so y=3. The solution is (2, 3).

Substitution Method: Step 1

  • Express one variable in terms of the other.
  • Use either equation.
  • Example: x = 5 - y

🎙️ The substitution method is like swapping places. First, pick one of the equations and rearrange it to express one variable (say, 'x') in terms of the other variable ('y'). For example, if you have x + y = 5, you can write x = 5 - y.

🔄
Rearrange

Substitution Method: Step 2

  • Substitute this expression into the *other* equation.
  • This creates a one-variable equation.
  • Solve it for that variable.

🎙️ Now, take the expression you just found (like x = 5 - y) and substitute it into the *other* original equation. This will eliminate one variable from that equation, leaving you with a single-variable equation that you can solve.

➡️
Plug In
22 / 30

Substitution Example

  • Solve: x + y = 5 and 2x - y = 1
  • From x+y=5, express y = 5-x.
  • Substitute into 2x-y=1: 2x - (5-x) = 1
  • 2x - 5 + x = 1 => 3x - 5 = 1 => 3x = 6 => x = 2
  • Substitute x=2 into y=5-x => y = 5-2 => y=3
  • Solution: (2, 3)

🎙️ Let's use our previous example: x + y = 5 and 2x - y = 1. From the first equation, let's say y = 5 - x. Now substitute this 'y' into the second equation: 2x - (5 - x) = 1. Solve this for x, which gives x=2. Then substitute x=2 back into y = 5 - x to get y=3. Again, the solution is (2, 3).

Common Mistakes to Avoid!

  • Sign errors during addition/subtraction.
  • Incorrectly substituting values.
  • Not checking solutions in both equations.
  • Calculation errors (e.g., multiplication).

🎙️ Even experienced mathematicians make mistakes! Common pitfalls include sign errors, especially during subtraction or when opening brackets in substitution. Always double-check your calculations and, most importantly, verify your final solution by plugging the values back into *both* original equations.

Class Interval and Class Mark

  • Class Interval: A range of values (e.g., 10-20). Lower and Upper limits.
  • Class Mark: Mid-point of a class interval. (Lower Limit + Upper Limit) / 2.

🎙️ When we group data, we create class intervals. For example, 0-10, 10-20. The class mark represents the middle value of that interval, which we often use for calculations when dealing with grouped data.

Mean for Ungrouped Data

  • The 'average' of a set of numbers.
  • Sum of all observations divided by the total number of observations.
  • Formula: Σx / n

🎙️ The mean is what most people refer to as the average. You add up all the values and then divide by how many values there are. It gives us a central value for the dataset.

➕
Sum and Divide
9 / 25

Worked Example: Mean (Ungrouped)

  • Marks: 7, 9, 5, 8, 6
  • Sum (Σx) = 7 + 9 + 5 + 8 + 6 = 35
  • Number of observations (n) = 5
  • Mean = 35 / 5 = 7

🎙️ Using our previous example of student marks: we sum them up to get 35. Since there are 5 students, we divide 35 by 5, giving us a mean of 7. This means, on average, students scored 7 marks.

Mean for Grouped Data: Direct Method

  • Used when data is organized into frequency distribution.
  • We use the class mark (xi) for each interval.
  • Formula: Σ(fi * xi) / Σfi

🎙️ When data is grouped, we can't use individual values. Instead, we use the class mark as a representative value for each interval. The direct method is straightforward: multiply each class mark by its frequency, sum them up, and divide by the total frequency.

🧮
Calculated Average
11 / 25

Steps for Direct Method (Mean)

  • 1. Find the class mark (xi) for each interval.
  • 2. Multiply each class mark (xi) by its frequency (fi).
  • 3. Find the sum of all (fi * xi) values.
  • 4. Find the sum of all frequencies (Σfi).
  • 5. Divide Σ(fi * xi) by Σfi.

🎙️ These are the clear steps to follow for the direct method. It involves creating a few extra columns in your frequency table to make the calculations systematic and less prone to error.

1Find Class Marks (xi)
2Calculate fi * xi
3Sum fi * xi
4Sum fi
5Divide
12 / 25

Worked Example: Mean (Direct Method)

  • Class: 0-10 (f=2), 10-20 (f=3), 20-30 (f=5)
  • xi: 5, 15, 25
  • fi*xi: 10, 45, 125
  • Σfi*xi = 180, Σfi = 10
  • Mean = 180 / 10 = 18

🎙️ Let's apply the direct method. For 0-10, xi is 5, fi is 2, so fi*xi is 10. We do this for all classes, sum up the fi*xi values and frequencies, then divide. The mean here is 18.

Assumed Mean Method (for Grouped Data)

  • A shortcut method to simplify calculations, especially with large numbers.
  • Choose an 'assumed mean' (A) from the class marks.
  • Calculate deviations (di = xi - A).

🎙️ The assumed mean method is very useful when the class marks and frequencies are large numbers, making direct multiplication tedious. By choosing an assumed mean, we work with smaller deviation values, simplifying the arithmetic.

🎯
Choosing a Reference
14 / 25

Steps for Assumed Mean Method

  • 1. Find class marks (xi).
  • 2. Choose an assumed mean (A) from xi (preferably middle one).
  • 3. Calculate deviation di = xi - A.
  • 4. Calculate fi * di.
  • 5. Mean = A + [Σ(fi * di) / Σfi]

🎙️ Follow these steps carefully. The key is to correctly calculate the deviations from the assumed mean. The formula then adjusts the assumed mean based on these average deviations to give the true mean.

1Find xi
2Choose A
3Calculate di = xi - A
4Calculate fi * di
5Apply Formula
15 / 25

Worked Example: Mean (Assumed Mean)

  • Class: 0-10 (f=2), 10-20 (f=3), 20-30 (f=5)
  • xi: 5, 15, 25. Let A = 15.
  • di: -10, 0, 10
  • fi*di: -20, 0, 50
  • Σfi*di = 30, Σfi = 10
  • Mean = 15 + (30 / 10) = 15 + 3 = 18

🎙️ Using the same data, let's pick A=15. The deviations become smaller. Notice that the mean calculated using the assumed mean method is the same as the direct method, confirming our calculations.

Median: The Middle Value

  • The middlemost observation when data is arranged in ascending or descending order.
  • Divides the data into two equal halves.
  • Less affected by extreme values than the mean.

🎙️ Imagine lining up all students by their height. The median height would be the height of the student exactly in the middle. It's a robust measure of central tendency because it's not skewed by very high or very low values.

Finding Median for Grouped Data

  • 1. Create a cumulative frequency (cf) table.
  • 2. Find n/2 (total frequency / 2).
  • 3. Identify the median class (first class with cf ≥ n/2).
  • 4. Use the formula: Median = L + [ (n/2 - cf) / f ] * h

🎙️ For grouped data, we can't just pick the middle value directly. We first need to find the cumulative frequencies to locate the median class. Then, a specific formula helps us pinpoint the median within that class, considering its frequency and previous cumulative frequency.

📚
Cumulative Frequency
18 / 25

Worked Example: Median (Grouped)

  • Class: 0-10 (f=2, cf=2), 10-20 (f=3, cf=5), 20-30 (f=5, cf=10)
  • n=10, n/2=5. Median class: 10-20 (cf=5).
  • L=10, cf_prev=2, f=3, h=10
  • Median = 10 + [(5-2)/3]*10 = 10 + (3/3)*10 = 10 + 10 = 20

🎙️ Here, n/2 is 5. The class 10-20 is the first one where cumulative frequency is 5 or more. So, L=10, previous cf=2, f=3, h=10. Plugging these into the formula gives us a median of 20.

Mode: The Most Frequent Value

  • The value that appears most often in a dataset.
  • Can have one mode (unimodal), multiple modes (multimodal), or no mode.
  • Useful for categorical data and identifying popular choices.

🎙️ The mode is simply the value that occurs with the highest frequency. If you ask students their favorite color, the color chosen by the most students would be the mode. It tells us what is most common or popular.

👑
Most Popular

Finding Mode for Grouped Data

  • 1. Identify the modal class (class with the highest frequency).
  • 2. Use the formula: Mode = L + [ (f1 - f0) / (2f1 - f0 - f2) ] * h

🎙️ For grouped data, the mode cannot be directly observed. We first identify the modal class, which is the class with the highest frequency. Then, a specific formula helps us estimate the mode within that class, considering the frequencies of the preceding and succeeding classes.

📊
Highest Bar
21 / 25

Worked Example: Mode (Grouped)

  • Class: 0-10 (f=2), 10-20 (f=3), 20-30 (f=5), 30-40 (f=2)
  • Modal class: 20-30 (highest f=5).
  • L=20, f1=5, f0=3, f2=2, h=10
  • Mode = 20 + [(5-3)/(2*5-3-2)]*10 = 20 + [2/(10-5)]*10 = 20 + (2/5)*10 = 20 + 4 = 24

🎙️ Here, the class 20-30 has the highest frequency of 5, making it the modal class. We then identify L, f1, f0, f2, and h and plug them into the formula. This gives us a mode of 24.

What is Carbon?

  • Carbon is a non-metal element.
  • Its symbol is 'C'.
  • It is found in all living things and many non-living things.

🎙️ Carbon is the sixth element on the periodic table. It's truly special because it forms the backbone of all organic molecules, meaning it's essential for all known life forms on Earth. From the air we breathe to the food we eat, carbon is present.

💎
Carbon Atom
3 / 25

Atomic Structure of Carbon

  • Atomic Number: 6
  • Electron Configuration: 2, 4
  • It has 6 protons, 6 neutrons, and 6 electrons.

🎙️ Understanding carbon's atomic structure helps us grasp its behavior. With an atomic number of 6, it has six protons. Its electron configuration shows four electrons in its outermost shell, which are key to its bonding capabilities.

Bohr's model of the atom proposed that electrons orbit the nucleus in fixed energy levels

Valency of Carbon: Tetravalency

  • Valency is the combining capacity of an element.
  • Carbon has a valency of 4.
  • It can form four chemical bonds with other atoms.

🎙️ Because carbon has four electrons in its outermost shell, it needs four more to achieve a stable octet. This means it can form four bonds, making it highly versatile in forming complex structures. This property is called tetravalency.

Four Bonds
5 / 25

Catenation: Carbon's Unique Ability

  • Catenation is the ability of an element to form bonds with atoms of the same element.
  • Carbon atoms can link together to form long chains, branches, and rings.
  • This property leads to a vast number of carbon compounds.

🎙️ Catenation is a superpower of carbon! Imagine tiny building blocks that can connect endlessly to form huge structures. This unique ability to bond with itself allows carbon to create incredibly diverse and complex molecules, from simple methane to large proteins.

🔗
Chains of Carbon

Real-life Example: Diamond

  • Diamond is a pure crystalline allotrope of carbon.
  • Each carbon atom is bonded to four other carbon atoms in a rigid 3D structure.
  • It is the hardest natural substance known.

🎙️ Diamond is perhaps the most famous and beautiful allotrope of carbon. Its incredible hardness comes from the strong, uniform bonds between every carbon atom, making it useful for cutting tools and, of course, jewelry.

💍
Sparkling Diamond
7 / 25

Real-life Example: Graphite

  • Graphite is another pure crystalline allotrope of carbon.
  • Carbon atoms are arranged in flat layers of hexagonal rings.
  • It is soft and a good conductor of electricity.

🎙️ Graphite, found in pencil lead, is very different from diamond despite being made of only carbon. The layered structure allows the layers to slide past each other easily, making it soft and a good lubricant. It also conducts electricity, unlike diamond.

✏️
Pencil Lead

Real-life Example: Fullerene (C60)

  • Fullerene is a crystalline allotrope, a cage-like molecule.
  • The most common fullerene is C60, also called Buckminsterfullerene.
  • It has a spherical shape, resembling a football.

🎙️ Fullerenes are a more recently discovered allotrope of carbon, named after architect Buckminster Fuller because of their geodesic dome-like structure. C60, the 'buckyball', is a perfect example of how carbon can form fascinating, stable structures.

Buckyball Structure

Allotropes of Carbon: Crystalline Forms

  • Allotropes are different forms of the same element.
  • They have different physical properties but similar chemical properties.
  • Diamond, Graphite, and Fullerene are crystalline allotropes.

🎙️ It's amazing how one element, carbon, can exist in such different forms! These crystalline allotropes showcase carbon's versatility. While their physical appearances and properties vary greatly, their fundamental chemical nature, being pure carbon, remains the same.

Amorphous Allotropes of Carbon

  • Amorphous forms do not have a definite crystal structure.
  • Examples include coal, charcoal, and lampblack.
  • These are impure forms of carbon.

🎙️ Besides the beautiful crystalline forms, carbon also exists in amorphous forms. These lack the ordered, repeating structure of crystals. They are often less pure and include everyday materials like the charcoal used for grilling or the soot from a candle flame.

Importance of Carbon in Daily Life

  • Carbon is the basis of all organic matter, including living organisms.
  • Fossil fuels (coal, oil, natural gas) are carbon compounds.
  • Plastics, medicines, and fabrics also contain carbon.

🎙️ Carbon is not just a scientific concept; it's fundamental to our existence. From the food we eat, which provides energy, to the clothes we wear, and the fuels that power our world, carbon compounds are indispensable. Life as we know it would not exist without carbon.

Carbon Compounds: Organic Chemistry

  • Carbon's ability to catenate and form four bonds leads to millions of compounds.
  • The study of carbon compounds is called Organic Chemistry.
  • These compounds are vital for life and industries.

🎙️ Because carbon can bond with itself and many other elements in so many ways, it forms an incredible diversity of compounds. This vast field of study, organic chemistry, is dedicated to understanding these carbon-based molecules, which are literally the building blocks of life.

🧪
Organic Molecules

Hydrocarbons: Carbon and Hydrogen

  • Hydrocarbons are organic compounds made only of carbon and hydrogen atoms.
  • They are the simplest organic compounds.
  • Examples include methane, ethane, and propane.

🎙️ Hydrocarbons are fundamental carbon compounds. As their name suggests, they consist solely of carbon and hydrogen. They are incredibly important as fuels, like the petrol and natural gas we use every day, and as starting materials for many other chemicals.

⛽
Fuel Molecules
14 / 25

Types of Hydrocarbons: Saturated

  • Saturated hydrocarbons contain only single bonds between carbon atoms.
  • They are also known as alkanes.
  • Examples: Methane (CH4), Ethane (C2H6).

🎙️ Saturated hydrocarbons are like molecules where all the carbon atoms are 'full' with as many hydrogen atoms as possible, connected only by single bonds. They are generally more stable and less reactive than their unsaturated counterparts.

🔒
Single Bonds
15 / 25

Types of Hydrocarbons: Unsaturated

  • Unsaturated hydrocarbons contain at least one double or triple bond between carbon atoms.
  • Alkenes have double bonds, Alkynes have triple bonds.
  • Examples: Ethene (C2H4), Ethyne (C2H2).

🎙️ Unsaturated hydrocarbons have 'room' for more hydrogen atoms because of their double or triple bonds. These multiple bonds make them more reactive and important in many industrial processes, such as making plastics.

🔓
Double/Triple Bonds

Chemical Properties: Combustion

  • Carbon burns in oxygen to produce carbon dioxide (CO2).
  • This reaction releases a significant amount of heat and light.
  • Example: Burning coal or wood.

🎙️ One of the most common chemical reactions involving carbon is combustion. When carbon-containing materials burn in the presence of oxygen, they produce carbon dioxide and release energy in the form of heat and light, which we use for many purposes.

Chemical Properties: Reaction with Oxygen (Oxidation)

  • Carbon reacts with oxygen to form carbon monoxide (CO) or carbon dioxide (CO2).
  • In limited oxygen, CO is formed, which is a poisonous gas.
  • In sufficient oxygen, CO2 is formed.

🎙️ Beyond simple burning, carbon's reaction with oxygen is crucial. Depending on the amount of oxygen available, it can form either carbon monoxide, a dangerous gas, or carbon dioxide, a key component of the atmosphere and a product of respiration.

Uses of Carbon

  • Diamond: Jewelry, cutting tools, abrasives.
  • Graphite: Pencil lead, lubricants, electrodes.
  • Coal/Charcoal: Fuel, reducing agent in metallurgy.

🎙️ Carbon, in its various forms, has countless applications. From the sparkle of a diamond to the lead in your pencil, and the fuel that heats our homes, carbon is truly indispensable. Its unique properties make it suitable for a wide range of uses across industries.

What is Sound?

  • A form of energy.
  • Produced by vibrations.
  • Travels as a wave.

🎙️ Sound is essentially a form of energy that we perceive through our ears. It always originates from something vibrating, meaning moving back and forth rapidly. This vibration then creates a disturbance that travels as a wave.

How is Sound Produced?

  • Objects vibrate to create sound.
  • Examples: Plucked guitar string, drum skin, vocal cords.

🎙️ Think about how you make sound. When you speak, your vocal cords vibrate. When you play a drum, the drum skin vibrates. These rapid movements push and pull the surrounding air, creating sound waves.

Sound Needs a Medium

  • Requires a material medium (solid, liquid, or gas) to travel.
  • Cannot travel through a vacuum.
  • Space is silent!

🎙️ Unlike light, sound cannot travel through empty space. It needs particles to vibrate and transfer energy. That's why space is perfectly silent – there's no air or other medium for sound to travel through.

🎙️ Waves are categorised based on how the particles of the medium move relative to the direction the wave travels. We'll look at these two types, as sound is a specific type of wave.

Understanding Longitudinal Waves

  • Particles move back and forth in the same direction as the wave.
  • Creates regions of compression (high density) and rarefaction (low density).

🎙️ Imagine pushing a spring back and forth. The coils compress and spread out along the same direction you push. This is similar to how particles in a longitudinal wave move, creating areas where they are crowded together and spread apart.

🎙️ Think of a ripple in a pond. The water moves up and down, but the wave itself travels horizontally across the surface. This 'up and down' motion perpendicular to the wave's travel direction defines a transverse wave.

Sound is a Longitudinal Wave

  • Sound waves consist of compressions and rarefactions.
  • These disturbances travel through the medium.
  • Energy is transferred, not matter.

🎙️ Now we connect it back to sound. When a source vibrates, it pushes the air, creating a compression. As it pulls back, it creates a rarefaction. These alternating regions travel through the air as a sound wave.

Wavelength (λ)

  • Distance between two consecutive compressions or rarefactions.
  • Unit: metre (m).
  • Determines how 'long' a wave is.

🎙️ Wavelength is a crucial characteristic. It's like measuring the distance from one peak of a wave to the next identical peak. A longer wavelength means a longer, more spread out wave.

📏
Measuring Wave Length

Amplitude (A)

  • Maximum displacement of particles from their mean position.
  • Related to the loudness or intensity of sound.
  • Larger amplitude = louder sound.

🎙️ Amplitude tells us how much the particles are displaced from their resting position. A bigger displacement means more energy is carried by the wave, resulting in a louder sound.

↕️
Wave Height

Frequency (f)

  • Number of oscillations (or compressions/rarefactions) per unit time.
  • Unit: Hertz (Hz).
  • Related to the pitch of sound.

🎙️ Frequency is how many complete waves pass a point in one second. A higher frequency means more waves per second, which our ears perceive as a higher-pitched sound, like a whistle.

⏱️
Waves per Second

Time Period (T)

  • Time taken for one complete oscillation or wave.
  • Unit: second (s).

🎙️ While frequency counts waves per second, time period measures the time for just one wave to complete its cycle. They are intrinsically linked, as we'll see next.

⏰
Time for one wave

Frequency and Time Period Relation

  • Frequency (f) is the reciprocal of Time Period (T).
  • Formula: f = 1/T (or T = 1/f).

🎙️ This inverse relationship is very important. If a wave has a high frequency, it means many waves pass quickly, so the time for each individual wave (time period) must be very short. They are two sides of the same coin.

🔢
Inverse Relationship

Velocity of Sound (v)

  • The speed at which sound travels through a medium.
  • Formula: v = λ × f (Wavelength × Frequency).
  • Unit: metre per second (m/s).

🎙️ Velocity tells us how fast the sound wave is moving. It's directly related to both its wavelength and its frequency. If you know two of these, you can always find the third.

Speed of Sound in Different Media

  • Sound travels fastest in solids.
  • Slower in liquids.
  • Slowest in gases.

🎙️ This is because particles are much closer together in solids, allowing vibrations to be transferred more quickly and efficiently. In gases, particles are far apart, making transmission slower.

🎙️ You've probably experienced an echo in a large empty room or near a mountain. This happens when sound waves hit a hard surface and bounce back to your ears, creating a distinct delayed sound.

Real-life Example: Echo

  • Standing near a large building or mountain.
  • Shouting and hearing your own voice return.
  • Used in measuring distances (e.g., depth of sea).

🎙️ For a clear echo, the reflected sound must reach your ear at least 0.1 seconds after the original sound. This time difference is what allows our brain to distinguish it as a separate sound.

Reverberation

  • Multiple reflections of sound in an enclosed space.
  • Causes persistence of sound.
  • Can make speech unclear if excessive.

🎙️ Reverberation is like a continuous series of echoes that are not distinct. In large halls, sound can reflect many times, causing it to linger. Architects design auditoriums to control reverberation for better acoustics.

Applications of Sound Reflection

  • SONAR (Sound Navigation And Ranging) for underwater mapping.
  • Medical ultrasound for imaging internal organs.
  • Stethoscopes use multiple reflections.

🎙️ The principle of sound reflection has many practical uses. SONAR devices send out sound waves and measure the time it takes for the echo to return, helping ships detect objects underwater or map the seabed.

Sound Frequency Ranges

  • Audible: 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz (human hearing range).
  • Infrasonic: Below 20 Hz (e.g., earthquake waves).
  • Ultrasonic: Above 20,000 Hz (e.g., bats, medical scans).

🎙️ Our ears can only detect a specific range of frequencies. Sounds below this range are infrasonic, and sounds above it are ultrasonic. Many animals can hear frequencies outside our audible range.

Example: Bats and Ultrasound

  • Bats emit high-frequency ultrasonic squeaks.
  • They listen for the echoes to navigate and hunt prey.
  • This is called echolocation.

🎙️ Bats are masters of echolocation. By sending out ultrasonic waves and interpreting the returning echoes, they can create a detailed 'sound map' of their surroundings, even in complete darkness.

Recap: Study of Sound

  • Sound is energy from vibrations, travels as longitudinal waves.
  • Key characteristics: Wavelength, Amplitude, Frequency, Time Period, Velocity.

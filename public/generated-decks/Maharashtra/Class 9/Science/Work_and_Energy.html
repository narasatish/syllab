Work: Everyday vs. Scientific Meaning

  • In daily life, 'work' is any activity requiring effort.
  • Scientifically, 'work' has a very specific definition.
  • It's not just about effort, but also about result.

🎙️ Imagine studying for hours for your exams – you'd say you did a lot of 'work', right? But in physics, if you push a wall with all your might and it doesn't move, no work is done. This is the key difference we'll explore.

Work: Force and Displacement

  • Work is done when a force causes an object to move.
  • The object must move in the direction of the force (or a component of it).
  • No movement, no work.

🎙️ For work to be done in science, two things are essential: a force must be applied, and the object must move because of that force. If you push a heavy box and it slides across the floor, work is done. If it stays put, no work.

➡️
Movement is Key

Conditions for Work to be Done

  • 1. A force must act on an object.
  • 2. The object must be displaced from its initial position.
  • 3. The displacement must be in the direction of the force (or its component).

🎙️ Think of it like this: You apply force to open a door. The door moves (displacement). So, work is done. If you just lean on the door without it moving, even with force, no work is done scientifically.

Force & Motion

Calculating Work: The Formula

  • Work (W) is the product of force (F) and displacement (S).
  • W = F × S
  • This formula applies when force and displacement are in the same direction.

🎙️ This simple formula is your key to calculating work. Just multiply the force applied by the distance the object moves in the direction of that force. Remember, force is measured in Newtons and displacement in meters.

➗
W = F × S
8 / 30

Units of Work: Joule and Newton-meter

  • The SI unit of work is the Joule (J).
  • 1 Joule is the work done when a force of 1 Newton moves an object by 1 meter.
  • So, 1 J = 1 N × 1 m (Newton-meter).

🎙️ Just like we measure length in meters or time in seconds, work has its own unit: the Joule. It's named after the famous scientist James Prescott Joule. A Joule is a relatively small amount of work, so you'll often see kilojoules too.

Example: Pushing a Trolley

  • A boy pushes a trolley with a force of 50 N.
  • The trolley moves a distance of 10 m.
  • Calculate the work done by the boy.

🎙️ Let's apply our new formula! Here, the force is 50 Newtons and the displacement is 10 meters. By simply multiplying these values, we find that the work done is 500 Joules. Always remember to write down the units!

1Given: F=50N, S=10m
2Formula: W = F × S
3Calculation: W = 50 N × 10 m
4Answer: W = 500 J

Types of Work: Positive, Negative, Zero

  • Work is not always straightforward.
  • Depending on the direction of force and displacement, work can be positive, negative, or even zero.
  • This concept is crucial for understanding energy changes.

🎙️ Work isn't just a single idea; it has different types! Imagine pushing a box – that's one type of work. But what about the friction trying to stop it? Or just holding a heavy bag without moving? Each of these represents a different 'type' of work.

Positive Work: Force and Displacement Align

  • When the force and displacement are in the same direction.
  • The object gains energy or its speed increases.
  • Example: Pushing a swing forward.

🎙️ Positive work is the most common type we think of. When you push a car to start it, and it moves forward, you are doing positive work. The force you apply helps the car move in the direction you want it to go.

Negative Work: Opposing Force

  • When the force acts opposite to the direction of displacement.
  • The object loses energy or slows down.
  • Example: Friction acting on a rolling ball.

🎙️ Negative work happens when a force tries to stop or slow down an object. Think about applying brakes in a car; the braking force acts opposite to the car's motion, doing negative work. Friction is another great example of a force doing negative work.

Zero Work: No Movement or Perpendicular Force

  • 1. No displacement (e.g., pushing a wall).
  • 2. Force is perpendicular to displacement (e.g., carrying a bag horizontally).
  • 3. No force acting (e.g., object moving at constant velocity in space).

🎙️ Zero work can occur in a few scenarios. If you push something but it doesn't move, no work. Also, if the force is at a 90-degree angle to the direction of motion, like a coolie carrying a load on his head and walking horizontally, no work is done by gravity or by the coolie's upward force on the load in the direction of motion.

Energy: The Ability to Do Work

  • Energy is the capacity of a body to do work.
  • If a body has energy, it can exert a force and cause displacement.
  • Work and energy are closely related and have the same units (Joules).

🎙️ Just like a car needs fuel to move, you need energy to do work! Energy is simply the ability to perform work. Without energy, no work can be done. They are two sides of the same coin, both measured in Joules.

Kinetic Energy: Energy of Motion

  • The energy possessed by an object due to its motion.
  • Any moving object has kinetic energy.
  • Example: A moving car, a flying bird, flowing water.

🎙️ Think about a cricket ball flying towards the batsman. That ball has energy because it's moving – that's kinetic energy! The faster an object moves and the heavier it is, the more kinetic energy it has.

🏃‍♂️
Energy in Motion
19 / 30

Calculating Kinetic Energy

  • Kinetic Energy (KE) depends on mass (m) and velocity (v).
  • KE = ½ mv²
  • Mass is in kg, velocity in m/s, KE in Joules.

🎙️ This formula shows us that kinetic energy is directly proportional to the mass and the square of the velocity. So, if you double the speed of an object, its kinetic energy becomes four times greater! This is why fast-moving objects can cause more damage.

➗
KE = ½ mv²
20 / 30

Example: A Rolling Ball

  • A ball of mass 2 kg is rolling at a speed of 5 m/s.
  • Calculate its kinetic energy.

🎙️ Let's put the KE formula into practice. With a mass of 2 kg and a velocity of 5 m/s, we can easily calculate the kinetic energy. Remember to square the velocity before multiplying. The ball has 25 Joules of kinetic energy.

1Given: m=2kg, v=5m/s
2Formula: KE = ½ mv²
3Calculation: KE = ½ × 2 × (5)²
4Answer: KE = 25 J

Potential Energy: Stored Energy

  • The energy possessed by an object due to its position or state.
  • It's 'stored' energy, ready to be converted into other forms.
  • Example: Water in an overhead tank, a stretched rubber band.

🎙️ Potential energy is like a savings account for energy. Water stored in a dam has potential energy due to its height. When released, this potential energy converts into kinetic energy to turn turbines. A stretched bow also has potential energy, ready to launch an arrow.

↕️
Energy of Position

Calculating Potential Energy (Gravitational)

  • Gravitational Potential Energy (PE) depends on mass (m), gravity (g), and height (h).
  • PE = mgh
  • g is acceleration due to gravity (approx. 9.8 m/s² on Earth).

🎙️ For objects near the Earth's surface, potential energy is primarily due to gravity. The higher an object is, the more potential energy it has. 'g' is a constant value for Earth, usually taken as 9.8 m/s² or sometimes 10 m/s² for simpler calculations.

➗
PE = mgh
23 / 30

Example: Lifting a Book

  • A book of mass 0.5 kg is lifted to a height of 2 m.
  • Calculate the potential energy gained by the book (g = 10 m/s²).

🎙️ Here's how to calculate potential energy. Notice that even a small book can store some potential energy when lifted. If we drop this book, its potential energy will convert into kinetic energy as it falls.

1Given: m=0.5kg, h=2m, g=10m/s²
2Formula: PE = mgh
3Calculation: PE = 0.5 × 10 × 2
4Answer: PE = 10 J

Law of Conservation of Energy

  • Energy can neither be created nor destroyed.
  • It can only be transformed from one form to another.
  • The total energy of an isolated system remains constant.

🎙️ This is one of the most fundamental laws in physics! It means the total amount of energy in the universe is always the same. Energy just changes its disguise, like money changing from cash to a bank balance, but the total value remains.

🔄
Energy Never Lost

Energy Transformations in Action

  • Example: A falling ball.
  • PE at height -> KE as it falls -> Heat/Sound on impact.
  • No energy is lost, it just changes form.

🎙️ Consider a ball dropped from a height. At the top, it has maximum potential energy. As it falls, PE converts into KE. Just before hitting the ground, it has maximum KE. Upon impact, this KE transforms into sound and heat energy. The total energy remains constant throughout.

1Top: High PE, Low KE
2Middle: PE -> KE
3Bottom: Low PE, High KE
4Impact: KE -> Heat/Sound

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Confusing 'effort' with scientific 'work'.
  • Forgetting units (Joule, Newton, meter).
  • Mixing up KE and PE formulas.
  • Not squaring velocity in KE = ½ mv².

🎙️ Students often make simple errors. Remember, just because you're tired doesn't mean scientific work was done! Always check your units and ensure you're using the correct formula, especially remembering to square the velocity for kinetic energy.

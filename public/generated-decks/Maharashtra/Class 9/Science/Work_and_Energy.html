Work: Force and Displacement

  • Work is done when a force causes an object to move.
  • The object must move in the direction of the applied force.
  • If there's no movement, no work is done, even with great effort.

🎙️ In everyday language, 'work' can mean anything from studying to chores. But in science, 'work' has a very precise definition. It requires two things: a force acting on an object, and that object moving a certain distance because of that force.

Two Conditions for Work to be Done

  • 1. A force must be applied to an object.
  • 2. The object must be displaced (move) from its original position.
  • 3. The displacement must be in the direction of the force.

🎙️ Think of pushing a wall. You apply force, but the wall doesn't move. So, no work is done! Now, imagine pushing a trolley. You apply force, and it moves. Work is done! The direction of movement is crucial.

The Unit of Work: Joule (J)

  • The SI unit of work is the Joule (J).
  • One Joule of work is done when a force of 1 Newton moves an object by 1 meter.
  • Formula: Work (W) = Force (F) × Displacement (s)

🎙️ Just like we measure length in meters and mass in kilograms, work has its own unit. The Joule is named after James Prescott Joule, a famous physicist. It's a fundamental unit you'll use often in physics.

CGS Unit of Work: Erg

  • The CGS unit of work is the Erg.
  • One Erg is when a force of 1 Dyne moves an object by 1 centimeter.
  • 1 Joule = 10^7 Ergs.

🎙️ While Joule is the standard, you might encounter the Erg, especially in older textbooks or specific contexts. It's important to know the relationship between the two units. Remember, 1 Newton is 10^5 Dyne, and 1 meter is 100 centimeters.

Types of Work: Positive Work

  • Work is positive when the force and displacement are in the same direction.
  • The object gains energy.
  • Example: Lifting a book upwards, pushing a car forward.

🎙️ Imagine pulling a toy car with a string. The force you apply and the direction the car moves are the same. This increases the car's energy, making it positive work. It's the most common type of work we think of.

👍
Force & Displacement Align

Types of Work: Negative Work

  • Work is negative when the force and displacement are in opposite directions.
  • The object loses energy.
  • Example: Friction acting on a moving object, braking a bicycle.

🎙️ When you apply brakes on a bicycle, the braking force acts opposite to the direction of motion. This slows down the bicycle, meaning the work done by the brakes is negative. It takes energy away from the moving object.

👎
Force Opposes Displacement

Types of Work: Zero Work

  • Work is zero if there is no displacement (object doesn't move).
  • Work is zero if force and displacement are perpendicular (at 90 degrees).
  • Example: Holding a heavy bag still, a satellite orbiting Earth.

🎙️ Holding a heavy bag for hours might make you tired, but scientifically, you're doing zero work because the bag isn't moving. Also, when a satellite orbits Earth, the gravitational force is perpendicular to its displacement, so no work is done by gravity.

Kinetic Energy: Energy of Motion

  • Kinetic energy is the energy possessed by an object due to its motion.
  • The faster an object moves, the more kinetic energy it has.
  • The heavier an object is, the more kinetic energy it has at the same speed.

🎙️ Anything that is moving has kinetic energy. A rolling ball, a running person, a flying bird – all possess kinetic energy. It's the energy that allows them to do work, like knocking something over.

🏃
Energy of Movement

Deriving the Kinetic Energy Formula

  • Consider an object of mass 'm' moving with velocity 'v'.
  • Force (F) = ma (Newton's 2nd Law).
  • Work (W) = Fs. Using equations of motion, we derive KE = ½mv².

🎙️ The formula for kinetic energy is very important. While the full derivation involves a few steps from Newton's laws and equations of motion, the key takeaway is that kinetic energy depends on both mass and the square of velocity. So, speed has a much bigger impact!

2Use W=Fs
3Apply v²=u²+2as
4Result: ½mv²

Potential Energy: Stored Energy

  • Potential energy is the energy stored in an object due to its position or state.
  • It's 'potential' because it has the ability to do work later.
  • Common types: gravitational potential energy, elastic potential energy.

🎙️ Unlike kinetic energy, potential energy is not about motion, but about position or configuration. A stretched rubber band, a coiled spring, or a book placed on a high shelf all have potential energy. They are ready to do work.

⬆️
Stored Energy

Gravitational Potential Energy Formula

  • Consider an object of mass 'm' raised to a height 'h'.
  • The force required to lift it is its weight (mg).
  • Work done (W) = Force × Displacement = mg × h. This work is stored as Potential Energy.

🎙️ For gravitational potential energy, the formula is quite straightforward: mass times acceleration due to gravity times height. The higher an object is, the more potential energy it has, and the more work it can do if it falls.

1Mass 'm'
2Height 'h'
3Force = mg
4PE = mgh

Law of Conservation of Energy

  • Energy can neither be created nor destroyed.
  • It can only be transformed from one form to another.
  • The total energy in an isolated system remains constant.

🎙️ This is one of the most fundamental laws in physics. It means that the total amount of energy in the universe is always the same. We simply change its form, like from chemical to electrical, or potential to kinetic.

Power: Rate of Doing Work

  • Power is the rate at which work is done or energy is transferred.
  • It tells us how quickly work is performed.
  • Formula: Power (P) = Work (W) / Time (t)

🎙️ Imagine two people lifting the same heavy box to the same height. If one person does it in 10 seconds and the other in 20 seconds, both do the same amount of work. But the first person has more power because they did the work faster.

The Unit of Power: Watt (W)

  • The SI unit of power is the Watt (W).
  • One Watt is defined as the rate of doing work at one Joule per second (1 J/s).
  • Other units: Kilowatt (kW), Megawatt (MW), Horsepower (hp).

🎙️ The Watt is named after James Watt, who improved the steam engine. You see 'Watt' on light bulbs and electrical appliances. A 60-Watt bulb uses 60 Joules of electrical energy every second to produce light and heat.

Chapter Recap: Work and Energy

  • Work is done when force causes displacement in its direction.
  • Energy is the capacity to do work, existing in many forms.
  • Kinetic energy (½mv²) is due to motion; Potential energy (mgh) is due to position.

  • Energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transformed.

What is Motion?

  • An object is in motion if its position changes over time.
  • Motion is relative – it depends on the observer.
  • Examples: A moving car, a flying bird, a spinning top.

🎙️ Motion is simply the change in position of an object with respect to its surroundings and time. Think about yourself sitting in a moving bus; you are at rest relative to the bus, but in motion relative to the trees outside.

Distance: How Far?

  • Total path length covered by an object.
  • It is a scalar quantity (only magnitude, no direction).
  • Always positive.
  • Unit: metre (m).

🎙️ Distance tells us the total length of the path an object has travelled, regardless of its direction. If you walk 5 meters forward and then 5 meters backward, your total distance covered is 10 meters.

Displacement: Where Did You End Up?

  • Shortest straight-line path between initial and final points.
  • It is a vector quantity (magnitude and direction).
  • Can be positive, negative, or zero.
  • Unit: metre (m).

🎙️ Displacement focuses only on the starting and ending points, ignoring the path taken. If you walk 5 meters forward and then 5 meters backward, your displacement is zero because you ended up at your starting point.

Start to End

Distance vs. Displacement

  • Distance: Total path covered.
  • Displacement: Shortest path from start to end.
  • Distance is scalar, Displacement is vector.
  • Distance ≥ Displacement.

🎙️ Let's compare these two key terms. Imagine walking around a circular park; your distance would be the circumference, but if you return to your starting point, your displacement is zero. Displacement gives us a direct line from start to finish.

Speed: How Fast?

  • Rate of change of distance.
  • Speed = Distance / Time.
  • Scalar quantity.
  • Unit: metre/second (m/s).

🎙️ Speed tells us how quickly an object is covering distance. If a car travels 100 meters in 10 seconds, its speed is 10 meters per second. It doesn't care about the direction.

Velocity: Speed with Direction

  • Rate of change of displacement.
  • Velocity = Displacement / Time.
  • Vector quantity.
  • Unit: metre/second (m/s).

🎙️ Velocity is like speed, but it also includes the direction of motion. Saying a car is moving at 60 km/h is speed, but saying it's moving at 60 km/h North is velocity. Direction makes a big difference!

Speed vs. Velocity

  • Speed: Scalar, only magnitude.
  • Velocity: Vector, magnitude and direction.
  • Speed can never be negative.
  • Velocity can be positive, negative, or zero.

🎙️ The main difference is direction. A car speedometer shows speed, but a GPS system shows velocity because it also indicates the direction you are heading. Understanding this distinction is crucial for advanced physics.

Uniform Motion

  • An object covers equal distances in equal intervals of time.
  • Moves at a constant speed in a straight line.
  • Its velocity remains constant.
  • Example: A train moving at a steady 80 km/h on a straight track.

🎙️ In uniform motion, an object moves very predictably. It covers the same amount of distance every second. This means its speed and direction are not changing.

↔️
Constant Speed

Non-uniform Motion

  • An object covers unequal distances in equal intervals of time.
  • Its speed or direction (or both) changes.
  • Example: A car accelerating from a stop, a roller coaster ride.

🎙️ Non-uniform motion is much more common in daily life. When you walk, run, or drive, your speed often changes, or you turn corners, making your motion non-uniform. This change in velocity leads us to our next concept.

Acceleration: Change in Velocity

  • Rate of change of velocity.
  • Acceleration = (Final Velocity - Initial Velocity) / Time.
  • Vector quantity.
  • Unit: metre/second² (m/s²).

🎙️ Acceleration is how quickly an object's velocity changes. If a car speeds up, it's accelerating. If it slows down, it's also accelerating, but in a negative way, which we call deceleration or retardation.

🚀
Velocity Change

Types of Acceleration

  • Positive Acceleration: Velocity increases (speeding up).
  • Negative Acceleration (Deceleration/Retardation): Velocity decreases (slowing down).
  • Zero Acceleration: Velocity is constant (uniform motion).

🎙️ Acceleration isn't just about speeding up. When you apply brakes in a car, you are causing negative acceleration. And if you're cruising at a steady speed on a straight road, your acceleration is zero.

Newton's First Law of Motion: Inertia

  • An object at rest stays at rest, and an object in motion stays in motion with the same speed and in the same direction unless acted upon by an unbalanced force.
  • Also known as the Law of Inertia.
  • Inertia is the tendency of an object to resist changes in its state of motion.

🎙️ Sir Isaac Newton's First Law is all about inertia. It means objects are 'lazy' – they prefer to keep doing whatever they're already doing, whether that's sitting still or moving at a constant speed. A force is needed to change this state.

🎙️ Think about why we wear seatbelts. If a car suddenly stops, your body, due to inertia, wants to keep moving forward. The seatbelt provides the necessary force to stop you, preventing injury.

Newton's Second Law: Force and Acceleration

  • The acceleration of an object is directly proportional to the net force acting on it and inversely proportional to its mass.
  • Formula: F = ma (Force = mass × acceleration).
  • A greater force produces greater acceleration for the same mass.

🎙️ Newton's Second Law quantifies how force, mass, and acceleration are related. If you push a small cart and a heavy cart with the same force, the small cart will accelerate more because it has less mass.

Newton's Third Law: Action and Reaction

  • For every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction.
  • Forces always occur in pairs.
  • Action and reaction forces act on different objects.

🎙️ Newton's Third Law states that forces never come alone. If you push a wall, the wall pushes back on you with an equal force. These forces are always equal in magnitude and opposite in direction, but they act on different objects.

⚖️
Equal and Opposite

Third Law Example: Rocket Launch

  • Hot gases are expelled downwards from the rocket (action force).
  • The gases push the rocket upwards with an equal and opposite force (reaction force).
  • This propels the rocket into space.

🎙️ A perfect example of the third law is a rocket launch. The rocket pushes hot gases downwards, and in return, the gases push the rocket upwards. This is how rockets overcome gravity and travel to space.

Equations of Motion (2nd Equation)

  • Relates displacement (s), initial velocity (u), time (t), and acceleration (a).
  • Equation: s = ut + ½ at².
  • Useful for calculating the distance covered.

🎙️ The second equation helps us find the displacement or distance covered by an object. If you know how fast it started, how long it moved, and its acceleration, you can calculate how far it travelled.

Equations of Motion (3rd Equation)

  • Relates final velocity (v), initial velocity (u), acceleration (a), and displacement (s).
  • Equation: v² = u² + 2as.
  • Useful when time (t) is not known or required.

🎙️ Finally, the third equation of motion is very handy when we don't have the time information. It directly connects the initial and final velocities with the acceleration and the distance covered.

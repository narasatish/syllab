What are Acids?

  • Turn blue litmus paper red.
  • Release H+ ions in water.

Properties of Acids: Taste & Corrosive Nature

  • Generally sour to taste (do NOT taste in lab!).
  • Corrosive: can damage materials like metals and skin.
  • Strong acids are very dangerous.

🎙️ While many edible acids are sour, it's crucial to remember that strong acids are highly corrosive. This means they can cause severe burns to skin and damage many materials. Always handle acids with extreme caution in the laboratory and never taste them unless explicitly told it's safe for consumption.

Properties of Acids: Litmus Test

🎙️ One of the most classic ways to identify an acid is using litmus paper. Blue litmus paper, when dipped into an acidic solution, will change its color to red. This color change is due to the chemical reaction between the acid and the indicator present in the litmus paper.

What are Bases?

  • Feel soapy or slippery to touch.
  • Turn red litmus paper blue.

  • Feel slippery or soapy when touched.

🎙️ While some bases are edible, like baking soda, many strong bases are just as corrosive as strong acids and can cause severe chemical burns. Therefore, never taste or touch unknown substances in the lab. The soapy feel is due to their reaction with oils on your skin.

What are Indicators?

  • Substances that change color in acidic or basic solutions.
  • Used to identify if a substance is acidic or basic.
  • Do not participate in the chemical reaction themselves.

🎙️ Since we cannot taste or touch all substances to identify them, especially in a lab, we rely on special substances called indicators. These clever chemicals change their color depending on whether they are in an acidic or a basic environment, giving us a visual cue without needing to interact directly with the unknown substance.

Synthetic Indicators

The pH Scale: Measuring Acidity and Basicity

  • A scale from 0 to 14.
  • Measures the strength of an acid or a base.
  • Lower pH means stronger acid, higher pH means stronger base.

🎙️ To precisely quantify how acidic or basic a substance is, scientists use the pH scale. pH stands for 'power of hydrogen.' This scale ranges from 0 to 14 and tells us the concentration of hydrogen ions in a solution. A lower pH indicates a higher concentration of hydrogen ions, meaning a stronger acid.

Understanding pH Values

  • pH < 7: Acidic solutions (e.g., lemon juice pH 2-3).
  • pH = 7: Neutral solutions (e.g., pure water pH 7).
  • pH > 7: Basic solutions (e.g., baking soda solution pH 8-9).

🎙️ Let's break down the pH scale: solutions with a pH value less than 7 are acidic. The closer to 0, the stronger the acid. Pure water is perfectly neutral with a pH of 7. Solutions with a pH greater than 7 are basic, and the closer to 14, the stronger the base. This scale helps us understand the nature of a substance at a glance.

Neutralization Reaction: Acid + Base = Salt + Water

  • Reaction between an acid and a base.
  • Forms salt and water.
  • The acidic and basic properties cancel each other out.

🎙️ The process by which salts are formed is called neutralization. In this reaction, an acid and a base react completely to form a salt and water. The key idea here is that the acid neutralizes the base, and the base neutralizes the acid, resulting in a product that is neither strongly acidic nor strongly basic.

Types of Salts: Acidic, Basic, Neutral

  • Neutral Salts: Strong Acid + Strong Base (e.g., NaCl).
  • Acidic Salts: Strong Acid + Weak Base (e.g., NH4Cl).
  • Basic Salts: Weak Acid + Strong Base (e.g., NaHCO3).

🎙️ While neutralization creates salts, not all salts are perfectly neutral. The pH of a salt solution depends on the strength of the acid and base from which it's formed. A strong acid and strong base yield a neutral salt. A strong acid and a weak base give an acidic salt, and a weak acid with a strong base results in a basic salt.

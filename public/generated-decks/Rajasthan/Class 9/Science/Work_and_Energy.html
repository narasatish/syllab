Work: Everyday vs. Scientific Meaning

  • In daily life, 'work' is any mental or physical effort.
  • Scientifically, work has a very specific definition.
  • It involves force AND displacement.

🎙️ When we say 'I did a lot of work today,' it could mean studying for hours or building a sandcastle. But in science, 'work' is much more precise. It's not just about effort.

Defining Work Scientifically

  • Work is done when a force causes a displacement.
  • The object must move in the direction of the applied force.
  • No movement means no work, even with great effort.

🎙️ Imagine pushing a wall. You apply a lot of force, you get tired, but if the wall doesn't move, no scientific work is done. Work requires both a force and a change in position.

Two Essential Conditions for Work

  • A force must act on an object.
  • The object must be displaced from its original position.
  • The displacement must be in the direction of the force (or a component of it).

🎙️ These two conditions are crucial. If either is missing, no work is done. Think of lifting a book – you apply force upwards, and the book moves upwards. Both conditions are met.

1Apply Force
2Object Moves
3Work Done!

Calculating Work Done

  • Work (W) is the product of Force (F) and Displacement (s).
  • W = F × s
  • This formula applies when force and displacement are in the same direction.

🎙️ So, if you push a box with a force of 10 Newtons and it moves 2 meters in the direction of your push, the work done is simply 10 multiplied by 2, which is 20 units.

Force times Displacement

The Unit of Work: Joule (J)

  • The SI unit of work is the Joule (J).
  • One Joule is the work done when a force of 1 Newton moves an object by 1 meter.
  • 1 J = 1 N × 1 m

🎙️ The Joule is named after James Prescott Joule, a famous British physicist. It's a fundamental unit that we'll encounter again when we talk about energy, as work and energy are closely related.

Types of Work: Positive, Negative, Zero

  • Positive Work: Force and displacement are in the same direction.
  • Negative Work: Force and displacement are in opposite directions.
  • Zero Work: Force and displacement are perpendicular, or no displacement.

🎙️ Work isn't always 'positive' in the sense of being helpful. Sometimes, forces can do negative work, which means they oppose the motion. And sometimes, no work is done at all, even if a force is present.

Examples of Work Types

  • Positive: Pushing a trolley forward.
  • Negative: Friction acting on a moving car.
  • Zero: Carrying a heavy bag horizontally (force up, displacement forward).

🎙️ When you push a trolley, your force and its movement are in the same direction – positive work. Friction, however, always acts against motion, doing negative work. And holding a bag while walking is an important example of zero work, as the force you apply to hold it up is perpendicular to your horizontal movement.

Worked Example: Calculating Work

  • A boy pushes a toy car with a force of 5 N.
  • The car moves a distance of 3 m in the direction of the force.
  • Calculate the work done by the boy.

🎙️ Here, we have a clear force and a displacement in the same direction. So, we simply apply the formula W = F × s. Five Newtons multiplied by three meters gives us fifteen Joules of work done.

1F = 5 N
2s = 3 m
3W = F × s = 15 J

Introduction to Energy

  • Without energy, no work can be done.
  • It exists in many forms and is essential for all activities.

🎙️ If work is the 'doing', then energy is the 'ability to do'. Everything that moves, grows, or changes needs energy. From a tiny ant to a massive rocket, energy is the driving force.

Kinetic Energy: Energy of Motion

  • Kinetic energy is the energy possessed by an object due to its motion.
  • The faster an object moves, the more kinetic energy it has.
  • A moving car, a flying bird, a rolling ball all have kinetic energy.

🎙️ Think about a cricket ball being bowled. The faster it travels, the more kinetic energy it has, and the more impact it can make. Even a gentle breeze has kinetic energy.

💨
Movement
14 / 25

Calculating Kinetic Energy

  • Kinetic Energy (KE) depends on mass (m) and velocity (v).
  • KE = ½ mv²
  • Units are Joules (J), same as work.

🎙️ Notice the 'v²' in the formula. This means velocity has a much greater impact on kinetic energy than mass. Doubling the speed quadruples the kinetic energy!

⚖️
Mass and Speed
15 / 25

Worked Example: Kinetic Energy

  • A car of mass 1000 kg is moving with a velocity of 10 m/s.
  • Calculate its kinetic energy.
  • KE = ½ × 1000 kg × (10 m/s)²

🎙️ Let's plug in the values. Half of 1000 is 500. Ten squared is 100. So, 500 multiplied by 100 gives us 50,000 Joules. That's a significant amount of energy!

1m = 1000 kg
2v = 10 m/s
3KE = 50,000 J

Potential Energy: Stored Energy

  • Potential energy is the energy stored in an object due to its position or state.
  • It's 'potential' because it has the ability to do work later.
  • Examples: water in a dam, a stretched rubber band, a coiled spring.

🎙️ Imagine a ball held high above the ground. It's not moving, so it has no kinetic energy, but it has the potential to do work if released, like falling and bouncing. That's potential energy.

17 / 25

Gravitational Potential Energy

  • Gravitational Potential Energy (PE) depends on mass (m), gravity (g), and height (h).
  • PE = mgh
  • Here, 'g' is the acceleration due to gravity (approx. 9.8 m/s² or 10 m/s²).

🎙️ This formula is specific to potential energy due to an object's height above the ground. The higher the object, the more potential energy it stores, ready to be converted into kinetic energy if it falls.

Worked Example: Potential Energy

  • A 2 kg book is placed on a shelf 1.5 m high.
  • Calculate its potential energy. (Take g = 10 m/s²)
  • PE = 2 kg × 10 m/s² × 1.5 m

🎙️ Using the formula PE = mgh, we multiply the mass (2 kg) by the acceleration due to gravity (10 m/s²) and the height (1.5 m). This gives us 30 Joules of potential energy stored in the book.

1m = 2 kg
2h = 1.5 m
3PE = 30 J

Law of Conservation of Energy

  • Energy can neither be created nor destroyed.
  • It can only be transformed from one form to another.
  • The total energy in an isolated system remains constant.

🎙️ This is a fundamental law of physics. It means we can't 'run out' of energy in the universe, but we can transform it into less useful forms, like heat, which is why we need to be efficient with energy use.

🔄
Energy Transformation

Energy Transformations in Action

  • Falling object: Potential Energy → Kinetic Energy
  • Burning candle: Chemical Energy → Light + Heat Energy
  • Electric fan: Electrical Energy → Kinetic Energy + Sound + Heat

🎙️ Think about a simple pendulum swing. At its highest point, it has maximum potential energy and zero kinetic. As it swings down, potential converts to kinetic, and at the lowest point, it's maximum kinetic and minimum potential.

Power: How Fast Work is Done

  • Power is the rate at which work is done or energy is transferred.
  • It tells us how quickly energy is used or converted.
  • A powerful machine does a lot of work in a short time.

🎙️ Imagine two people lifting the same heavy box to the same height. If one person does it faster, they are more powerful, even though they do the same amount of work. Power is all about speed!

⏱️
Work per unit time

Calculating Power

  • Power (P) = Work (W) / Time (t)
  • Also, Power (P) = Energy (E) / Time (t)
  • The SI unit of power is the Watt (W). 1 Watt = 1 Joule/second.

🎙️ The unit Watt is named after James Watt, who improved the steam engine. When you see a light bulb rated 60W, it means it converts 60 Joules of electrical energy into light and heat every second.

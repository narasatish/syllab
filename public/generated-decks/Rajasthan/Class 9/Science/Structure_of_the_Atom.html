What is an Atom?

  • The smallest particle of an element.
  • Retains chemical identity of the element.
  • Too tiny to be seen with the naked eye.

🎙️ Imagine breaking down a piece of chalk into smaller and smaller pieces. Eventually, you'd reach a point where you can't break it further while still having chalk. That smallest piece is an atom.

Dalton's Atomic Theory (1808)

  • Matter consists of indivisible atoms.
  • Atoms of a given element are identical.
  • Atoms combine in simple whole-number ratios.

🎙️ John Dalton proposed the first scientific theory about atoms. He suggested that atoms were like tiny, hard spheres that couldn't be broken down further, and that all atoms of a specific element were exactly alike.

📜

Real Example: Dalton's Theory and Water

  • Water is always H₂O (2 Hydrogen atoms, 1 Oxygen atom).
  • This fixed ratio supports Dalton's idea.
  • Atoms combine in simple whole-number ratios.

🎙️ Think about water. No matter where you find it, it's always made of two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom. This consistent composition is a perfect example of Dalton's theory of fixed ratios.

Discovery of the Electron (J.J. Thomson)

  • Discovered by J.J. Thomson in 1897.
  • Used Cathode Ray Tube experiment.
  • Electrons are negatively charged particles.

🎙️ Towards the end of the 19th century, scientists found that atoms weren't as indivisible as Dalton thought. J.J. Thomson discovered the electron, a tiny, negatively charged particle much smaller than the atom itself.

💡
Spark of Discovery

Thomson's Model of the Atom ('Plum Pudding')

  • Positive sphere with electrons embedded.
  • Like 'plums in a pudding' or 'seeds in a watermelon'.
  • Overall atom is electrically neutral.

🎙️ To explain his discovery, Thomson proposed a model where the atom was a uniform sphere of positive charge, with the negatively charged electrons scattered within it, much like plums in a pudding.

🎙️ Imagine a watermelon. The large, red, fleshy part can be thought of as the positive sphere, and the small, black seeds are the electrons embedded within it. This helps visualize Thomson's idea.

Discovery of the Proton (E. Goldstein)

  • Discovered by E. Goldstein in 1886.
  • Used Canal Ray Experiment (anode rays).
  • Protons are positively charged particles.

🎙️ Even before Thomson, Goldstein observed positively charged rays, which he called canal rays. These later led to the discovery of the proton, a positively charged particle within the atom.

Rutherford's Alpha-Particle Scattering Experiment

  • Bombarded a thin gold foil with alpha particles.
  • Alpha particles are positively charged (He²⁺ ions).
  • Observed how particles scattered.

🎙️ Ernest Rutherford conducted a famous experiment to test Thomson's model. He fired tiny, positively charged alpha particles at a very thin sheet of gold foil and observed where they went.

Observations from Rutherford's Experiment

  • Most alpha particles passed straight through.
  • A few were deflected by small angles.
  • Very few (1 in 12,000) bounced back.

🎙️ Rutherford expected most particles to pass straight through with only minor deflections if Thomson's model was correct. But the results were surprising! Most went through, but some bounced back, indicating something dense in the atom.

Conclusions from Rutherford's Experiment

  • Most of the atom is empty space.
  • Positive charge is concentrated in a tiny nucleus.
  • Nucleus is very dense and small.

🎙️ Based on his observations, Rutherford concluded that an atom is mostly empty space. All the positive charge and nearly all the mass are concentrated in a very small region at the center, which he called the nucleus.

Rutherford's Model of the Atom (1911)

  • Central, positively charged nucleus.
  • Electrons revolve around the nucleus in orbits.
  • Nucleus is much smaller than the atom.

🎙️ Rutherford's model was like a miniature solar system, with the nucleus at the center (like the sun) and electrons orbiting around it (like planets). This was a major step forward in understanding atomic structure.

Drawbacks of Rutherford's Model

  • Electron in orbit should lose energy.
  • Spiral into the nucleus, making atom unstable.
  • Could not explain the stability of the atom.

🎙️ While revolutionary, Rutherford's model had a major flaw. According to classical physics, an electron orbiting the nucleus should continuously emit energy and eventually crash into the nucleus, making the atom unstable. But atoms are stable!

⚠️
Warning Sign

Bohr's Model of the Atom (1913)

  • Electrons revolve in discrete orbits (energy shells).
  • Electrons do not radiate energy in these orbits.
  • Energy levels are fixed (K, L, M, N shells).

🎙️ Niels Bohr addressed Rutherford's problem. He proposed that electrons occupy specific, fixed energy levels or shells, and they do not lose energy while revolving in these particular orbits. This explained the stability of atoms.

Bohr's model of the atom proposed that electrons orbit the nucleus in fixed energy levels

Discovery of the Neutron (James Chadwick)

  • Discovered by James Chadwick in 1932.
  • Neutral particle, no electric charge.
  • Located in the nucleus along with protons.

🎙️ Scientists noticed that the mass of the nucleus was often more than the mass of its protons. James Chadwick discovered a third subatomic particle, the neutron, which has no charge but has mass, explaining the extra mass in the nucleus.

Subatomic Particles: A Comparison

  • Proton: Positive charge, mass ≈ 1 amu, in nucleus.
  • Neutron: No charge, mass ≈ 1 amu, in nucleus.
  • Electron: Negative charge, negligible mass, orbits nucleus.

🎙️ So, atoms are made of three fundamental particles: protons (positive), neutrons (neutral) in the nucleus, and electrons (negative) orbiting around it. Protons and neutrons contribute most of the atom's mass.

Atomic Number (Z)

  • Number of protons in an atom's nucleus.
  • Defines the element (e.g., all Carbon atoms have 6 protons).
  • Also equals number of electrons in a neutral atom.

🎙️ The atomic number, represented by 'Z', is like an atom's unique identification number. It tells us how many protons are in the nucleus, and this number is unique to each element.

Mass Number (A)

  • Total number of protons and neutrons.
  • Also called 'nucleons' (particles in nucleus).
  • Concentrated in the nucleus of the atom.

🎙️ The mass number, 'A', tells us the total number of protons and neutrons in an atom's nucleus. Since electrons have negligible mass, the mass number essentially represents the entire mass of the atom.

Worked Example: Calculating P, N, E

  • Element Sodium (Na) has Atomic Number (Z) = 11.
  • Mass Number (A) = 23.
  • Protons = Z = 11; Electrons = Z = 11; Neutrons = A - Z = 23 - 11 = 12.

🎙️ Let's apply what we learned. For Sodium, with Z=11 and A=23, we can easily find the number of each subatomic particle. Remember, in a neutral atom, protons equal electrons.

Isotopes

  • Atoms of the same element.
  • Same atomic number (Z), different mass number (A).
  • Different number of neutrons.

🎙️ Isotopes are like siblings of the same element. They have the same number of protons, so they are the same element, but they differ in the number of neutrons, leading to different mass numbers.

👯
Atomic Twins

Isobars

  • Atoms of different elements.
  • Same mass number (A), different atomic number (Z).
  • Example: Argon (⁴⁰₁₈Ar) and Calcium (⁴⁰₂₀Ca).

🎙️ Isobars are different elements that happen to have the same mass number. This means they have a different number of protons (different Z) but the total number of protons and neutrons (A) is the same.

Valency

  • Combining capacity of an element.
  • Determined by the number of electrons in the outermost shell.
  • Atoms combine to achieve stability (octet rule).

🎙️ Valency is crucial for understanding how atoms form molecules and compounds. It tells us how many bonds an atom can form, which is driven by its desire to achieve a stable electron configuration, usually eight electrons in its outermost shell.

🤝

How to Calculate Valency

  • 1. Find the atomic number (Z).
  • 2. Write the electron configuration (K, L, M shells).
  • 3. Count valence electrons (outermost shell).
  • 4. If valence electrons < 4, valency is that number. If > 4, valency is 8 - valence electrons.

🎙️ Let's learn the steps to calculate valency. For example, Carbon has 6 electrons (2 in K, 4 in L). Its valence electrons are 4, so its valency is 4. For Oxygen (8 electrons: 2 in K, 6 in L), its valency is 8-6=2.

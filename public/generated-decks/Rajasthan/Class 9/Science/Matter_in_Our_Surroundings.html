What is Matter?

  • Anything that occupies space and has mass.
  • Matter is made up of tiny particles.
  • We can perceive matter with our five senses.

🎙️ In simple terms, if something takes up space and has weight, it's matter. A chair occupies space and has mass, so it's matter. Even something invisible like air is matter because it occupies space and has mass.

Characteristics of Particles of Matter: Space Between Particles

  • Particles of matter are not tightly packed.
  • There are empty spaces, or intermolecular spaces, between them.

🎙️ When you dissolve sugar in water, the water level doesn't rise significantly. This is because the sugar particles fit into the spaces between the water particles, demonstrating that there are gaps.

Characteristics of Particles of Matter: Constantly Moving

  • Particles of matter are never at rest; they possess kinetic energy.
  • They continuously move and collide with each other and container walls.

🎙️ If you light an incense stick in one corner of a room, its fragrance quickly spreads throughout the entire room. This happens because the particles of the incense stick move and mix with air particles, demonstrating their constant motion.

Characteristics of Particles of Matter: Attract Each Other

  • A force of attraction exists between the particles of matter.
  • The strength of this force varies from one type of matter to another.

🎙️ Try to break a piece of chalk, a block of ice, and an iron nail. It's easiest to break the chalk, harder to break the ice, and very difficult to break the iron nail. This shows that particles in iron attract each other with much stronger forces than in chalk or ice.

States of Matter: An Overview

  • Matter primarily exists in three common states.
  • These are solid, liquid, and gas.
  • Each state has unique properties based on particle arrangement.

🎙️ We observe many different forms of matter around us. But largely, they can be categorized into three main states: solid, liquid, and gas. Let's look at each one in detail.

Matter exists in three states: solid, liquid, and gas. Each state has different properties

The Solid State

  • Solids have a definite shape and fixed volume.
  • Particles are tightly packed and vibrate in fixed positions.
  • They have very strong intermolecular forces of attraction.

🎙️ Think of a chair or a book. They maintain their shape and volume no matter where you place them. This is because their particles are very close to each other and held by strong forces, giving them rigidity.

🧱
A solid brick
9 / 25

The Liquid State

  • Liquids have a definite volume but no definite shape.
  • They take the shape of the container they are poured into.
  • Particles are less tightly packed than solids and can slide past each other.

🎙️ Water, milk, and oil are examples of liquids. If you pour water from a glass into a bowl, it takes the shape of the bowl, but the amount of water (its volume) remains the same. This 'flow' is due to weaker forces between particles.

🥛
Water in a glass

The Gaseous State

  • Gases have neither a definite shape nor a definite volume.
  • Particles are far apart and move randomly and freely.
  • They have very weak intermolecular forces of attraction.

🎙️ Air, oxygen, and the gas in cooking cylinders (LPG) are gases. A gas will completely fill any container it is put into, taking its shape and volume. This is because gas particles are widely separated and move at high speeds.

Gas filling a balloon

Comparing the States of Matter

🎙️ Here's a quick comparison. Solids are very rigid, liquids can flow, and gases can be compressed very easily. These characteristics are direct results of how their tiny particles are arranged and how strongly they attract each other.

Can Matter Change its State?

  • Yes, matter can change from one state to another.
  • This change can be brought about by altering temperature or pressure.
  • Think of ice melting into water, or water boiling into steam.

🎙️ We see this phenomenon all the time in our daily lives. Water can exist as ice (solid), water (liquid), and steam (gas). This shows that matter is not fixed in one state but can transition between them.

Effect of Temperature Change

  • Increasing temperature provides kinetic energy to particles.
  • Particles start vibrating more vigorously.
  • This energy helps overcome the forces of attraction.

🎙️ When you heat a substance, you are essentially giving its particles more energy. With more energy, they move faster and further apart, which can lead to a change in state, like a solid becoming a liquid.

🔥
Adding heat

Melting Point and Fusion

  • Melting Point: The temperature at which a solid melts into a liquid at atmospheric pressure.
  • Fusion: The process of changing a solid state into a liquid state.
  • Latent heat of fusion is the hidden heat absorbed during melting.

🎙️ For ice, the melting point is 0°C. Even if you keep adding heat at this temperature, the ice won't get hotter until all of it has turned into water. The extra heat absorbed is used to overcome the forces of attraction between particles, not to raise the temperature.

Boiling Point and Vaporization

  • Boiling Point: The temperature at which a liquid starts boiling to become a gas at atmospheric pressure.
  • Vaporization: The process of changing a liquid state into a gaseous state.
  • Latent heat of vaporization is the hidden heat absorbed during boiling.

🎙️ For water, the boiling point is 100°C. Similar to melting, the temperature remains constant during boiling. The absorbed heat, called latent heat of vaporization, is used to break the bonds between liquid particles, allowing them to escape as gas.

Sublimation: Direct Change

  • The direct conversion of a solid into a gas, without passing through the liquid state.
  • Also, the direct conversion of gas into solid.
  • An unusual but important phase change.

🎙️ Have you ever seen camphor disappear when left in the open? Or dry ice (solid carbon dioxide) producing a smoky effect? These are examples of sublimation, where the substance bypasses the liquid phase entirely.

Effect of Pressure Change

  • Increasing pressure can bring particles of matter closer together.
  • This can change gases into liquids, and even liquids into solids.
  • Decreasing pressure can have the opposite effect.

🎙️ Think of the LPG (Liquefied Petroleum Gas) cylinders in your kitchen. The gas inside is converted into liquid by applying high pressure. This significantly reduces its volume, allowing more gas to be stored.

Evaporation: A Surface Phenomenon

  • The process where a liquid changes into vapor at any temperature below its boiling point.
  • It is a surface phenomenon, meaning it occurs only at the surface of the liquid.
  • Unlike boiling, it doesn't require heating to the boiling point.

🎙️ Your wet clothes dry even on a cool day, much below 100°C. This is due to evaporation. Particles at the surface gain enough energy from the surroundings to overcome attractive forces and escape as vapor.

  • Surface Area: An increase in surface area increases the rate of evaporation.
  • Temperature: An increase in temperature increases the rate of evaporation.

🎙️ Clothes dry faster when spread out because the larger surface area allows more particles to escape. Similarly, water evaporates faster on a hot day because particles gain more kinetic energy.

  • Wind Speed: An increase in wind speed increases the rate of evaporation.

🎙️ On a humid day, the air already contains a lot of water vapor, so it can't hold much more, slowing evaporation. A windy day carries away water vapor, allowing more liquid particles to escape, thus speeding up drying.

Evaporation Causes Cooling

  • During evaporation, particles absorb energy from the surroundings.
  • This absorbed energy is the latent heat of vaporization.
  • By taking energy from the surroundings, evaporation leaves the surroundings cool.

🎙️ When you put some acetone (nail polish remover) on your palm, you feel a cooling sensation. This is because the acetone evaporates quickly, absorbing heat from your palm to change into vapor, leaving your palm feeling cool.

Chapter Recap: Matter in Our Surroundings

  • Matter occupies space and has mass, made of tiny particles.
  • Particles are constantly moving, have space, and attract each other.
  • Three states: Solid (definite shape/volume), Liquid (definite volume, no definite shape), Gas (no definite shape/volume).
  • States can change with temperature (melting, boiling, sublimation) and pressure.
  • Evaporation is a surface phenomenon that causes cooling.

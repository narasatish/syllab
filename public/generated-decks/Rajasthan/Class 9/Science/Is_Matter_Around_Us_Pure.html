Pure Substances: Elements

  • Cannot be broken down into simpler substances.
  • Made of only one type of atom.
  • Examples: Gold, Oxygen, Iron.

🎙️ Elements are the basic building blocks of all matter. Think of them as the simplest forms, like individual LEGO bricks that cannot be broken down further.

Pure Substances: Compounds

  • Formed when two or more elements combine chemically.
  • Have a fixed composition.
  • Properties are different from their constituent elements.
  • Example: Water (H₂O), Salt (NaCl).

🎙️ Compounds are like LEGO models built from different types of bricks. When elements join chemically, they form a compound with entirely new properties, different from the original elements.

Mixtures: Definition & Types

  • Contain two or more pure substances physically combined.
  • Components retain their original properties.
  • Can be separated by physical methods.

🎙️ Unlike compounds, in a mixture, the substances just mix together without chemical change. They keep their own properties and can often be separated easily.

Combined

Homogeneous Mixtures

  • Have a uniform composition throughout.
  • Particles are evenly distributed and indistinguishable.
  • Example: Salt solution, air.

🎙️ Imagine dissolving sugar in water. The sugar disappears, and the entire solution looks the same everywhere. That's a homogeneous mixture – perfectly blended!

Heterogeneous Mixtures

Solutions: Homogeneous Mixtures

  • A homogeneous mixture of two or more substances.
  • Particles are very small and cannot be seen.
  • Example: Sugar solution, vinegar.

🎙️ Solutions are a very important type of homogeneous mixture. They are so well mixed that you cannot distinguish the individual components, even under a microscope.

🧪
Perfectly Mixed

Solute & Solvent

  • Solute: The substance dissolved (usually smaller amount).
  • Solvent: The substance that dissolves the solute (usually larger amount).
  • Example: In sugar solution, sugar is solute, water is solvent.

🎙️ Every solution has two main parts. The solute is what gets dissolved, and the solvent is what does the dissolving. Water is often called the 'universal solvent'.

Properties of a Solution

  • Particles are smaller than 1 nm (nanometer).
  • Cannot be seen by naked eye.
  • Do not scatter a beam of light (no Tyndall effect).
  • Stable: particles do not settle down.

🎙️ Solutions have distinct characteristics. Their particles are incredibly tiny, making them transparent and stable. You can't see the path of light through them.

Concentration of a Solution

  • Amount of solute present in a given amount of solvent or solution.
  • Can be expressed as mass by mass percentage or mass by volume percentage.
  • Helps describe how much solute is dissolved.

🎙️ Concentration tells us how 'strong' a solution is. A highly concentrated solution has a lot of solute dissolved, while a dilute one has less.

Suspensions: Heterogeneous

  • A heterogeneous mixture where solute particles do not dissolve.
  • Particles remain suspended throughout the bulk of the medium.
  • Example: Muddy water, chalk powder in water.

🎙️ Suspensions are mixtures where particles are large enough to be seen and don't dissolve. Think of muddy water – the mud particles eventually settle down.

🥛
Cloudy Mix
17 / 25

Properties of Suspensions

  • Particles are visible to the naked eye.
  • Particles scatter a beam of light (Tyndall effect).
  • Unstable: particles settle down when left undisturbed.
  • Can be separated by filtration.

🎙️ Unlike solutions, suspensions are opaque or translucent. Their larger particles can scatter light, and they are not stable – the particles will eventually settle at the bottom.

Colloids: In-Between

  • Particles are larger than solutions but smaller than suspensions.
  • Appear homogeneous but are actually heterogeneous.
  • Example: Milk, fog, smoke.

🎙️ Colloids are fascinating because they seem homogeneous but behave differently. Their particle size is intermediate, giving them unique properties.

☁️

Properties of Colloids

  • Particles cannot be seen by naked eye, but visible under microscope.
  • Particles scatter a beam of light (Tyndall effect).
  • Stable: particles do not settle down.
  • Cannot be separated by filtration.

🎙️ Colloids are stable and don't settle like suspensions. However, their particles are large enough to scatter light, which is a key characteristic.

👁️

The Tyndall Effect

  • The scattering of a beam of light by colloidal particles.
  • Also seen in suspensions, but not in true solutions.
  • Example: Sunlight passing through a dusty room.

🎙️ The Tyndall effect is a cool phenomenon. When you see dust particles dancing in a sunbeam, or fog scattering car headlights, you're witnessing the Tyndall effect in action.

Separation by Evaporation

  • Used to separate a volatile solvent from a non-volatile solute.
  • Liquid changes to gas, leaving solid behind.
  • Example: Separating salt from water.

🎙️ Evaporation is a simple method. When you boil saltwater, the water turns into steam, leaving the salt behind. This is commonly used to produce salt.

Separation by Centrifugation

  • Used to separate denser particles from lighter liquid.
  • Mixture is rotated rapidly, forcing heavier particles to bottom.
  • Example: Separating cream from milk, blood tests.

🎙️ Centrifugation uses centrifugal force. When milk is spun, the denser cream separates from the lighter skim milk. It's also used in labs to separate blood components.

What is Motion?

  • An object is in motion if its position changes.
  • This change happens relative to its surroundings.
  • The change in position occurs over a period of time.

🎙️ Look around you. You might see a fan rotating, a car moving on the road, or a bird flying. All these are examples of motion, where an object's location is continuously changing.

Rest vs. Motion

  • An object is at rest if its position does not change relative to its surroundings.
  • Motion and rest are relative terms, meaning they depend on the observer.
  • An object can be at rest for one person and in motion for another.

🎙️ Think about a passenger sitting inside a moving bus. For another passenger inside, they are at rest. But for someone standing outside the bus, the passenger is definitely in motion. This illustrates the relative nature of rest and motion.

Describing Motion: The Need for a Reference Point

  • To describe an object's position, we need a fixed point.
  • This fixed point is called the reference point or origin.
  • It helps us determine if an object is moving or at rest.

🎙️ Imagine giving directions to a friend. You always start from a known place, right? That known place is your reference point. Without it, describing where something is or how it's moving becomes difficult.

Types of Motion: Linear Motion

  • Motion along a straight line path.
  • Also known as Rectilinear or Translatory motion.
  • Examples include a car on a straight road or a falling apple.

🎙️ When an object moves from one point to another in a straight line, we call it linear motion. Think of a soldier marching straight ahead or a stone dropped from a height falling directly downwards.

Types of Motion: Circular Motion

  • Motion along a circular path.
  • The object maintains a constant distance from the center.
  • Examples include a spinning top or the hands of a clock.

🎙️ If you tie a stone to a string and whirl it around, it performs circular motion. The Earth revolving around the Sun is another grand example of circular motion, though it's an ellipse, it's often approximated as circular.

Types of Motion: Oscillatory Motion

  • Motion that repeats back and forth about a fixed mean position.
  • Also known as Vibratory motion.
  • Examples include a swinging pendulum or the strings of a guitar.

🎙️ A child on a swing moves back and forth around its lowest point. This rhythmic, repetitive motion is called oscillatory motion. It's a very common type of motion seen in many systems.

Distance: How Far We Travel

  • The actual length of the path covered by an object.
  • It is a scalar quantity, meaning it only has magnitude.
  • Distance is always positive or zero; it cannot be negative.

🎙️ If you walk from your home to school, the total length of the path you take, even if you take detours, is the distance you covered. It doesn't matter which direction you went, only how much you moved.

📏
Path Length
9 / 25

Displacement: Straight Line Change

  • The shortest straight-line distance between the initial and final positions.
  • It is a vector quantity, having both magnitude and direction.
  • Displacement can be positive, negative, or even zero.

🎙️ Consider the home-to-school example again. Displacement would be the straight line drawn directly from your home to your school, along with the specific direction. If you return home after school, your total displacement is zero.

🧭
Start to End

Distance vs. Displacement

  • Distance depends on the path taken; Displacement is path-independent.
  • Distance is always greater than or equal to displacement magnitude.
  • Distance is scalar; Displacement is vector.

🎙️ If you walk 5 meters east, then 5 meters west, your distance covered is 10 meters. However, your displacement is zero because you ended up at your starting point. This highlights their key difference.

Uniform Motion: Constant Pace

  • An object covers equal distances in equal intervals of time.
  • The speed of the object remains constant throughout the motion.
  • Represented by a straight line on a distance-time graph.

🎙️ Imagine a car moving on a perfectly empty highway at exactly 60 km/h without changing its speed or direction. This is a classic example of uniform motion, where the pace is constant.

Non-Uniform Motion: Changing Pace

  • An object covers unequal distances in equal intervals of time.
  • The speed of the object changes during the motion.
  • Represented by a curved line on a distance-time graph.

🎙️ A car moving in city traffic is a perfect example of non-uniform motion. It frequently speeds up, slows down, or stops due to traffic lights and other vehicles, meaning its speed is constantly changing.

Speed: How Fast an Object Moves

  • The rate at which an object covers distance.
  • Formula: Speed = Distance / Time.
  • It is a scalar quantity, only having magnitude. SI Unit: meters per second (m/s).

🎙️ Speed tells us how quickly an object is moving, regardless of its direction. The speedometer in a car measures its instantaneous speed, showing you how fast you are going at that exact moment.

⏱️
Distance per Time
14 / 25

Velocity: Speed with Direction

  • The rate of change of displacement.
  • Formula: Velocity = Displacement / Time.
  • It is a vector quantity, having both magnitude and direction. SI Unit: meters per second (m/s).

🎙️ Velocity is crucial when direction matters. For example, a storm moving at 20 km/h North has a specific velocity. If it changes direction to East, its velocity changes even if its speed remains 20 km/h.

💨
Speed & Direction

Speed vs. Velocity

  • Speed is a scalar quantity; Velocity is a vector quantity.
  • Speed measures the rate of covering distance; Velocity measures the rate of change of displacement.
  • Speed is always positive; Velocity can be positive, negative, or zero.

🎙️ While both speed and velocity are measured in meters per second, remember that velocity provides more complete information by including the direction of motion. This distinction is very important in physics.

Acceleration: Change in Velocity

  • The rate of change of velocity.
  • Formula: Acceleration = (Final Velocity - Initial Velocity) / Time.
  • It is a vector quantity. SI Unit: meters per second squared (m/s²).

🎙️ When you press the accelerator pedal in a car, you are causing the car to accelerate, meaning its velocity is changing. This change can be in magnitude (speeding up or slowing down) or in direction.

Positive & Negative Acceleration

  • Positive acceleration: Velocity is increasing in the direction of motion.
  • Negative acceleration (Deceleration/Retardation): Velocity is decreasing.
  • A change in direction also causes acceleration, even if speed is constant.

🎙️ When a car speeds up from a traffic light, it experiences positive acceleration. When the driver applies brakes and the car slows down, it experiences negative acceleration, also known as deceleration or retardation.

Worked Example 1: Distance & Displacement

  • Problem: A person walks 4 km East, then 3 km North.
  • Question 1: What is the total distance covered?
  • Question 2: What is the magnitude of their displacement?

🎙️ For distance, we simply add the lengths of all the paths taken. For displacement, we find the shortest straight-line distance from the starting point to the final point, often using the Pythagorean theorem for perpendicular movements.

1Distance = 4 km + 3 km = 7 km
2Displacement = √(4² + 3²) = √25 = 5 km (North-East)

Worked Example 2: Speed & Velocity

  • Problem: A car travels 100 km in 2 hours.
  • Question 1: What is its average speed?
  • Question 2: If it travels from city A to city B (100 km East) in 2 hours, what is its average velocity?

🎙️ Notice how the calculation for magnitude is the same, but for velocity, we must include the direction. Speed only gives the magnitude, while velocity gives both magnitude and direction.

1Average Speed = Distance/Time = 100 km / 2 h = 50 km/h
2Average Velocity = Displacement/Time = 100 km East / 2 h = 50 km/h East
20 / 25

Worked Example 3: Acceleration

  • Problem: A bike's velocity changes from 10 m/s to 20 m/s in 5 seconds.
  • Question: Calculate its acceleration.

🎙️ This calculation shows us that the bike's velocity is increasing by 2 meters per second every second. This is a positive acceleration, indicating it is speeding up.

1Change in Velocity (Δv) = Final V - Initial V = 20 m/s - 10 m/s = 10 m/s
2Acceleration (a) = Δv / Time = 10 m/s / 5 s = 2 m/s²

Chapter Recap: Motion Fundamentals

  • Motion is relative, defined by a reference point.
  • Distance is the total path length; Displacement is the shortest path from start to end.
  • Speed is scalar (magnitude only); Velocity is vector (magnitude and direction).
  • Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity.

What is Force?

  • A push or a pull on an object.
  • It can change an object's state of motion.
  • Measured in Newtons (N).

🎙️ Force is simply a push or a pull. When you open a door, you either push it or pull it. This push or pull is what we call force. It's the agent that tries to change an object's movement.

Balanced Forces

  • Equal forces acting in opposite directions.
  • Net force is zero.
  • Does not change the state of motion.

🎙️ When two forces acting on an object are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction, they are called balanced forces. The object will either remain at rest or continue moving with constant velocity, as the net effect is zero.

⚖️
Equal & Opposite

Unbalanced Forces

  • Unequal forces or forces not opposite.
  • Net force is non-zero.
  • Causes a change in the state of motion.

🎙️ If the forces acting on an object are unbalanced, meaning they are not equal and opposite, then there will be a net force. This net force will cause the object to accelerate, either by starting to move, stopping, speeding up, slowing down, or changing direction.

Galileo's Idea: The Concept of Inertia

  • Objects tend to resist changes in their state of motion.
  • A ball on an ideal frictionless surface would roll forever.
  • Introduced the idea before Newton.

🎙️ Galileo observed that objects tend to maintain their state of motion unless an external force acts on them. He imagined a ball rolling on a perfectly smooth surface, suggesting it would never stop without friction or air resistance, laying the groundwork for inertia.

🛷
Frictionless Motion

Inertia: Resistance to Change

  • The natural tendency of an object to resist a change in its state of rest or motion.
  • More mass means more inertia.
  • Types: Inertia of Rest, Inertia of Motion, Inertia of Direction.

🎙️ Inertia is like an object's stubbornness to change what it's doing. If it's still, it wants to stay still. If it's moving, it wants to keep moving at the same speed and in the same direction. This resistance is directly related to its mass.

Resistance

Newton's First Law of Motion (Law of Inertia)

  • An object at rest stays at rest, and an object in motion stays in motion with the same speed and in the same direction unless acted upon by an unbalanced force.
  • Formalizes Galileo's idea.
  • Explains why things resist change.

🎙️ Newton's First Law is also known as the Law of Inertia. It tells us that objects are 'lazy' – they prefer to keep doing what they are already doing. A force is needed to overcome this inertia and change their state.

Mass and Inertia: A Direct Link

  • A heavier object has more inertia.
  • It's harder to start or stop a more massive object.

🎙️ The more mass an object has, the greater its inertia. Think about pushing a small toy car versus pushing a real car. The real car has much more mass, so it's much harder to get it moving or to stop it once it's in motion.

Momentum: Quantity of Motion

  • The product of an object's mass and its velocity.
  • Formula: Momentum (p) = mass (m) × velocity (v).
  • Unit: kilogram-meter per second (kg m/s).

🎙️ Momentum is a measure of how much 'motion' an object has. A heavy object moving slowly can have the same momentum as a light object moving very fast. It's a vector quantity, meaning it has both magnitude and direction.

Change in Momentum

  • Usually, velocity changes due to a force.
  • A large force over a short time, or a small force over a long time, can cause the same change.

🎙️ Since momentum depends on mass and velocity, any change in these will change momentum. In most cases, it's a change in velocity due to an applied force. This concept is crucial for understanding Newton's Second Law.

Newton's Second Law of Motion

  • The rate of change of momentum of an object is proportional to the applied unbalanced force in the direction of the force.
  • Simplified: Force = mass × acceleration (F = ma).
  • Unit of Force: Newton (N).

🎙️ Newton's Second Law is one of the most important laws in physics. It tells us that the harder you push an object, the faster it will accelerate, and the more massive an object is, the more force you need to accelerate it.

💪

Second Law in Real Life: Catching a Ball

  • A cricketer pulls his hands back while catching a fast ball.
  • Increases the time over which the ball's momentum changes.
  • Reduces the force exerted on the hands, preventing injury.

🎙️ When catching a fast-moving ball, pulling your hands back increases the time taken for the ball's momentum to become zero. According to F=Δp/Δt, increasing time (Δt) reduces the force (F) on your hands, making the catch less painful.

Worked Example: Calculating Force

  • Problem: A car of mass 1000 kg accelerates from rest to 20 m/s in 5 seconds.
  • Calculate the force required.
  • Solution: a = (v-u)/t = (20-0)/5 = 4 m/s². F = ma = 1000 kg × 4 m/s² = 4000 N.

🎙️ Let's apply F=ma to a practical problem. First, we find the acceleration using the given velocity and time. Then, we multiply the mass by this acceleration to find the force. This shows how forces cause acceleration.

Newton's Third Law of Motion

  • To every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction.
  • Forces always occur in pairs.
  • Action and reaction forces act on different objects.

🎙️ Newton's Third Law explains why forces always come in pairs. When you push on a wall (action), the wall pushes back on you with an equal and opposite force (reaction). These forces don't cancel out because they act on different objects.

🚀

Third Law in Real Life: Walking

  • When you walk, you push the ground backward (action).
  • The ground pushes you forward with an equal and opposite force (reaction).
  • This forward force propels you.

🎙️ Walking is a perfect example of the third law. Without the ground pushing back on your foot, you wouldn't be able to move forward. The reaction force from the ground is what makes us move.

Conservation of Momentum

  • In an isolated system, the total momentum before and after a collision or interaction remains constant.
  • No external unbalanced force acts on the system.
  • Total momentum before = Total momentum after.

🎙️ The Law of Conservation of Momentum states that if no external forces act on a system, the total momentum of that system never changes. This means momentum is transferred between objects, but the total amount stays the same.

⚖️
Momentum Balance

Conservation of Momentum in Real Life: Gun Recoil

  • When a bullet is fired from a gun, the gun recoils backward.
  • The forward momentum of the bullet equals the backward momentum of the gun.
  • Total momentum of the system (gun + bullet) remains zero.

🎙️ Before firing, both the gun and bullet are at rest, so total momentum is zero. After firing, the bullet gains forward momentum. To conserve momentum, the gun must gain an equal and opposite (backward) momentum, causing recoil.

Chapter Recap: Key Concepts

  • Force: Push or pull, causes change in motion/shape.
  • Inertia: Resistance to change in state of motion (related to mass).
  • Newton's Laws: First (Inertia), Second (F=ma), Third (Action-Reaction).
  • Momentum: Mass × Velocity (conserved in isolated systems).

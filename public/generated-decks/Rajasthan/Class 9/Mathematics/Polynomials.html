What are Algebraic Expressions?

  • A combination of variables and constants.
  • Connected by mathematical operations (+, -, *, /).
  • They represent quantities whose values can change.

🎙️ Think of algebraic expressions as recipes where you mix ingredients like numbers and letters using operations. For example, '2x + 5' is an expression where 'x' is a variable and '2' and '5' are constants.

Variables vs. Constants

  • Variables: Symbols (like x, y, a) that can take different values.
  • Constants: Fixed numerical values that do not change.

🎙️ Imagine a variable as a chameleon that can change its color, while a constant is like a rock, always staying the same. In '3x + 4', 'x' is the variable and '3' and '4' are constants.

Understanding Terms

  • Parts of an expression separated by '+' or '-' signs.
  • Each term is a product of factors.
  • Example: In 3x² + 2x - 5, the terms are 3x², 2x, and -5.

🎙️ Think of an algebraic expression as a LEGO structure. Each individual LEGO block, like '3x²' or '2x' or '-5', is a term. They combine to form the whole expression.

What are Coefficients?

  • The numerical factor of a term.
  • It tells us how many times the variable part is multiplied.
  • Example: In 5x², '5' is the coefficient of x².

🎙️ The coefficient is like the 'quantity tag' attached to a variable part. It specifies 'how much' of that variable we have. If you see 'y', its coefficient is implicitly '1'.

What Exactly is a Polynomial?

  • An algebraic expression where the powers of variables are whole numbers (0, 1, 2, 3...).
  • No negative or fractional powers.
  • Example: 2x³ + 4x - 7 is a polynomial.

🎙️ Not all algebraic expressions are polynomials. The key rule is that the exponents of the variables must always be non-negative integers. This restriction makes polynomials well-behaved and easier to work with.

🎙️ Understanding the origin of the word helps us remember its definition. While it means 'many terms', it can also have just one term, like a monomial, which is still a polynomial.

Degree of a Polynomial

  • The highest power of the variable in a polynomial.
  • For a constant polynomial (e.g., 5), the degree is 0.
  • Example: In 4x⁵ + 2x² - 1, the degree is 5.

🎙️ The degree is like the 'rank' of a polynomial. It's determined by looking at all the terms and finding the largest exponent of the variable. This is a very important characteristic of a polynomial.

Polynomials by Number of Terms

  • Monomial: One term (e.g., 5x²).
  • Binomial: Two terms (e.g., 3x + 2).
  • Trinomial: Three terms (e.g., x² + 2x - 1).

🎙️ We classify polynomials based on how many 'LEGO blocks' they have. Monomials are single blocks, binomials are two blocks joined, and trinomials are three. Anything with more than three terms is generally just called a polynomial.

The Zero Polynomial

  • The constant polynomial 0 is called the zero polynomial.
  • Its degree is undefined.
  • It is unique because it makes any variable term zero.

🎙️ The zero polynomial is a bit of an exception. While other constant polynomials like '5' have a degree of 0, the degree of '0' itself is considered undefined. This is because we can write 0 as 0x^1, 0x^2, etc., so no single highest power exists.

Value of a Polynomial at a Point

  • To find the value, substitute the given number for the variable.
  • Then, simplify the expression using order of operations.
  • Example: If P(x) = x² + 2, then P(1) = 1² + 2 = 3.

🎙️ Finding the value of a polynomial is like using a function machine. You put a number in for 'x', and the polynomial gives you an output. This is a fundamental skill for solving problems involving polynomials.

Zeroes of a Polynomial

  • A 'zero' of a polynomial P(x) is a value 'c' such that P(c) = 0.
  • These are also called roots of the polynomial.
  • They represent the x-intercepts when graphed.

🎙️ The zeroes of a polynomial are incredibly important. They tell us the specific values of 'x' that make the entire polynomial expression equal to zero. Geometrically, these are the points where the graph of the polynomial crosses the x-axis.

Introduction to Remainder Theorem

  • If a polynomial P(x) is divided by (x - a), the remainder is P(a).
  • It helps find the remainder without actual division.
  • Useful for checking divisibility.

🎙️ The Remainder Theorem is a powerful shortcut! Instead of performing long polynomial division, we can simply substitute the value 'a' into the polynomial P(x) to find out what the remainder would be. This saves a lot of time and effort.

A shortcut for division

Introduction to Factor Theorem

  • (x - a) is a factor of P(x) if and only if P(a) = 0.
  • This means if P(a)=0, then there is no remainder when P(x) is divided by (x-a).
  • It's a special case of the Remainder Theorem.

🎙️ The Factor Theorem builds directly on the Remainder Theorem. If the remainder P(a) is zero, it means (x - a) divides P(x) perfectly, making (x - a) a factor of the polynomial. This is crucial for factoring polynomials.

Polynomials: A Quick Recap

  • Polynomials are expressions with whole number exponents.
  • Classified by terms (monomial, binomial, trinomial) and degree (linear, quadratic, cubic).
  • Value of P(x) by substitution, zeroes are where P(x)=0.
  • Remainder and Factor Theorems simplify division and factoring.

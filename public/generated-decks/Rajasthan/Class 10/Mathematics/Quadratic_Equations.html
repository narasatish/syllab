What is a Quadratic Polynomial?

  • A polynomial with degree 2.
  • Its highest power is always 2.
  • Example: 2x² + 3x - 5.

🎙️ A quadratic polynomial is simply a polynomial where the highest power of the variable is exactly two. This 'two' is what makes it 'quadratic', derived from the Latin word 'quadratus' meaning square.

What is a Quadratic Equation?

  • When a quadratic polynomial is set to zero.
  • It always has an 'equal to' sign (=).
  • Example: ax² + bx + c = 0.

🎙️ When we take a quadratic polynomial and set it equal to zero, it transforms into a quadratic equation. The 'equals' sign is key, as it implies we are looking for specific values of the variable that make the statement true.

Standard Form of a Quadratic Equation

  • The general form is ax² + bx + c = 0.
  • Where 'a', 'b', 'c' are real numbers.
  • Crucially, 'a' cannot be zero (a ≠ 0).

🎙️ This is the most common and organized way to write any quadratic equation. It helps us easily identify the coefficients 'a', 'b', and 'c', which are crucial for solving these equations later on.

1Term 1: ax² (Quadratic)
2Term 2: bx (Linear)
3Term 3: c (Constant)
4= 0

Identifying Quadratic Equations

  • Check if the highest power of the variable is 2.
  • Ensure the coefficient of x² (i.e., 'a') is not zero.
  • Always simplify expressions before making a decision.

🎙️ It's important to be able to recognize a quadratic equation from various given expressions. Always simplify the expression first to see if it truly fits the standard form and has a degree of two.

Roots of a Quadratic Equation

  • Values of 'x' that satisfy the equation.
  • Also known as 'solutions' or 'zeros' of the equation.
  • A quadratic equation has at most two roots.

🎙️ The roots of a quadratic equation are the special values of 'x' that make the equation true, meaning when you substitute them, the equation equals zero. Think of them as the answers to the question posed by the equation.

Method 1: Finding Roots by Factorisation

  • Split the middle term (bx) into two terms.
  • Group terms and find common factors.
  • Set each factor to zero to find the roots.

🎙️ Factorisation is a powerful method to find the roots, especially when the quadratic expression can be easily factored. It involves breaking down the polynomial into simpler products, then using the zero product property.

1Split middle term
2Group terms
3Factor out common factors
4Set factors to zero
12 / 25

Worked Example 1: Factorisation

  • Solve: x² - 3x - 10 = 0
  • We can write this as (x - 5)(x + 2) = 0
  • Setting each factor to zero: x - 5 = 0 or x + 2 = 0
  • The roots are x = 5 and x = -2.

🎙️ Let's apply the factorisation method to this example. We look for two numbers that multiply to -10 and add up to -3. These numbers are -5 and 2. This allows us to factor the expression directly.

13 / 25

Worked Example 2: Factorisation

  • Solve: 2x² + x - 6 = 0
  • Split 'x' as '4x - 3x': 2x² + 4x - 3x - 6 = 0
  • Factor by grouping: 2x(x+2) - 3(x+2) = 0
  • Common factor (x+2): (2x-3)(x+2) = 0
  • Roots are x = 3/2 and x = -2.

🎙️ Here's another example involving splitting the middle term when 'a' is not 1. We need two numbers whose product is 2*(-6) = -12 and whose sum is 1. These numbers are 4 and -3.

Method 2: Completing the Square

  • Transform the equation into a perfect square trinomial.
  • Add and subtract a specific term to both sides.
  • Useful when factorisation is difficult or not possible.

🎙️ This method is a bit more involved but is very useful, especially when you cannot easily factor the quadratic equation. The goal is to create a perfect square trinomial on one side of the equation, making it easier to solve for 'x'.

Worked Example 3: Completing the Square

  • Solve: x² + 4x - 5 = 0
  • Move constant: x² + 4x = 5
  • Add (4/2)² = 4 to both sides: x² + 4x + 4 = 5 + 4
  • Form perfect square: (x + 2)² = 9
  • Take square root: x + 2 = ±3
  • Roots are x = 1 and x = -5.

🎙️ In this example, to complete the square for x² + 4x, we need to add (coefficient of x / 2)² which is (4/2)² = 4. Remember to add it to both sides of the equation to maintain balance.

Method 3: The Quadratic Formula

  • A general formula to find roots of any quadratic equation.
  • Always works, even when other methods fail.
  • Formula: x = [-b ± √(b² - 4ac)] / 2a

🎙️ The quadratic formula is a universal tool. It guarantees finding the roots of any quadratic equation, regardless of whether it can be factored or not. It's an essential formula to memorize and understand.

Derivation of Quadratic Formula (Briefly)

  • Starts from the standard form ax²+bx+c=0.
  • Uses the 'completing the square' method on the general form.
  • Shows how the formula is systematically obtained.

🎙️ Understanding how the quadratic formula is derived from the standard form using completing the square helps you appreciate its power and remember it better. It's not magic, but a logical deduction from basic algebraic principles.

1Divide by 'a'
2Move constant to RHS
3Complete the square
4Simplify & solve for 'x'
18 / 25

Worked Example 4: Quadratic Formula

  • Solve: 2x² + 3x - 5 = 0
  • Identify a=2, b=3, c=-5.
  • Substitute into formula: x = [-3 ± √(3² - 4(2)(-5))] / (2*2)
  • Simplify: x = [-3 ± √(9 + 40)] / 4 = [-3 ± √49] / 4
  • x = [-3 ± 7] / 4. Roots are x = 1 and x = -5/2.

🎙️ Here, we substitute the values of a, b, and c directly into the formula. Be careful with the signs and calculations, especially the term under the square root, which is called the discriminant.

Nature of Roots: The Discriminant

  • D = b² - 4ac
  • It's the part under the square root in the quadratic formula.
  • Determines the type of roots without fully solving the equation.

🎙️ The discriminant, D, is a crucial part of the quadratic formula. It's like a detective that tells us about the nature of the roots – whether they are real and distinct, real and equal, or not real – even before we fully calculate them.

Case 1: Discriminant D > 0

  • The equation has two distinct real roots.
  • Graphically, the parabola intersects the x-axis at two different points.
  • Example: x² - 3x - 10 = 0 (D = 49 > 0)

🎙️ When the discriminant is positive, it means there are two different real numbers that satisfy the equation. This indicates that the quadratic graph, a parabola, crosses the x-axis at two unique points.

Case 2: Discriminant D = 0

  • The equation has two equal real roots (or one repeated root).
  • Graphically, the parabola touches the x-axis at exactly one point.
  • Example: x² - 4x + 4 = 0 (D = 0)

🎙️ If the discriminant is zero, the quadratic equation has two roots that are identical. This means the parabola just touches the x-axis at a single point, which is its vertex. The roots are the same.

Case 3: Discriminant D < 0

  • The equation has no real roots.
  • The roots are complex (imaginary) numbers.
  • Graphically, the parabola does not intersect the x-axis at all.

🎙️ When the discriminant is negative, there are no real numbers that satisfy the equation. This means the parabola never crosses or touches the x-axis. The roots exist, but they belong to the set of complex numbers.

Recap & Summary

  • Definition: An equation of the form ax²+bx+c=0, where a≠0.
  • Methods to solve: Factorisation, Completing the Square, Quadratic Formula.
  • Discriminant (D=b²-4ac) tells us the nature of the roots.
  • Quadratic equations have at most two roots.

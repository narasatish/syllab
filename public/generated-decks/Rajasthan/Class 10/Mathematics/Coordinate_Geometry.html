What is Coordinate Geometry?

  • It's a branch of mathematics.
  • Uses coordinates to study geometric figures.
  • Helps locate points and describe shapes using numbers.

🎙️ Imagine trying to tell someone exactly where something is on a map. Coordinate Geometry gives us a powerful tool to do just that! It uses a system of numbers, called coordinates, to pinpoint locations and understand geometric figures.

The Cartesian Plane

  • Two perpendicular lines: X-axis (horizontal) and Y-axis (vertical).
  • They intersect at the Origin (0,0).
  • Forms a grid for plotting points.

🎙️ The foundation of coordinate geometry is the Cartesian Plane, named after the mathematician René Descartes. Think of it as a special graph paper. We have a horizontal line, the X-axis, and a vertical line, the Y-axis, meeting at the very center, which we call the Origin.

Plotting Points (x,y)

  • Every point is represented by an ordered pair (x, y).
  • x is the horizontal distance from the Y-axis (abscissa).
  • y is the vertical distance from the X-axis (ordinate).

🎙️ To locate any point on this plane, we use two numbers: 'x' and 'y'. The 'x' tells us how far left or right to go from the origin, and the 'y' tells us how far up or down. Always remember, the x-coordinate comes first, then the y-coordinate, written as (x, y).

Quadrants of the Plane

  • The axes divide the plane into four regions.
  • These regions are called Quadrants.
  • Numbered counter-clockwise from the top-right.

🎙️ When the X and Y axes cross, they create four distinct areas, much like four different rooms in a house. We call these 'quadrants'. We number them starting from the top-right and moving in an anti-clockwise direction, from Quadrant I to Quadrant IV.

The Distance Formula

  • Measures the length of a line segment.
  • Connects two points (x1, y1) and (x2, y2).
  • Based on the Pythagorean theorem.

🎙️ Now that we know how to place points, what if we want to know the exact distance between two of them? The Distance Formula is our tool for this. It allows us to calculate the length of the straight line connecting any two points on the Cartesian plane.

Understanding the Distance Formula

  • Imagine a right-angled triangle formed by the two points.
  • The difference in x-coordinates forms one leg.
  • The difference in y-coordinates forms the other leg.
  • The distance is the hypotenuse (using Pythagoras).

🎙️ The distance formula isn't magic; it comes directly from the famous Pythagorean theorem! If you draw two points and then draw a horizontal and vertical line to connect them, you'll see a right-angled triangle. The distance between the points is simply the hypotenuse of this triangle.

Worked Example: Distance Calculation

  • Find the distance between P(2, 3) and Q(5, 7).
  • x1=2, y1=3, x2=5, y2=7
  • Formula: √[(x2-x1)² + (y2-y1)²]
  • Distance = √[(5-2)² + (7-3)²] = √[3² + 4²] = √[9+16] = √25 = 5 units.

🎙️ Let's put the distance formula into practice. Here, we have two points, P and Q. We substitute their x and y coordinates into the formula. Remember to square the differences, add them, and then take the square root. The final answer is 5 units.

The Section Formula

  • Finds the coordinates of a point.
  • Divides a line segment in a given ratio.
  • Can be internal or external division.

🎙️ Imagine you have a line segment, and you want to find a point on that segment that divides it into a specific proportion, say 2:3. The Section Formula helps us find the exact coordinates of that dividing point. It's like cutting a rope at a particular ratio.

✂️
Dividing a Line
12 / 25

Internal Division Formula

  • Point P(x,y) divides A(x1,y1) and B(x2,y2).
  • Ratio is m1:m2.
  • x = (m1x2 + m2x1) / (m1 + m2)
  • y = (m1y2 + m2y1) / (m1 + m2)

🎙️ For internal division, the point lies *between* the two given points. The formula looks a bit complex, but it's logical. For the x-coordinate, we multiply m1 by x2 and m2 by x1, then sum them up and divide by the sum of the ratios. The same logic applies to the y-coordinate.

The Midpoint Formula

  • A special case of the Section Formula.
  • When the ratio is 1:1 (m1=1, m2=1).
  • Finds the exact middle point of a segment.
  • x = (x1 + x2) / 2
  • y = (y1 + y2) / 2

🎙️ The midpoint is simply the point that divides a line segment into two equal halves. This means the ratio of division is 1:1. If you substitute m1=1 and m2=1 into the section formula, you get the simpler midpoint formula: just average the x-coordinates and average the y-coordinates.

Worked Example: Section Formula

  • Find the point that divides A(1, 7) and B(4, -3) in ratio 2:3.
  • x1=1, y1=7, x2=4, y2=-3, m1=2, m2=3
  • x = (2*4 + 3*1) / (2+3) = (8+3)/5 = 11/5
  • y = (2*-3 + 3*7) / (2+3) = (-6+21)/5 = 15/5 = 3
  • Point is (11/5, 3)

🎙️ Let's calculate a point using the section formula. We have the two endpoints and the ratio of division. Carefully substitute the values into the x and y formulas. Remember to perform the multiplications first, then the additions, and finally the division. The resulting point is (11/5, 3).

Area of a Triangle using Coordinates

  • Calculate the area of a triangle.
  • Uses the coordinates of its three vertices.
  • No need for base and height measurements.

🎙️ Normally, to find the area of a triangle, you need its base and height. But in coordinate geometry, if you know the coordinates of its three corners, or vertices, you can directly calculate its area using a special formula. This is very powerful!

Area of a Triangle Formula

  • Given vertices A(x1, y1), B(x2, y2), C(x3, y3).
  • Area = ½ |x1(y2 - y3) + x2(y3 - y1) + x3(y1 - y2)|
  • The absolute value ensures area is positive.
  • The points must be taken in order (clockwise or anti-clockwise).

🎙️ This formula might look long, but it's quite systematic. You take half of the absolute value of the sum of three terms. Each term involves one x-coordinate multiplied by the difference of the other two y-coordinates. Remember to keep the order of the y-coordinates consistent within each bracket.

Worked Example: Area of a Triangle

  • Find area of triangle with vertices (1, -1), (-4, 6), (-3, -5).
  • x1=1, y1=-1; x2=-4, y2=6; x3=-3, y3=-5
  • Area = ½ |1(6 - (-5)) + (-4)(-5 - (-1)) + (-3)(-1 - 6)|
  • Area = ½ |1(11) + (-4)(-4) + (-3)(-7)|
  • Area = ½ |11 + 16 + 21| = ½ |48| = 24 square units.

🎙️ Let's apply the area formula. Be very careful with the signs, especially when subtracting negative numbers. Substitute each coordinate into its correct place in the formula, calculate the terms inside the brackets, then multiply, sum, and finally take half. The area is 24 square units.

Collinearity of Points

  • Three or more points are collinear if they lie on the same straight line.
  • If points are collinear, the area of the triangle formed by them is zero.
  • This is a key test for collinearity.

🎙️ What if three points are so perfectly aligned that they form a straight line? We call such points 'collinear'. If three points are collinear, they cannot form a triangle, right? So, a powerful test for collinearity is to calculate the area of the triangle formed by them. If the area is zero, the points are collinear!

Recap: Key Concepts of Coordinate Geometry

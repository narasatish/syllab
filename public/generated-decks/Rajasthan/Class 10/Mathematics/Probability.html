What is Probability?

  • It's a measure of how likely an event is to occur.
  • Expressed as a number between 0 and 1.
  • A higher number means a higher chance.

🎙️ Probability tells us about the chances of something happening. Think about predicting rain or winning a lottery. We assign a numerical value, from 0 to 1, to these chances. A value of 0 means it's impossible, and 1 means it's certain.

Key Terms: Experiment and Outcome

  • An 'Experiment' is an action with uncertain results.
  • Example: Tossing a coin, rolling a die.
  • An 'Outcome' is a possible result of an experiment.
  • Example: Getting 'Heads' when tossing a coin.

🎙️ Let's learn some important words. An experiment is any activity where the result isn't fixed beforehand, like flipping a coin. Each possible result from that experiment is called an outcome. For a coin toss, 'Heads' is one outcome, and 'Tails' is another.

Key Term: Sample Space

  • The set of ALL possible outcomes of an experiment.
  • Represented by 'S' or 'Ω'.
  • Example: Tossing a coin, S = {Heads, Tails}.

🎙️ The sample space is like a complete list of everything that can possibly happen in an experiment. If you roll a standard six-sided die, the sample space would be the numbers 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and 6. It includes every single outcome.

Key Term: Event

  • An 'Event' is one or more outcomes of an experiment.
  • It's a subset of the sample space.
  • Example: Getting an 'even number' when rolling a die.
  • Outcomes for this event: {2, 4, 6}.

🎙️ An event is a specific outcome or a collection of outcomes that we are interested in. For instance, if you roll a die, getting a '5' is an event. Getting an 'odd number' is also an event, which includes the outcomes 1, 3, and 5.

The Probability Formula

  • P(E) = (Number of Favourable Outcomes) / (Total Number of Outcomes)
  • P(E) is the probability of event E.
  • Favourable outcomes are the ones we want.

🎙️ This is the core formula for calculating probability. To find the probability of an event, you count how many ways your desired event can happen, and then divide that by the total number of all possible outcomes. Remember, it's always a fraction or a decimal between 0 and 1.

Example 1: Tossing a Coin

  • Experiment: Tossing a fair coin.
  • Sample Space (S) = {Heads, Tails}. Total outcomes = 2.
  • Event (E): Getting a Head. Favourable outcome = {Heads}. Number of favourable outcomes = 1.
  • P(Head) = 1/2.

🎙️ Let's apply the formula. When you toss a coin, there are two equally likely outcomes: Heads or Tails. If we want to find the probability of getting a Head, there's only one way for that to happen. So, it's 1 (favourable) divided by 2 (total), which is 1/2 or 0.5.

Example 2: Rolling a Die

  • Experiment: Rolling a fair six-sided die.
  • Sample Space (S) = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}. Total outcomes = 6.
  • Event (E): Getting a '4'. Favourable outcome = {4}. Number of favourable outcomes = 1.
  • P(Getting a '4') = 1/6.

🎙️ Consider rolling a standard die. The sample space has six outcomes. If we are interested in getting the number '4', there's only one '4' on the die. Therefore, the probability is 1 (favourable outcome) divided by 6 (total outcomes), giving us 1/6.

Range of Probability

  • The probability of any event E, P(E), is always between 0 and 1 (inclusive).
  • 0 ≤ P(E) ≤ 1.
  • P(E) = 0 means the event is impossible.
  • P(E) = 1 means the event is certain.

🎙️ It's very important to remember that probability values always fall between 0 and 1. You can never have a probability less than 0 or greater than 1. If your calculation gives a value outside this range, you know there's a mistake.

Impossible Event

  • An event that cannot happen.
  • The number of favourable outcomes is 0.
  • P(Impossible Event) = 0.
  • Example: Getting a '7' when rolling a single six-sided die.

🎙️ An impossible event is exactly what it sounds like – something that absolutely cannot occur. For instance, if you roll a standard die, it's impossible to get the number 7, because 7 is not on any face of the die. In this case, the probability is 0.

Sure or Certain Event

  • An event that is sure to happen.
  • The number of favourable outcomes equals the total number of outcomes.
  • P(Sure Event) = 1.
  • Example: Getting a number less than '7' when rolling a single six-sided die.

🎙️ A sure or certain event is one that is guaranteed to happen. If you roll a six-sided die, you are certain to get a number less than 7, because all possible outcomes (1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6) are less than 7. In such cases, the probability is 1.

Complementary Events

  • If E is an event, then 'not E' (or E') is its complementary event.
  • 'not E' means the event E does not happen.
  • P(E) + P(not E) = 1.
  • This means P(not E) = 1 - P(E).

🎙️ Complementary events are two events that are opposites of each other. If one happens, the other cannot, and together they cover all possibilities. The sum of their probabilities is always 1. This formula is very useful for finding the probability of an event not happening.

Example: Complementary Events (Coin)

  • Experiment: Tossing a coin.
  • Event E: Getting a Head. P(E) = 1/2.
  • Complementary Event (not E): Not getting a Head (i.e., getting a Tail).
  • P(not E) = 1 - P(E) = 1 - 1/2 = 1/2.
  • P(Tail) = 1/2.

🎙️ Let's revisit the coin toss. The probability of getting a Head is 1/2. The complementary event is 'not getting a Head', which means getting a Tail. Using the formula, the probability of not getting a Head is 1 minus 1/2, which is also 1/2. This makes perfect sense, as the probability of getting a Tail is indeed 1/2.

Probability with Playing Cards

  • 4 suits: Hearts ❤️, Diamonds ♦️, Clubs ♣️, Spades ♠️.
  • Each suit has 13 cards: A, 2, 3, ..., 10, J, Q, K.
  • Hearts and Diamonds are red; Clubs and Spades are black.

🎙️ Playing card problems are common in probability. It's crucial to know the structure of a standard deck. Remember there are 52 cards, divided into four suits, with two red and two black suits. Each suit has specific cards, including face cards like Jack, Queen, and King.

Example: Probability with Playing Cards

  • Experiment: Drawing one card from a well-shuffled deck.
  • Total outcomes = 52.
  • Event (E): Drawing a King.
  • There are 4 Kings (one in each suit). Favourable outcomes = 4.
  • P(Drawing a King) = 4/52 = 1/13.

🎙️ Let's find the probability of drawing a King. A standard deck has 52 cards. There are four Kings in total: King of Hearts, King of Diamonds, King of Clubs, and King of Spades. So, the probability is 4 (favourable) divided by 52 (total), which simplifies to 1/13.

Probability with Two Dice

  • Experiment: Rolling two fair dice simultaneously.
  • Each die has 6 outcomes.
  • Total outcomes = 6 × 6 = 36.
  • Sample Space: (1,1), (1,2), ..., (6,6).

🎙️ When rolling two dice, the number of possible outcomes increases significantly. Since each die has 6 faces, and the outcome of one doesn't affect the other, we multiply the possibilities: 6 times 6 equals 36 total outcomes. It's helpful to list these pairs, like (1,1), (1,2), and so on, up to (6,6).

Example: Sum of Two Dice

  • Experiment: Rolling two dice. Total outcomes = 36.
  • Event (E): Getting a sum of '7'.
  • Favourable outcomes: (1,6), (2,5), (3,4), (4,3), (5,2), (6,1).
  • Number of favourable outcomes = 6.
  • P(Sum of 7) = 6/36 = 1/6.

🎙️ Let's find the probability of getting a sum of 7 when rolling two dice. We need to identify all pairs that add up to 7. These are (1,6), (2,5), (3,4), (4,3), (5,2), and (6,1). There are 6 such pairs. So, the probability is 6 divided by the total 36 outcomes, which simplifies to 1/6.

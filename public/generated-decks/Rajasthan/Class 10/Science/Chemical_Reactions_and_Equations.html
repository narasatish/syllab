How to Write a Chemical Equation

  • Step 1: Write the word equation
  • Step 2: Replace words with chemical symbols/formulas
  • Step 3: Indicate physical states (s, l, g, aq)

🎙️ To write a chemical equation, first, we think of the reaction in words. Then, we replace those words with the correct chemical symbols and formulas. Finally, we can add symbols to show if they are solid, liquid, gas, or aqueous.

Worked Example: Writing an Equation

  • Word: Magnesium + Oxygen → Magnesium Oxide
  • Symbolic: Mg + O₂ → MgO
  • With states: Mg(s) + O₂(g) → MgO(s)

🎙️ Let's try an example. When magnesium burns in air, it reacts with oxygen to form magnesium oxide. The word equation becomes Mg + O₂ → MgO. We can also add their physical states: Mg(s) + O₂(g) → MgO(s).

Why Balance Chemical Equations?

  • To obey the Law of Conservation of Mass
  • Mass cannot be created or destroyed
  • Number of atoms of each element must be equal on both sides

🎙️ Balancing equations is crucial because of the Law of Conservation of Mass. This law states that matter cannot be created or destroyed. So, the number of atoms of each element must be the same on both sides of the equation.

Steps for Balancing a Chemical Equation

  • Write the unbalanced skeletal equation
  • Count atoms of each element on both sides
  • Balance one element at a time using coefficients
  • Verify the final balanced equation

🎙️ Balancing an equation might seem tricky, but it's a systematic process. We start by writing the unbalanced equation, then count the atoms, and adjust coefficients one element at a time until everything is balanced.

Worked Example: Balancing H₂ + O₂ → H₂O (Step 1-2)

  • Unbalanced equation: H₂ + O₂ → H₂O
  • Reactants: H=2, O=2
  • Products: H=2, O=1

🎙️ Let's balance the formation of water: H₂ + O₂ → H₂O. First, we write the equation. Then, we count atoms: Reactants have 2 H, 2 O. Products have 2 H, but only 1 O. The oxygen is not balanced.

Worked Example: Balancing H₂ + O₂ → H₂O (Step 3-4)

  • Balance Oxygen: H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O
  • Now: Reactants H=2, O=2
  • Products: H=4, O=2 (Hydrogen is now unbalanced!)

🎙️ To balance oxygen, we put a '2' in front of H₂O on the product side: H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O. Now, we have 2 oxygen atoms on both sides. But look, hydrogen is now 4 on the product side and only 2 on the reactant side.

Worked Example: Balancing H₂ + O₂ → H₂O (Final)

  • Balance Hydrogen: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O
  • Final check: Reactants H=4, O=2
  • Products: H=4, O=2 (Equation is balanced!)

🎙️ To balance hydrogen, we put a '2' in front of H₂ on the reactant side: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O. Now, let's count again: 4 H and 2 O on both sides. Perfect! The equation is now balanced.

Type 1: Combination Reaction (A + B → AB)

  • Two or more reactants combine
  • Form a single new product
  • Simpler substances form a complex one

🎙️ In a combination reaction, two or more simple substances come together to form one new, more complex substance. Think of it like two friends joining to form a team.

Type 2: Decomposition Reaction (AB → A + B)

🎙️ A decomposition reaction is the opposite of combination. Here, one complex substance breaks down into two or more simpler substances. This usually requires energy, like heating it up or passing electricity through it.

Oxidation and Reduction: Redox Reactions

  • Oxidation: Gain of oxygen OR loss of hydrogen/electrons
  • Reduction: Loss of oxygen OR gain of hydrogen/electrons
  • These processes occur simultaneously in 'redox' reactions

🎙️ Some reactions involve a transfer of oxygen or hydrogen, or even electrons. We call these 'redox' reactions. Oxidation is gaining oxygen or losing electrons, while reduction is losing oxygen or gaining electrons. They always happen together!

Redox reactions involve electron transfer between reactants. Oxidation = loss of electrons

Effects of Oxidation in Everyday Life

  • Corrosion: Metals reacting with air/moisture (e.g., rusting)
  • Rancidity: Fats and oils spoiling due to oxidation (food spoilage)
  • Both are undesirable effects we try to prevent

🎙️ Oxidation isn't always good. Corrosion, like rusting of iron, weakens metals. Rancidity, where food containing fats and oils spoils and smells bad, is also due to oxidation. We try to prevent these!

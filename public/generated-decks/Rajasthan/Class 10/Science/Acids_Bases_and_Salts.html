What are Acids?

  • Acids are substances that taste sour.
  • They turn blue litmus paper red.
  • Acids are corrosive in nature, meaning they can eat away at materials.
  • They release hydrogen ions (H+) in water.

🎙️ Think of a lemon or tamarind; that sour taste is characteristic of acids. Many acids we encounter daily are weak, like those in fruits, but strong acids can be very dangerous and must be handled with care. The key chemical property is their ability to produce H+ ions.

🍋

3 / 25

Properties of Acids

  • Sour taste (never taste in lab!)
  • Corrosive action on skin and materials
  • React with metals to produce hydrogen gas
  • Neutralize bases to form salt and water

🎙️ It's crucial to remember that while some acids are safe to consume, you should never taste anything in a lab setting. The corrosive nature of strong acids is why they are stored in special containers and handled with extreme caution. Their reaction with metals is also a significant property.

Examples of Acids

  • Hydrochloric Acid (HCl): Found in our stomach to aid digestion.
  • Sulphuric Acid (H₂SO₄): Used in car batteries, a strong industrial acid.
  • Acetic Acid (CH₃COOH): Present in vinegar, used in cooking and preservation.
  • Citric Acid: Found in citrus fruits like oranges and lemons.

🎙️ We encounter acids daily! HCl helps digest our food, showing its vital biological role. Sulphuric acid is a powerhouse in industries, while acetic acid is a common household item. Citric acid gives many fruits their refreshing tang.

What are Bases?

  • Bases are substances that taste bitter.
  • They feel soapy or slippery to touch.
  • Bases turn red litmus paper blue.
  • They release hydroxide ions (OH-) in water.

🎙️ Think of soap or baking soda; they have that characteristic bitter taste and slippery feel. Like acids, strong bases can also be corrosive. The defining chemical property of a base is its ability to produce hydroxide ions when dissolved in water.

🧼
Soapy Feel

Properties of Bases

  • Bitter taste (never taste in lab!)
  • Soapy/slippery feel
  • Corrosive to some extent (strong bases)
  • Neutralize acids to form salt and water

🎙️ Just like with acids, tasting bases in the lab is a strict no-no. That slippery feel is a good indicator of a basic solution. Strong bases, like sodium hydroxide, are used in drain cleaners because of their corrosive nature, so always handle them carefully.

Examples of Bases

  • Sodium Hydroxide (NaOH): Also known as caustic soda, used in soap making.
  • Potassium Hydroxide (KOH): Used in alkaline batteries.
  • Calcium Hydroxide (Ca(OH)₂): Called slaked lime, used in whitewash.
  • Magnesium Hydroxide (Mg(OH)₂): Known as Milk of Magnesia, an antacid.

🎙️ From cleaning products to medicines, bases are everywhere. Sodium hydroxide is fundamental to soap production, while magnesium hydroxide helps settle upset stomachs. These examples show the diverse applications of bases in our daily lives.

Indicators: The Chemical Detectives!

  • Indicators are substances that change color.
  • They help us identify if a solution is acidic or basic.
  • They do not tell us the strength of the acid or base.
  • We use different types: natural and synthetic.

🎙️ Imagine you have an unknown liquid and need to know if it's an acid or a base. Indicators come to your rescue! They are like chemical detectives, giving us a visual clue through their color change. They are essential tools in chemistry labs and even in our homes.

Natural Indicators

  • Litmus: The most common indicator, extracted from lichens.
  • Turmeric: Changes from yellow to reddish-brown in bases.
  • Red Cabbage Extract: Shows different colors across the pH range.
  • China Rose Petals: Another natural indicator with distinct color changes.

🎙️ Nature provides us with many useful indicators. Litmus paper is a staple in any science lab. You might have noticed turmeric stains changing color when exposed to soap, which is a base. Red cabbage juice is a fantastic homemade indicator, showing a rainbow of colors!

The pH Scale: Measuring Strength

  • The pH scale measures the strength of acids and bases.
  • It ranges from 0 to 14.
  • A pH of 7 is neutral (e.g., pure water).
  • Lower pH (0-6) means more acidic; higher pH (8-14) means more basic.

🎙️ While indicators tell us if something is an acid or base, the pH scale gives us a numerical value for its strength. It's a logarithmic scale, meaning each whole number change in pH represents a tenfold change in acidity or basicity. Understanding pH is crucial for many applications.

pH scale (0-14) measures acidity and alkalinity. Acids < 7 (H+ ions), bases > 7 (OH− ions)

Importance of pH in Everyday Life (Part 1)

  • Our body works best within a narrow pH range (7.0-7.8).
  • Stomach pH: Highly acidic (1.5-3.5) for digestion.
  • Soil pH: Plants need specific pH for healthy growth.
  • Acid rain: Harms buildings, forests, and aquatic life.

🎙️ pH isn't just a lab concept; it's vital for life! Our bodies are very sensitive to pH changes. The acidic environment in our stomach is critical for breaking down food. Farmers monitor soil pH to ensure optimal crop yield, and environmentalists track acid rain to protect ecosystems.

Importance of pH in Everyday Life (Part 2)

  • Tooth Decay: Starts when mouth pH drops below 5.5.
  • Bee Sting: Acidic, causes pain. Can be neutralized by baking soda (a base).
  • Wasp Sting: Basic, causes pain. Can be neutralized by vinegar (an acid).
  • Self-defense by animals and plants through chemical warfare.

🎙️ Even common problems like tooth decay are pH-related; brushing helps restore a neutral pH. Knowing the pH of stings can help us find simple remedies. This shows how understanding acids and bases can directly benefit our health and wellbeing.

What are Salts?

  • Salts are ionic compounds formed when an acid reacts with a base.
  • This reaction is called neutralization.
  • They are generally solids and have high melting points.
  • Many salts are soluble in water.

🎙️ When an acid and a base meet, they neutralize each other, forming salt and water. This is a fundamental reaction in chemistry. Common table salt, sodium chloride, is just one example of a vast family of salts, each with unique properties and uses.

🧂
Neutral Compound
17 / 25

Family of Salts

  • Salts can be acidic, basic, or neutral depending on parent acid and base.
  • Strong Acid + Strong Base → Neutral Salt (e.g., NaCl)
  • Strong Acid + Weak Base → Acidic Salt (e.g., NH₄Cl)
  • Weak Acid + Strong Base → Basic Salt (e.g., CH₃COONa)

🎙️ Just like families, salts have different characteristics based on their 'parents' – the acid and base they came from. This determines whether the resulting salt solution will be acidic, basic, or neutral. Understanding this helps predict their behavior in solutions.

Common Salt (Sodium Chloride - NaCl)

  • Obtained from seawater evaporation and rock salt deposits.
  • An essential ingredient in our food.

🎙️ Common salt, or sodium chloride, is far more than just a seasoning. It's vital for our body's functions and has been used for centuries as a food preservative. Industrially, it's a starting point for synthesizing many other important chemicals, making it incredibly versatile.

Chemicals from Common Salt: Part 1

  • Sodium Hydroxide (NaOH): Caustic soda, used in soap and paper industries.
  • Bleaching Powder (CaOCl₂): Used as a bleaching agent and disinfectant.
  • Both are produced by the electrolysis of brine (salt water).
  • This process is called the chlor-alkali process.

🎙️ Common salt is a chemical treasure chest! Through a process called chlor-alkali, it gives us sodium hydroxide, a strong base, and bleaching powder, a powerful disinfectant. These compounds are cornerstones of many industrial processes, highlighting salt's economic importance.

Chemicals from Common Salt: Part 2

  • Baking Soda (NaHCO₃): Sodium hydrogen carbonate, used in baking and as an antacid.
  • Washing Soda (Na₂CO₃·10H₂O): Sodium carbonate, used for cleaning and softening hard water.
  • Both are derived from common salt through different chemical reactions.
  • They are vital in households and industries.

🎙️ Beyond industrial uses, common salt also gives us household staples like baking soda and washing soda. Baking soda is known for its leavening properties in cakes and its ability to neutralize stomach acid. Washing soda is excellent for laundry and water treatment, showcasing salt's versatility.

Recap: Acids, Bases and Salts

  • Acids are sour, turn blue litmus red, release H+.

What is Heredity?

  • Passing of traits from parents to offspring.
  • Ensures continuity of species.
  • Responsible for similarities within a family.

🎙️ Heredity is simply the process by which characteristics, or traits, are passed from one generation to the next. It's why children often resemble their parents and ensures that a cat gives birth to kittens, not puppies.

Understanding Variation

  • Differences among individuals of the same species.
  • No two individuals are exactly alike (except identical twins).
  • Essential for evolution and survival.

🎙️ While heredity explains similarities, variation explains the differences. Even within the same family, siblings are not identical. These differences, big or small, are called variations and are crucial for how species adapt over time.

Sources of Variation

  • Sexual Reproduction: Mixing of genes from two parents.
  • Mutations: Sudden changes in genetic material.
  • Environmental factors can also influence expression.

🎙️ The biggest source of variation in higher organisms is sexual reproduction, where genetic material from two parents combines. Mutations, which are random changes in DNA, also contribute significantly to variations over time.

Accumulation of Variation

🎙️ Imagine a small difference appearing in one generation. If this difference is beneficial, it might be passed on. Over many generations, these small differences accumulate, creating significant diversity within a population, which is the raw material for evolution.

🎙️ Inside every cell's nucleus, we find chromosomes, which are like long threads. Along these threads are specific sections called genes. Think of genes as tiny instruction manuals that tell your body how to build and function, determining your traits.

Inherited vs. Acquired Traits

  • Inherited traits: Passed from parents to offspring.
  • Acquired traits: Developed during an individual's lifetime.
  • Only inherited traits are passed on genetically.

🎙️ It's important to distinguish between two types of traits. Inherited traits are those you get from your parents, like eye color. Acquired traits are things you learn or develop during your life, like riding a bicycle, and these are not passed down to your children.

Gregor Mendel: Father of Genetics

  • Austrian monk who worked with pea plants.
  • Discovered fundamental laws of heredity.
  • His work was recognized decades later.

🎙️ Our understanding of heredity largely began with Gregor Mendel, an Austrian monk. Through his careful experiments with pea plants, he uncovered the basic rules by which traits are passed from one generation to the next, earning him the title 'Father of Genetics'.

Monohybrid Cross: Dominant & Recessive

  • Study of inheritance of one pair of contrasting traits.
  • Dominant trait: Expressed even with one copy.
  • Recessive trait: Expressed only with two copies.

🎙️ Mendel used terms like dominant and recessive. A dominant trait, like tallness in pea plants, will always show up if at least one gene for it is present. A recessive trait, like dwarfness, only appears if two copies of its gene are present.

Monohybrid and dihybrid crosses demonstrate Mendel's laws through Punnett squares showing

Mendel's Pea Plant Experiment

  • Crossed pure tall (TT) and pure dwarf (tt) pea plants.
  • F1 generation: All plants were tall (Tt).
  • F2 generation: Tall and Dwarf plants in 3:1 ratio.

🎙️ Mendel's classic experiment involved crossing pure tall pea plants with pure dwarf ones. All the offspring in the first generation (F1) were tall. But when he self-pollinated these F1 plants, the second generation (F2) showed both tall and dwarf plants in a predictable 3:1 ratio.

Punnett Square: Predicting Outcomes

  • A diagram to predict genetic crosses.
  • Shows all possible combinations of alleles.

🎙️ To make predicting genetic crosses easier, we use a tool called a Punnett Square. It's a simple grid that helps us visualize all the possible combinations of genes that offspring can inherit from their parents, and then calculate the probability of each outcome.

Worked Example: Monohybrid Cross (F1)

  • Parental Cross: Pure Tall (TT) x Pure Dwarf (tt)
  • Gametes: T, T from Tall; t, t from Dwarf
  • F1 Genotype: All Tt (Heterozygous Tall)

🎙️ Let's work through an example. If we cross a pure tall pea plant (TT) with a pure dwarf pea plant (tt), the Punnett square shows that all offspring in the F1 generation will have the genotype Tt. Since 'T' (tall) is dominant, all these plants will be phenotypically tall.

Worked Example: Monohybrid Cross (F2)

  • F1 x F1 Cross: Tt x Tt
  • Gametes: T, t from each F1 parent
  • F2 Genotypes: 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt
  • F2 Phenotypes: 3 Tall : 1 Dwarf

🎙️ Now, if we cross two of these F1 generation plants (Tt x Tt), the Punnett Square reveals a different outcome. In the F2 generation, we get a genotypic ratio of 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt. Phenotypically, this translates to 3 tall plants for every 1 dwarf plant.

Dihybrid Cross: Two Traits

  • Study of inheritance of two pairs of contrasting traits.
  • Example: Seed color (Yellow/Green) AND Seed shape (Round/Wrinkled).

🎙️ While a monohybrid cross looks at one trait, a dihybrid cross examines the inheritance of two different traits simultaneously, like seed color and seed shape in pea plants. Mendel found that these traits are inherited independently of each other.

  • Males have XY chromosomes.

🎙️ In humans, the sex of an individual is determined by specific chromosomes called sex chromosomes. Females typically have two X chromosomes (XX), while males have one X and one Y chromosome (XY).

How Sex is Determined

  • Female gametes (eggs) always carry X chromosome.
  • Male gametes (sperms) carry either X or Y chromosome.
  • If X sperm fertilizes egg: XX (female).
  • If Y sperm fertilizes egg: XY (male).

🎙️ During reproduction, the female egg always contributes an X chromosome. The male sperm can contribute either an X or a Y chromosome. If an X-carrying sperm fertilizes the egg, the child will be female (XX). If a Y-carrying sperm fertilizes the egg, the child will be male (XY). So, the father determines the sex of the child.

Key Terms in Heredity

  • Genotype: Genetic makeup (e.g., TT, Tt, tt).
  • Phenotype: Observable trait (e.g., Tall, Dwarf).
  • Homozygous: Two identical alleles (TT or tt).
  • Heterozygous: Two different alleles (Tt).

🎙️ Before we wrap up, let's quickly review some important vocabulary. Understanding these terms – genotype, phenotype, homozygous, and heterozygous – is crucial for discussing and solving problems in genetics.

What is Electricity?

  • Electricity is a form of energy.
  • It is due to the flow of electric charges.
  • It powers most of our devices and appliances.

🎙️ Think of electricity as the movement of tiny particles, much like water flowing in a river. This flow carries energy, which we can harness to do work, like lighting up a bulb or running a fan.

Electric Charge

  • Electric charge is a fundamental property of matter.
  • There are two types: positive (+) and negative (-).
  • Like charges repel, unlike charges attract.
  • Its SI unit is Coulomb (C).

🎙️ All matter is made of atoms, which contain protons (positive charge) and electrons (negative charge). The interaction between these charges is what creates electrical phenomena. A Coulomb is a very large amount of charge.

Electric Current

  • Electric current is the rate of flow of electric charges.
  • Formula: I = Q/t (Charge / Time).
  • Its SI unit is Ampere (A).
  • Conventionally, current flows from positive to negative terminal.

🎙️ Imagine a highway where cars are electric charges. The rate at which these cars pass a certain point is like the electric current. One Ampere means one Coulomb of charge flows per second.

Measuring Electric Current

  • An Ammeter is used to measure electric current.
  • It is always connected in series in a circuit.
  • It has very low resistance to avoid affecting the current.

🎙️ To measure the current flowing through a component, you must place the ammeter directly in the path of the current, like breaking a circuit and inserting the ammeter. This ensures all the current passes through it.

Electric Potential and Potential Difference

  • Potential difference (V) is the work done to move a unit charge.
  • Formula: V = W/Q (Work / Charge).
  • Its SI unit is Volt (V).
  • It's like pressure pushing water through a pipe.

🎙️ For current to flow, there must be a difference in electrical 'pressure' or potential. Just as water flows from a higher level to a lower level, charge flows from higher potential to lower potential. A battery provides this potential difference.

🎙️ Unlike an ammeter, a voltmeter is connected across the two points where you want to measure the potential difference. It's like measuring the pressure difference between two points in a pipe without stopping the flow.

Ohm's Law

  • States that V ∝ I (Voltage is proportional to Current).
  • Provided temperature and physical conditions remain constant.
  • Formula: V = IR, where R is the Resistance.
  • Discovered by Georg Simon Ohm.

🎙️ Ohm's Law is a cornerstone of electricity. It tells us that if you increase the voltage across a resistor, the current flowing through it will increase proportionally, as long as the resistance stays the same.

Resistance

  • Resistance is the opposition to the flow of current.
  • Its SI unit is Ohm (Ω).
  • Factors: Length (L), Area (A), Material (ρ), Temperature.
  • R = ρL/A (where ρ is resistivity).

🎙️ Think of resistance as a narrow or bumpy road for the electric charges. A longer wire has more resistance, a thicker wire has less resistance. Different materials also offer different levels of resistance.

Worked Example 1: Ohm's Law

  • Question: A 12V battery is connected across a 2Ω resistor.
  • Calculate the current flowing through the resistor.
  • Given: V = 12V, R = 2Ω.
  • Using Ohm's Law: I = V/R = 12V / 2Ω = 6A.

🎙️ This is a straightforward application of Ohm's Law. Always write down the given values and the formula you intend to use. Remember to include the correct units in your final answer.

Resistors in Series

  • Resistors are connected end-to-end.
  • Total resistance (Rs) is the sum of individual resistances.
  • Rs = R1 + R2 + R3 + ...
  • Current is the same through each resistor.

🎙️ When resistors are in series, there's only one path for the current to flow. It's like adding more obstacles one after another in a single lane. The total opposition to current increases.

Worked Example 2: Series Resistance

  • Question: Three resistors of 2Ω, 3Ω, and 5Ω are connected in series.
  • What is the total equivalent resistance?
  • Given: R1=2Ω, R2=3Ω, R3=5Ω.
  • Rs = R1 + R2 + R3 = 2Ω + 3Ω + 5Ω = 10Ω.

🎙️ This example shows how simple it is to calculate total resistance in a series circuit. Just add them up! This equivalent resistance can then be used with Ohm's Law for the entire circuit.

Resistors in Parallel

  • Resistors are connected across the same two points.
  • Total resistance (Rp) is given by 1/Rp = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + ...
  • Voltage is the same across each resistor.
  • Current divides among the branches.

🎙️ In a parallel connection, there are multiple paths for the current. It's like having several lanes on a highway. Adding more paths actually decreases the total resistance, making it easier for current to flow.

Worked Example 3: Parallel Resistance

  • Question: Two resistors of 6Ω and 3Ω are connected in parallel.
  • Calculate their equivalent resistance.
  • Given: R1=6Ω, R2=3Ω.
  • 1/Rp = 1/6 + 1/3 = 1/6 + 2/6 = 3/6 = 1/2. So, Rp = 2Ω.

🎙️ Notice that the equivalent resistance in parallel (2Ω) is less than the smallest individual resistance (3Ω). This is a key characteristic of parallel circuits and a good way to check your calculations.

Heating Effect of Electric Current

  • When current flows through a resistor, electrical energy converts to heat.
  • This is due to collisions between electrons and atoms.
  • Joule's Law of Heating: H = I²Rt.
  • H is heat produced, I is current, R is resistance, t is time.

🎙️ You experience the heating effect every day. When you use an electric heater or even your phone for a long time, they get warm. This is because some electrical energy is 'lost' as heat due to the resistance of the components.

Electric Power

  • Electric power is the rate at which electrical energy is consumed or dissipated.
  • Formula: P = VI (Voltage × Current).
  • Other formulas: P = I²R and P = V²/R.
  • Its SI unit is Watt (W).

🎙️ Power tells us how quickly an appliance uses energy. A 100-Watt bulb consumes energy twice as fast as a 50-Watt bulb. Understanding power helps us choose efficient appliances.

Commercial Unit of Electrical Energy

  • The SI unit of energy is Joule (J).
  • For commercial purposes, Kilowatt-hour (kWh) is used.
  • 1 kWh = 3.6 × 10⁶ Joules.
  • This is what your electricity meter measures.

🎙️ Your electricity bill is calculated based on the number of kilowatt-hours you consume. One kilowatt-hour means using 1000 Watts of power for one hour. This unit is more practical for large amounts of energy.

Worked Example 4: Power and Energy

  • Question: A bulb is rated 60W, 220V. Find the current it draws.
  • Given: P = 60W, V = 220V.
  • Using P = VI, so I = P/V = 60W / 220V ≈ 0.27A.
  • How much energy does it consume in 5 hours? E = P × t = 60W × 5h = 300Wh = 0.3kWh.

🎙️ This example combines power and energy calculations. Remember to convert units if necessary, especially for energy in kilowatt-hours, which requires power in kilowatts and time in hours.

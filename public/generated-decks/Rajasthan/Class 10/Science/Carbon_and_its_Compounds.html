Covalent Bonding in Carbon

  • Carbon has 4 electrons in its outermost shell.
  • It cannot gain or lose 4 electrons easily.
  • Instead, it shares electrons with other atoms.
  • This sharing forms strong 'Covalent Bonds'.

🎙️ To achieve a stable electron configuration, carbon atoms do not tend to gain or lose four electrons. Doing so would require too much energy. Instead, carbon prefers to share its valence electrons with other atoms, forming strong covalent bonds.

How Covalent Bonds Form (Methane Example)

  • Carbon shares 4 electrons, one with each Hydrogen.
  • Each Hydrogen shares 1 electron with Carbon.
  • Both achieve a stable electron configuration.
  • Example: Methane (CH₄) – a simple carbon compound.

🎙️ Let's look at methane, the simplest hydrocarbon. A carbon atom shares its four valence electrons with four hydrogen atoms. Each hydrogen atom shares its single electron with carbon, resulting in a stable molecule where both carbon and hydrogen have complete outer shells.

Tetravalency of Carbon

  • Carbon has a valency of four.
  • This means it can form four bonds.
  • These bonds can be with other carbon atoms or other elements.
  • This property allows for complex structures.

🎙️ The ability of carbon to form four bonds is called tetravalency. This unique characteristic allows carbon to connect with up to four other atoms simultaneously, leading to a vast array of compounds with diverse structures and properties. Think of it as having four hands to join with others.

Catenation: Carbon's Self-Linking Power

  • Carbon atoms can link with other carbon atoms.
  • They form long chains, branched chains, or rings.
  • This property is called Catenation.
  • It's the reason for the huge number of carbon compounds.

🎙️ Catenation is a truly remarkable property of carbon. Imagine carbon atoms holding hands to form long lines, or even circles! This self-linking ability allows for the creation of incredibly diverse and complex molecular structures, from simple gases to large biological molecules.

Allotropes of Carbon: Different Forms, Same Element

  • Allotropes are different structural forms of an element.
  • They have different physical properties but same chemical properties.
  • Carbon has several allotropes.
  • Examples: Diamond, Graphite, Fullerenes.

🎙️ Allotropes are fascinating because they show how the same element can exist in completely different physical forms. Carbon's allotropes, such as diamond and graphite, look and feel very different, yet they are both made purely of carbon atoms arranged in distinct ways.

Diamond: The Hardest Allotrope

  • Each carbon atom is bonded to four other carbon atoms.
  • Forms a rigid, three-dimensional structure.
  • Extremely hard and a poor conductor of electricity.
  • Used in jewellery and for cutting tools.

🎙️ Diamond is famous for its incredible hardness and brilliant sparkle. This is due to its strong, tightly packed crystal structure where every carbon atom is covalently bonded to four others. It's so hard that it's used in industrial drills and cutting instruments.

💎
Sparkling Hardness

Graphite: The Soft and Conductive Allotrope

  • Carbon atoms arranged in hexagonal layers.
  • Layers can slide over each other, making it soft.
  • Good conductor of electricity.
  • Used in pencils and as a lubricant.

🎙️ Graphite, commonly found in pencil 'lead', is surprisingly soft and slippery. Unlike diamond, its carbon atoms are arranged in flat layers that can easily slide past each other. This layered structure also allows for the movement of electrons, making graphite a good electrical conductor.

✏️

Fullerenes: The Cage-like Allotrope

  • Spherical molecules of carbon atoms.
  • Example: Buckminsterfullerene (C₆₀).
  • Looks like a soccer ball.
  • Has potential uses in medicine and nanotechnology.

🎙️ Fullerenes are a more recently discovered allotrope of carbon, with C₆₀ being the most well-known, often called a 'buckyball'. Its spherical, cage-like structure resembles a football. Scientists are exploring its applications in drug delivery and advanced materials.

Hydrocarbons: Compounds of Carbon and Hydrogen

  • Organic compounds made of only Carbon and Hydrogen.
  • They are the simplest organic compounds.
  • Form the basis of many fuels (petrol, diesel).
  • Can be saturated or unsaturated.

🎙️ Hydrocarbons are the building blocks of organic chemistry. As their name suggests, they are composed solely of carbon and hydrogen atoms. They are incredibly important, forming the backbone of fossil fuels and many industrial chemicals.

Saturated Hydrocarbons (Alkanes)

  • Contain only single bonds between carbon atoms.
  • They are very stable and less reactive.
  • General formula: CnH₂n+₂.
  • Example: Ethane (C₂H₆).

🎙️ Saturated hydrocarbons, also known as alkanes, are characterized by having only single covalent bonds between their carbon atoms. This makes them 'saturated' with hydrogen atoms. They are relatively unreactive and are common components of natural gas and petroleum.

➖
Single Bonds
13 / 25

Unsaturated Hydrocarbons (Alkenes)

  • Contain at least one double bond between carbon atoms.
  • More reactive than alkanes.
  • General formula: CnH₂n.
  • Example: Ethene (C₂H₄) – used to ripen fruits.

🎙️ Alkenes are unsaturated hydrocarbons because they contain at least one carbon-carbon double bond. This double bond makes them more reactive than alkanes. Ethene, for example, is a plant hormone that helps fruits ripen naturally.

🪢
Double Bond

Unsaturated Hydrocarbons (Alkynes)

  • Contain at least one triple bond between carbon atoms.
  • Even more reactive than alkenes.
  • General formula: CnH₂n-₂.
  • Example: Ethyne (C₂H₂) – used in welding.

🎙️ Alkynes are another type of unsaturated hydrocarbon, featuring at least one carbon-carbon triple bond. These molecules are highly reactive due to the presence of the triple bond. Ethyne, also known as acetylene, is widely used in oxy-acetylene torches for welding and cutting metals.

Haloalkanes: Carbon with Halogens

  • Hydrogen atom replaced by a halogen (F, Cl, Br, I).
  • Commonly used as solvents and refrigerants.
  • Example: Chloromethane (CH₃Cl).
  • The '-Cl' is the functional group here.

🎙️ Haloalkanes are formed when one or more hydrogen atoms in an alkane are replaced by a halogen atom. Chloromethane, for instance, is a simple example where a chlorine atom is attached. These compounds have various industrial applications, including in aerosol propellants and as solvents.

Alcohols: The Hydroxyl Group (-OH)

  • Contains the hydroxyl functional group (-OH).
  • Often used as solvents and in beverages.
  • Example: Ethanol (CH₃CH₂OH).
  • Ethanol is found in alcoholic drinks.

🎙️ Alcohols are characterized by the presence of the hydroxyl (-OH) functional group. Ethanol is a very common alcohol, known for its use in alcoholic beverages and as a solvent in many products. It's also used as a fuel additive.

Aldehydes: The Carbonyl Group at the End (-CHO)

  • Contains a carbonyl group (-C=O) at the end of a chain.
  • The carbon is also bonded to a hydrogen atom.
  • Example: Methanal (HCHO) – used as a preservative.
  • Often have pungent smells.

🎙️ Aldehydes have a carbonyl group where the carbon atom is bonded to at least one hydrogen and an alkyl group. Methanal, also known as formaldehyde, is a well-known aldehyde used as a preservative for biological specimens. Many aldehydes are recognized by their strong, often distinct, odors.

Ketones: The Carbonyl Group in the Middle (-CO-)

  • Contains a carbonyl group (-C=O) within the carbon chain.
  • The carbonyl carbon is bonded to two other carbon atoms.
  • Example: Propanone (CH₃COCH₃) – nail polish remover.
  • Good solvents for many organic substances.

🎙️ Ketones are similar to aldehydes in having a carbonyl group, but in ketones, the carbonyl carbon is located within the carbon chain, bonded to two other carbon atoms. Propanone, or acetone, is a common ketone used as a solvent, notably in nail polish removers.

Carboxylic Acids: The Carboxyl Group (-COOH)

  • Contains the carboxyl functional group (-COOH).
  • These compounds are acidic in nature.
  • Example: Ethanoic acid (CH₃COOH) – main component of vinegar.
  • Give sour taste to many foods.

🎙️ Carboxylic acids are characterized by the carboxyl functional group, which consists of both a carbonyl and a hydroxyl group attached to the same carbon. Ethanoic acid, also known as acetic acid, is the key ingredient that gives vinegar its sour taste and preservative qualities.

Chemical Properties: Combustion

  • Carbon compounds burn in air (oxygen).
  • They produce carbon dioxide, water, heat, and light.
  • This is an exothermic reaction.
  • Example: Burning of Methane (natural gas).

🎙️ One of the most important chemical properties of carbon compounds is combustion, or burning. When hydrocarbons burn completely in sufficient oxygen, they release a lot of energy in the form of heat and light, producing carbon dioxide and water. This is why they are excellent fuels.

What is Light?

  • Light is a form of energy that enables us to see objects.
  • It is a form of electromagnetic radiation.
  • Light itself is invisible, but it makes other things visible.

🎙️ Think about how you see things. It's all thanks to light! Light travels from a source, reflects off objects, and then enters our eyes, allowing us to perceive them.

Key Properties of Light

  • Light travels in a straight line (rectilinear propagation).
  • It does not require a medium to travel (can travel through vacuum).
  • The speed of light in vacuum is approximately 3 x 10^8 m/s.

🎙️ These properties are fundamental to understanding how light behaves. The idea of light traveling in straight lines is crucial for drawing ray diagrams later on.

Reflection of Light

  • Reflection is the bouncing back of light when it strikes a surface.
  • The surface can be smooth and polished, like a mirror.
  • This phenomenon allows us to see our own image.

🎙️ When light hits a surface and bounces back, we call it reflection. This is the basic principle behind how mirrors work and how we see objects around us.

Laws of Reflection

  • The angle of incidence is equal to the angle of reflection.
  • The incident ray, the reflected ray, and the normal to the surface all lie in the same plane.

🎙️ These two laws are universally true for all types of reflection. Remember, the 'normal' is an imaginary line perpendicular to the surface at the point of incidence.

Real-life Example: Seeing Your Image

  • When you look into a plane mirror, you see your own image.
  • Light rays from your face strike the mirror and reflect.
  • These reflected rays enter your eyes, forming an image.

🎙️ This is the most common example of reflection. The mirror doesn't create you; it simply reflects the light coming from you, allowing your eyes to perceive an image.

Types of Mirrors

  • Mirrors are broadly classified into Plane mirrors and Spherical mirrors.
  • Plane mirrors have a flat reflecting surface.
  • Spherical mirrors have a curved reflecting surface.

🎙️ While plane mirrors are common for daily use, spherical mirrors offer unique properties due to their curvature, leading to diverse applications.

Image Formation by Plane Mirrors

  • The image formed is virtual and erect.
  • It is laterally inverted (left appears right).
  • The image is the same size as the object.
  • The image is formed as far behind the mirror as the object is in front.

🎙️ These are the four key characteristics of an image formed by a plane mirror. Lateral inversion is why an ambulance writes its name backwards.

Spherical Mirrors: Concave and Convex

  • Spherical mirrors are parts of a hollow sphere.
  • Concave mirrors curve inwards, like a spoon's inner surface.
  • Convex mirrors curve outwards, like a spoon's outer surface.

🎙️ Imagine a glass sphere; if you cut a part of it and polish one of its surfaces, you get a spherical mirror. The inner or outer polish determines its type.

Key Terms for Spherical Mirrors

  • Pole (P): Centre of the mirror's reflecting surface.
  • Centre of Curvature (C): Centre of the sphere from which the mirror is cut.
  • Radius of Curvature (R): Distance from the Pole to the Centre of Curvature.
  • Principal Axis: Straight line passing through P and C.

🎙️ Understanding these terms is crucial for accurately drawing ray diagrams and comprehending image formation. They form the basis of our coordinate system for mirrors.

Focal Point and Focal Length (Concave Mirror)

  • Principal Focus (F): Point on the principal axis where parallel rays converge after reflection.
  • Focal Length (f): Distance from the Pole (P) to the Principal Focus (F).
  • For spherical mirrors, f = R/2.

🎙️ The focal point is a very important concept. For a concave mirror, it's a real point where light actually converges. This relationship f=R/2 is vital for calculations.

Focal Point and Focal Length (Convex Mirror)

  • Principal Focus (F): Point on the principal axis from which parallel rays *appear* to diverge after reflection.
  • Focal Length (f): Distance from the Pole (P) to the Principal Focus (F).
  • For spherical mirrors, f = R/2.

🎙️ For a convex mirror, the focal point is virtual because the reflected rays only *appear* to come from it. The formula f=R/2 still holds true.

Rules for Ray Diagrams (Concave Mirror)

  • Ray parallel to principal axis passes through F after reflection.
  • Ray passing through F becomes parallel to principal axis after reflection.
  • Ray passing through C is reflected back along the same path.
  • Ray incident at P is reflected symmetrically with respect to the principal axis.

🎙️ These three rules are your toolkit for drawing accurate ray diagrams. You only need to use any two of these rays to find the position and nature of the image.

Uses of Concave Mirrors

  • Used in torches, searchlights, and vehicle headlights to get powerful parallel beams of light.

🎙️ The converging property of concave mirrors makes them highly versatile. From focusing light to magnifying objects, their applications are widespread.

Refraction of Light

  • Refraction is the bending of light as it passes from one transparent medium to another.
  • This bending occurs at the boundary between the two media.
  • Examples include light passing from air to water or air to glass.

🎙️ Unlike reflection, where light bounces back, refraction involves light passing through a surface and changing direction. This is why objects in water appear distorted.

Cause of Refraction: Change in Speed

  • Light travels at different speeds in different transparent media.
  • When light enters a denser medium, its speed decreases, and it bends towards the normal.
  • When light enters a rarer medium, its speed increases, and it bends away from the normal.

🎙️ The change in the speed of light is the fundamental reason for refraction. Imagine a car moving from a smooth road to a muddy patch – it changes direction.

Laws of Refraction (Snell's Law)

  • The incident ray, the refracted ray, and the normal to the interface at the point of incidence, all lie in the same plane.
  • The ratio of the sine of the angle of incidence to the sine of the angle of refraction is a constant for a given pair of media (Snell's Law).

🎙️ Similar to reflection, the first law ensures coplanarity. The second law, known as Snell's Law, is crucial for quantitative analysis of refraction and introduces the concept of refractive index.

1Rays & Normal in same plane
2sin i / sin r = constant

Refractive Index

  • The constant in Snell's Law is called the refractive index (n).
  • It is the ratio of the speed of light in vacuum to the speed of light in the medium (absolute refractive index).
  • It indicates how much light bends when entering a medium.

🎙️ The refractive index is a very important property of a medium. A higher refractive index means light bends more when entering that medium.

Real-life Example: Pencil in Water

  • A pencil partially immersed in water appears bent at the water surface.
  • Light from the submerged part of the pencil refracts as it passes from water to air.
  • Our eyes perceive the light rays as coming from a different position, making the pencil appear bent.

🎙️ This everyday observation is a classic example of refraction. The apparent bending is an optical illusion caused by the change in light's path.

Recap: Light — Reflection and Refraction

  • Light is energy that helps us see, traveling in straight lines.
  • Reflection is light bouncing off a surface, following specific laws.
  • Mirrors (plane, concave, convex) form images based on reflection.
  • Refraction is light bending as it passes through different media due to speed change.

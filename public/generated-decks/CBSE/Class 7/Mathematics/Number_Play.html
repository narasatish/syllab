Factors: Breaking Down Numbers

  • A factor divides a number completely, with no remainder.
  • Every number has at least two factors: 1 and itself.
  • Factors are always less than or equal to the number.

🎙️ Think of factors as the building blocks of a number. If you can divide a number by another number and get a whole number result with nothing left over, then that second number is a factor. For example, 2 is a factor of 10 because 10 divided by 2 is exactly 5.

Multiples: Building Up Numbers

  • A multiple is the result of multiplying a number by an integer.
  • Multiples are always greater than or equal to the number.
  • There are infinitely many multiples for any given number.

🎙️ If factors are about breaking down, multiples are about building up! When you multiply a number by 1, 2, 3, and so on, the results you get are its multiples. Think of skip-counting; those are multiples. For example, multiples of 2 are 2, 4, 6, 8, and so on.

  • 3 x 2 = 6

Prime Numbers: The Lone Rangers

  • A prime number has exactly two factors: 1 and itself.
  • Examples: 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, etc.
  • The number 1 is NOT a prime number.

🎙️ Prime numbers are very special! They can only be divided evenly by 1 and themselves. Think of them as unique numbers that can't be broken down into smaller whole number products, apart from 1 times themselves. The number 2 is the only even prime number.

Composite Numbers: The Team Players

  • A composite number has more than two factors.
  • Examples: 4, 6, 8, 9, 10, 12, etc.
  • All numbers greater than 1 are either prime or composite.

🎙️ Composite numbers are the opposite of prime numbers. They can be formed by multiplying smaller prime numbers together, or have more than two factors. For example, 4 has factors 1, 2, and 4. They are like numbers that can be built from different combinations of smaller numbers.

Real-Life Example: Arranging Students

  • If you have 7 students, how many equal rows can you make?
  • Only 1 row of 7 or 7 rows of 1.
  • 7 is a prime number because it only has factors 1 and 7.

🎙️ Imagine you have 7 students and want to arrange them into equal rows for a photo. You can either have one long row of 7 students, or 7 individual rows with one student each. You can't make 2 or 3 equal rows. This shows that 7 is a prime number.

Divisibility Rule for 2

  • A number is divisible by 2 if its last digit is even.
  • Even digits are 0, 2, 4, 6, 8.
  • Example: 128 is divisible by 2 (ends in 8).

🎙️ This is one of the easiest divisibility rules! Just look at the very last digit of the number. If it's an even number like 0, 2, 4, 6, or 8, then the entire number can be divided by 2 without any remainder. For instance, 456 is divisible by 2 because it ends in 6.

Divisibility Rule for 3

  • A number is divisible by 3 if the sum of its digits is divisible by 3.
  • Example: For 123, 1+2+3 = 6.
  • Since 6 is divisible by 3, 123 is divisible by 3.

🎙️ The rule for 3 is a bit different. You need to add up all the individual digits of the number. If that sum is a number that can be divided by 3, then the original number is also divisible by 3. Try it with 72: 7 + 2 = 9, and 9 is divisible by 3, so 72 is too.

Divisibility Rule for 5

  • A number is divisible by 5 if its last digit is 0 or 5.
  • Example: 75 is divisible by 5 (ends in 5).
  • Example: 210 is divisible by 5 (ends in 0).

🎙️ This rule is also quite straightforward. For a number to be divisible by 5, its last digit must either be a 0 or a 5. Any number ending in these digits will give a whole number when divided by 5. For example, 100 ends in 0, so it's divisible by 5.

Why Do We Need Divisibility Rules?

  • To quickly check if a number is divisible by another.
  • Saves time in calculations and problem-solving.
  • Helps in simplifying fractions and finding factors.

🎙️ Divisibility rules are like shortcuts! Instead of performing long division every time, these rules help us quickly determine if one number can be divided by another. This is super useful for simplifying fractions, finding factors, and solving various math problems faster.

Common Factors: What Numbers Share

  • Factors that are shared by two or more numbers.
  • Example: Factors of 12 are {1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12}.
  • Factors of 18 are {1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 18}.
  • Common factors of 12 and 18 are {1, 2, 3, 6}.

🎙️ When we look at the factors of two different numbers, we often find some factors that appear in both lists. These are called common factors. It's like finding common friends between two groups of people. For 12 and 18, the numbers 1, 2, 3, and 6 are common to both.

Common Multiples: Where Numbers Meet

  • Multiples that are shared by two or more numbers.
  • Example: Multiples of 3 are {3, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18, 21, 24...}.
  • Multiples of 4 are {4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24, 28...}.
  • Common multiples of 3 and 4 are {12, 24, ...}.

🎙️ Just like common factors, we can also find common multiples. These are the numbers that appear in the list of multiples for two or more different numbers. It's where the 'skip counting' of different numbers eventually meets up. For 3 and 4, the first common multiple is 12.

HCF: Highest Common Factor

  • The largest of all the common factors of two or more numbers.
  • Also known as Greatest Common Divisor (GCD).
  • Example: Common factors of 12 and 18 are {1, 2, 3, 6}. HCF = 6.

🎙️ Among all the common factors that two or more numbers share, the Highest Common Factor, or HCF, is simply the biggest one. It's the largest number that can divide both (or all) the given numbers without leaving a remainder. Finding the HCF is very useful in simplifying fractions.

LCM: Lowest Common Multiple

  • The smallest non-zero common multiple of two or more numbers.
  • Example: Common multiples of 3 and 4 are {12, 24, ...}. LCM = 12.

🎙️ Similar to HCF, the Lowest Common Multiple, or LCM, is the smallest number that is a multiple of two or more given numbers. It's the first number where their 'skip counting' sequences meet. LCM is important when we need to add or subtract fractions with different denominators.

Real-Life Example: Distributing Sweets

  • You have 12 ladoos and 18 jalebis.
  • What is the maximum number of friends you can share them equally with?
  • Find the HCF of 12 and 18. (HCF = 6)
  • You can share with 6 friends, each getting 2 ladoos and 3 jalebis.

🎙️ Imagine you have a plate of 12 ladoos and another of 18 jalebis. You want to distribute them among your friends so that each friend gets an equal number of both types of sweets, and no sweets are left over. To find the maximum number of friends, you need to find the HCF of 12 and 18, which is 6.

Real-Life Example: Bus Schedules

  • Bus A leaves every 15 minutes.
  • Bus B leaves every 20 minutes.
  • If they both left at 9:00 AM, when will they leave together again?
  • Find the LCM of 15 and 20. (LCM = 60 minutes)

🎙️ Consider two buses from the same station. One bus departs every 15 minutes, and another every 20 minutes. If they both departed at 9:00 AM, to find out when they will next depart together, we need to find the LCM of 15 and 20. The LCM is 60, meaning they will leave together again after 60 minutes, at 10:00 AM.

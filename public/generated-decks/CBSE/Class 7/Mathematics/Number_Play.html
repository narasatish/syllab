Factors: The Building Blocks

  • A factor divides a number exactly, with no remainder.
  • Every number has at least two factors: 1 and itself.
  • Factors are always less than or equal to the number.

🎙️ Remember factors from previous classes? They are like the building blocks of a number. If you can divide a number perfectly by another number, that number is a factor. For example, 2 is a factor of 10 because 10 divided by 2 is exactly 5.

Example: Finding Factors of 12

  • 1 x 12 = 12
  • 2 x 6 = 12
  • 3 x 4 = 12
  • Factors of 12 are: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12.

🎙️ Let's find the factors of 12. We look for pairs of numbers that multiply to give 12. We start from 1 and go up. So, 1 and 12, 2 and 6, 3 and 4 are the pairs. This means 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, and 12 are all factors of 12.

Multiples: Repeated Additions

  • A multiple is the result of multiplying a number by an integer.
  • Multiples of a number go on infinitely.
  • Every number is a multiple of itself.

🎙️ Multiples are the numbers you get when you multiply a given number by 1, 2, 3, and so on. Think of it as skip-counting. For example, multiples of 3 are 3, 6, 9, 12, and so on. They keep growing and are always greater than or equal to the number itself.

  • 5 x 1 = 5
  • 5 x 2 = 10
  • 5 x 3 = 15
  • First few multiples of 5: 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, ...

🎙️ To find the multiples of 5, we simply multiply 5 by consecutive whole numbers. This gives us 5, 10, 15, 20, and so on. Notice how these numbers are all part of the 5 times table.

Divisibility Rules: Your Shortcut!

  • Quickly check if a number can be divided by another without remainder.
  • Useful for simplifying fractions and finding factors.
  • Saves time in calculations, especially with large numbers.

🎙️ Imagine having a very large number and needing to know if it can be divided by 2 or 3. Instead of doing the long division, divisibility rules offer a quick mental check. These rules are super helpful for many math problems!

Rule for Divisibility by 2

  • A number is divisible by 2 if its last digit is an even number.
  • Even numbers are 0, 2, 4, 6, 8.
  • Example: 348 (ends in 8) is divisible by 2.

🎙️ This is one of the easiest rules! Just look at the very last digit of the number. If it's 0, 2, 4, 6, or 8, then the entire number can be divided by 2. It's like checking if you can make perfect pairs with the items.

Rule for Divisibility by 3

  • A number is divisible by 3 if the sum of its digits is divisible by 3.
  • Example: For 123, 1+2+3 = 6. Since 6 is divisible by 3, 123 is divisible by 3.

🎙️ This rule requires a small calculation. Add up all the digits in the number. If that sum is a multiple of 3, then the original number is also divisible by 3. It's a clever trick to avoid long division.

Rule for Divisibility by 4

  • A number is divisible by 4 if the number formed by its last two digits is divisible by 4.
  • Example: For 716, the last two digits form 16. Since 16 is divisible by 4, 716 is divisible by 4.

🎙️ For this rule, we only need to focus on the end of the number. Take the last two digits and see if that smaller number is a multiple of 4. If it is, then the whole big number is divisible by 4. This is very useful for larger numbers.

🎙️ Another very simple rule! Just like divisibility by 2, you only need to look at the last digit. If it's a 0 or a 5, then the number is perfectly divisible by 5. Think of counting by fives, they always end in 0 or 5.

Rule for Divisibility by 6

  • A number is divisible by 6 if it is divisible by BOTH 2 and 3.
  • Example: For 42, it's even (divisible by 2) AND 4+2=6 (divisible by 3). So, 42 is divisible by 6.

🎙️ This rule combines two other rules. To check for divisibility by 6, you must check if the number passes both the divisibility test for 2 AND the divisibility test for 3. If it passes both, then it's divisible by 6.

Rule for Divisibility by 9

  • A number is divisible by 9 if the sum of its digits is divisible by 9.
  • This is similar to the rule for 3, but the sum must be a multiple of 9.

🎙️ The rule for 9 is very similar to the rule for 3. You add up all the digits, but this time, the sum must be divisible by 9. If the sum is a multiple of 9, then the original number is also a multiple of 9.

Rule for Divisibility by 11

  • Find the difference between the sum of digits at odd places and the sum of digits at even places.
  • If this difference is 0 or a multiple of 11, the number is divisible by 11.
  • Example: For 1331: (1+3) - (3+1) = 4 - 4 = 0. So, 1331 is divisible by 11.

🎙️ This rule is a bit more involved but very clever. You alternate adding and subtracting digits, starting from the rightmost digit. If the final result is 0 or a multiple of 11, then the number is divisible by 11. Practice this one a few times!

HCF: The Biggest Common Factor

  • The HCF of two or more numbers is the largest factor that they share.
  • Also known as Greatest Common Divisor (GCD).
  • Useful for grouping items into largest possible equal sets.

🎙️ The HCF is all about finding the biggest number that can divide into two or more numbers without leaving any remainder. It's like finding the common ground between numbers, but specifically the largest one. We use it when we want to divide things into the largest possible equal groups.

  • Factors of 12: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12
  • Factors of 18: 1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 18
  • Common factors: 1, 2, 3, 6
  • Highest Common Factor (HCF) is 6.

🎙️ Let's find the HCF of 12 and 18. First, list all factors for both numbers. Then, identify the factors that appear in both lists. Finally, pick the largest number from the common factors. In this case, 6 is the largest common factor.

LCM: The Smallest Common Multiple

  • The LCM of two or more numbers is the smallest non-zero multiple that they share.
  • Useful for finding when events will happen at the same time again.
  • Important for adding/subtracting fractions with different denominators.

🎙️ The LCM is the smallest number that is a multiple of two or more given numbers. Think of it as the first point where numbers 'meet' if they are skip-counting. We use LCM often in real life, like when two buses leave at different intervals and you want to know when they'll leave together again.

Example: Finding LCM of 4 and 6

  • Multiples of 4: 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24, ...
  • Multiples of 6: 6, 12, 18, 24, 30, ...
  • Common Multiples: 12, 24, ...
  • Lowest Common Multiple (LCM) is 12.

🎙️ To find the LCM of 4 and 6, we list out their multiples until we find the first number that appears in both lists. For 4, the multiples are 4, 8, 12... For 6, they are 6, 12... The first common multiple is 12, so that's our LCM.

HCF & LCM in Everyday Life

  • HCF: Dividing items into largest equal groups (e.g., distributing candies).
  • LCM: Finding when events will coincide (e.g., two bells ringing together).
  • Both help in solving practical problems efficiently.

🎙️ HCF and LCM are not just abstract concepts. HCF helps when you need to divide things into the largest possible equal parts, like arranging books on shelves. LCM helps when you need to find a common point in time or quantity, like scheduling events or finding a common denominator in fractions.

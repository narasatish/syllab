Understanding Integers

  • Integers include all whole numbers (0, 1, 2, ...) and their negative counterparts (-1, -2, -3, ...).
  • They do not include fractions or decimals.
  • Represented on a number line.

🎙️ Integers are essentially whole numbers and their opposites. The number line is an excellent tool to visualise integers, with positive numbers to the right of zero and negative numbers to the left.

Absolute Value: Distance from Zero

  • The absolute value of an integer is its distance from zero on the number line.
  • It is always non-negative (positive or zero).
  • Represented by two vertical bars: |a|.

🎙️ Think of absolute value as how far a number is from zero, regardless of direction. So, whether a number is positive or negative, its absolute value will always be positive, because distance cannot be negative.

Calculating Absolute Value

  • |5| = 5 (distance of 5 from zero)
  • |-5| = 5 (distance of 5 from zero)
  • |0| = 0 (distance of 0 from zero)

🎙️ Let's look at some examples. The absolute value of 5 is 5, and the absolute value of -5 is also 5. Both are 5 units away from zero on the number line. The absolute value of zero is simply zero.

Adding Integers with Same Signs

  • Add the absolute values of the integers.
  • Keep the common sign.
  • Example: 3 + 5 = 8; (-3) + (-5) = -8

🎙️ When you add integers with the same sign, it's like combining similar quantities. For example, if you gain 3 points and then gain 5 more, you've gained a total of 8 points. If you lose 3 points and then lose 5 more, you've lost 8 points.

Real-Life Addition: Same Signs

  • Scenario: You earn ₹100 on Monday and ₹50 on Tuesday.
  • Total earnings: 100 + 50 = ₹150.
  • Scenario: You lose 3 marbles, then lose 2 more marbles.
  • Total loss: (-3) + (-2) = -5 marbles.

🎙️ Imagine your pocket money. If you get ₹100 and then another ₹50, your total increases. Similarly, if you have a deficit of 3 items and then another deficit of 2 items, your total deficit increases.

Adding Integers with Different Signs

  • Subtract the smaller absolute value from the larger absolute value.
  • Keep the sign of the integer with the larger absolute value.
  • Example: 5 + (-3) = 2; (-5) + 3 = -2

🎙️ When signs are different, it's like a tug-of-war. The stronger side (the number with the larger absolute value) wins, and you subtract the weaker side. The result takes the sign of the winner.

Real-Life Addition: Different Signs

  • Scenario: You have ₹500 in your account and withdraw ₹200.
  • Balance: 500 + (-200) = ₹300.

Properties of Addition: Commutative

  • The order in which you add integers does not change the sum.
  • a + b = b + a
  • Example: 3 + (-5) = -2 and (-5) + 3 = -2

🎙️ The commutative property simply means that you can swap the positions of the numbers when adding, and the answer will remain the same. It's like putting on your socks then shoes, or shoes then socks – the end result is the same!

Change Order

Properties of Addition: Associative

  • The way you group integers for addition does not change the sum.
  • (a + b) + c = a + (b + c)
  • Example: (2 + 3) + 4 = 5 + 4 = 9; 2 + (3 + 4) = 2 + 7 = 9

🎙️ The associative property tells us that when adding three or more numbers, how you group them with parentheses doesn't matter. You can add the first two and then the third, or the second and third then the first, and the sum will be identical.

Properties of Addition: Additive Identity

  • Adding zero to any integer does not change its value.
  • a + 0 = a
  • Zero is the additive identity.
  • Example: -7 + 0 = -7

🎙️ Zero is a special number in addition. When you add zero to any integer, the integer remains unchanged. That's why zero is called the additive identity – it preserves the identity of the number.

Subtracting Integers: Add the Opposite

  • To subtract an integer, add its additive inverse (opposite).
  • a - b = a + (-b)
  • Change the subtraction sign to addition and change the sign of the second number.
  • Example: 5 - (-3) = 5 + 3 = 8

🎙️ Subtraction of integers can be tricky, but there's a simple rule: 'Keep, Change, Change'. Keep the first number, change the subtraction sign to addition, and change the sign of the second number to its opposite. Then, follow the rules of addition.

Real-Life Subtraction

  • Scenario: The temperature was 10°C and dropped by 15°C.
  • New temperature: 10 - 15 = 10 + (-15) = -5°C.
  • Scenario: A submarine is at -50m and descends another 20m.
  • New depth: -50 - 20 = -50 + (-20) = -70m.

🎙️ Let's apply this. If the temperature drops, it's a subtraction. A drop of 15 degrees from 10 degrees takes us below freezing. When a submarine descends further, its depth becomes even more negative.

Multiplying Integers: Rules of Signs

  • Positive × Positive = Positive (e.g., 3 × 4 = 12)
  • Negative × Negative = Positive (e.g., (-3) × (-4) = 12)
  • Positive × Negative = Negative (e.g., 3 × (-4) = -12)
  • Negative × Positive = Negative (e.g., (-3) × 4 = -12)

🎙️ Multiplication of integers has clear rules for signs. When the signs are the same (both positive or both negative), the product is positive. When the signs are different, the product is negative. This is a crucial rule to remember.

Properties of Multiplication

  • Commutative: a × b = b × a (e.g., 2 × (-3) = -6; (-3) × 2 = -6)
  • Associative: (a × b) × c = a × (b × c) (e.g., (2 × 3) × 4 = 24; 2 × (3 × 4) = 24)

🎙️ Good news! The commutative and associative properties also apply to multiplication of integers, just like with addition. The order and grouping of numbers don't change the final product.

Multiplicative Identity & Zero Property

  • Multiplicative Identity: a × 1 = a (1 is the multiplicative identity).
  • Zero Property: a × 0 = 0 (Any integer multiplied by zero is zero).
  • Example: -9 × 1 = -9; 15 × 0 = 0

🎙️ For multiplication, the number 1 is special. Multiplying any integer by 1 keeps its identity. And the number 0 is also very powerful: anything multiplied by zero becomes zero, always!

Dividing Integers: Rules of Signs

  • Positive ÷ Positive = Positive (e.g., 12 ÷ 4 = 3)
  • Negative ÷ Negative = Positive (e.g., (-12) ÷ (-4) = 3)
  • Positive ÷ Negative = Negative (e.g., 12 ÷ (-4) = -3)
  • Negative ÷ Positive = Negative (e.g., (-12) ÷ 4 = -3)

🎙️ The rules for signs in division are exactly the same as for multiplication. If the signs of the numbers you are dividing are the same, the quotient is positive. If they are different, the quotient is negative.

Real-Life Division

🎙️ Division helps us share or distribute quantities. If a debt is shared, each person takes on a portion of that negative amount. Similarly, an average drop in temperature over time can be found using division.

Order of Operations (BODMAS/PEMDAS)

  • To solve expressions with multiple operations, follow a specific order.
  • BODMAS: Brackets, Orders (powers/roots), Division, Multiplication, Addition, Subtraction.
  • PEMDAS: Parentheses, Exponents, Multiplication, Division, Addition, Subtraction.

🎙️ When an expression has more than one operation, we need a standard order to get the correct answer. This order is commonly known as BODMAS or PEMDAS. Always work from left to right for division/multiplication and addition/subtraction.

1Brackets/Parentheses
2Orders/Exponents
3Div/Mult (L to R)
4Add/Sub (L to R)

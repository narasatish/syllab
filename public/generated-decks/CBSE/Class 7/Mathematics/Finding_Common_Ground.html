Revisiting Factors

  • A factor divides a number exactly, with no remainder.
  • For example, the factors of 12 are 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, and 12.
  • Every number has at least two factors: 1 and itself.

🎙️ Remember factors from previous classes? They are like building blocks for numbers. If you have 10 cookies, you can divide them into 1 group of 10, 2 groups of 5, 5 groups of 2, or 10 groups of 1. These are its factors.

Understanding Multiples

  • A multiple is the result of multiplying a number by an integer.
  • For example, multiples of 3 are 3, 6, 9, 12, 15, and so on.
  • Multiples go on infinitely for any given number.

🎙️ Multiples are like skip-counting. If you count by 4s, you get 4, 8, 12, 16, and so on. These are the multiples of 4. They are the products you get when you multiply 4 by 1, 2, 3, 4, and so forth.

Prime vs. Composite Numbers

  • Prime numbers have exactly two factors: 1 and themselves (e.g., 2, 3, 5, 7).
  • Composite numbers have more than two factors (e.g., 4, 6, 8, 9, 10).
  • The number 1 is neither prime nor composite.

🎙️ This is a quick recap of prime and composite numbers, which are crucial for understanding prime factorization. Remember, prime numbers are the 'fundamental' numbers with only two factors, while composite numbers can be broken down further.

Breaking Down Numbers: Prime Factorization

  • Prime factorization is expressing a number as a product of its prime factors.
  • This can be done using a factor tree or the division method.
  • It's a unique way to represent any composite number.

🎙️ Prime factorization is like finding the secret code of a number, breaking it down into its prime components. We'll explore two main methods: the factor tree, which is very visual, and the repeated division method.

1Start with number (12)
2Divide by smallest prime (2 x 6)
3Divide composite factors (2 x 2 x 3)
4All factors are prime!
8 / 25

Worked Example: Prime Factorization of 36

  • Using the factor tree method:
  • 36 = 2 × 18
  • 18 = 2 × 9
  • 9 = 3 × 3
  • So, 36 = 2 × 2 × 3 × 3 = 2² × 3².

🎙️ Let's work through an example together. To find the prime factorization of 36, we start by dividing it by the smallest prime number, 2. We continue this process until all factors are prime numbers. This gives us 2 multiplied by 2, multiplied by 3, multiplied by 3.

What are Common Factors?

  • Common factors are factors shared by two or more numbers.
  • To find them, list all factors for each number and identify the ones that appear in all lists.
  • Example: Factors of 12 (1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12) and 18 (1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 18).

🎙️ Imagine you have two groups of balloons, some red and some blue. The common factors are like the balloons that are both red AND blue. For 12 and 18, we see that 1, 2, 3, and 6 are present in both lists of factors.

Finding the HCF (Highest Common Factor)

  • HCF is the largest factor that two or more numbers share.
  • It's also known as the Greatest Common Divisor (GCD).
  • HCF helps us divide things into the largest possible equal groups.

🎙️ The Highest Common Factor, or HCF, is the biggest number that can divide two or more numbers without leaving a remainder. Think of it as finding the 'champion' among all the common factors. It has many practical applications.

🥇
The 'Highest' Factor
11 / 25

HCF Method 1: Listing Factors

  • Step 1: List all factors of each number.
  • Step 2: Identify the factors that are common to all numbers.
  • Step 3: The largest among these common factors is the HCF.

🎙️ This method is straightforward for smaller numbers. You simply write down all the factors for each number. Then, you look for the factors that appear in every list. Finally, pick the biggest one, and that's your HCF.

1Factors of 12: 1,2,3,4,6,12
2Factors of 18: 1,2,3,6,9,18
3Common Factors: 1,2,3,6
4Highest: 6
12 / 25

HCF Method 2: Prime Factorization

  • Step 1: Find the prime factorization of each number.
  • Step 2: Identify the common prime factors.
  • Step 3: Multiply these common prime factors, taking the lowest power of each.

🎙️ For larger numbers, the prime factorization method is more efficient. After finding the prime factors of each number, you look for the prime factors that are common to all. Then, for each common prime factor, you choose the one with the smallest exponent and multiply them together.

124 = 2³ × 3
236 = 2² × 3²
3Common Primes: 2, 3
4Lowest Powers: 2², 3¹
5HCF = 2² × 3 = 12
13 / 25

Worked Example: HCF of 24 and 36

  • Prime factors of 24: 2 × 2 × 2 × 3 = 2³ × 3¹
  • Prime factors of 36: 2 × 2 × 3 × 3 = 2² × 3²
  • Common prime factors with lowest powers: 2² and 3¹
  • HCF = 2² × 3 = 4 × 3 = 12.

🎙️ Let's apply the prime factorization method. We break down both 24 and 36 into their prime factors. Then, we identify the common prime factors (2 and 3) and take the lowest power for each. Multiplying these gives us 12, which is the HCF.

Real-Life Application: HCF in Action

  • You have 24 laddoos and 36 jalebis.
  • You want to pack them into identical boxes, with no sweets left over.
  • What is the greatest number of boxes you can make?
  • Answer: HCF(24, 36) = 12 boxes.

Finding the LCM (Lowest Common Multiple)

  • LCM is the smallest non-zero multiple shared by two or more numbers.
  • It helps us find when events will happen simultaneously or when cycles align.
  • LCM is always greater than or equal to the largest of the given numbers.

🎙️ The Lowest Common Multiple, or LCM, is the smallest number that is a multiple of two or more numbers. It's like finding the first time two different schedules or cycles will coincide again. It's a very useful concept in many real-world scenarios.

🌱
The 'Lowest' Multiple
18 / 25

LCM Method 1: Listing Multiples

  • Step 1: List the first few multiples of each number.
  • Step 2: Identify the multiples that are common to all numbers.
  • Step 3: The smallest among these common multiples is the LCM.

🎙️ Similar to the HCF listing method, for LCM, you list out the multiples. Keep listing until you find a number that appears in all lists. The first number that appears in all lists is your LCM. This works well for smaller numbers.

1Multiples of 4: 4,8,12,16,20,24...
2Multiples of 6: 6,12,18,24,30...
3Common Multiples: 12, 24...
4Lowest: 12
19 / 25

LCM Method 2: Prime Factorization

  • Step 1: Find the prime factorization of each number.
  • Step 2: Identify all prime factors that appear in any of the factorizations.
  • Step 3: Multiply these prime factors, taking the highest power of each.

🎙️ The prime factorization method for LCM is also very powerful. Here, after finding the prime factors, you take every unique prime factor from all numbers. For each prime factor, you select the one with the highest exponent and multiply them all together to get the LCM.

112 = 2² × 3
218 = 2 × 3²
3All Primes: 2, 3
4Highest Powers: 2², 3²
5LCM = 2² × 3² = 36
20 / 25

Worked Example: LCM of 12 and 18

  • Prime factors of 12: 2 × 2 × 3 = 2² × 3¹
  • Prime factors of 18: 2 × 3 × 3 = 2¹ × 3²
  • All prime factors involved: 2 and 3.
  • Highest powers: 2² (from 12) and 3² (from 18).
  • LCM = 2² × 3² = 4 × 9 = 36.

🎙️ Let's apply the prime factorization method to find the LCM of 12 and 18. We list their prime factors. Then, we identify all unique prime factors and pick the highest power for each. Multiplying these highest powers gives us 36, which is the LCM.

The HCF-LCM Connection

  • For any two positive integers 'a' and 'b':
  • Product of the numbers = HCF(a, b) × LCM(a, b).
  • This formula is very useful for checking answers or finding one value if the others are known.

🎙️ There's a fascinating relationship between HCF and LCM for any two numbers. If you multiply the two numbers, the result is always equal to the product of their HCF and LCM. This formula is a powerful tool in number theory.

HCF(12, 18) = 6
LCM(12, 18) = 36
Product = 12 x 18 = 216
HCF x LCM = 6 x 36 = 216

Understanding Factors

  • Numbers that divide another number exactly.
  • No remainder is left after division.
  • Every number has at least two factors: 1 and itself.

🎙️ A factor is like a building block for a number. If you can divide a number by another number and get a whole number answer, then the divisor is a factor. For example, the factors of 10 are 1, 2, 5, and 10.

Factors in Real Life

  • Arranging items into equal groups.
  • Sharing things fairly among people.
  • Organising objects in rows and columns.

🎙️ Imagine you have 6 cookies. You can arrange them into 1 group of 6, 2 groups of 3, 3 groups of 2, or 6 groups of 1. So, 1, 2, 3, and 6 are factors of 6, representing the number of equal groups you can make.

Exploring Multiples

  • The result of multiplying a number by another whole number.
  • Multiples are like the 'times table' of a number.
  • They are always equal to or greater than the original number.

🎙️ Multiples are what you get when you count by a certain number. For instance, the multiples of 3 are 3, 6, 9, 12, and so on. They represent repeated additions of the number itself.

Multiples Around Us

  • Counting steps in a repeating pattern.
  • Scheduling events that recur at fixed intervals.
  • Calculating distances travelled over time.

🎙️ If a bus comes every 5 minutes, the times it arrives (starting from 0) are 0, 5, 10, 15, 20 minutes and so on. These are multiples of 5, helping us predict future events.

Finding Common Factors

  • Factors that are shared by two or more numbers.
  • We list all factors for each number.
  • Then, we find the ones that appear in all lists.

🎙️ When we look at two different numbers, they might have some factors that are the same. These shared factors are what we call common factors, representing numbers that can divide both.

Introducing Highest Common Factor (HCF)

  • The largest among the common factors of two or more numbers.
  • Also known as Greatest Common Divisor (GCD).
  • It's the biggest number that divides all given numbers exactly.

🎙️ Out of all the common factors we just found, the HCF is simply the biggest one. It's the largest number that can divide both numbers without leaving a remainder, a very useful concept.

🎙️ This method is straightforward for smaller numbers. It directly applies what we learned about finding common factors, with one extra step to pick the greatest value.

Worked Example: HCF of 20 and 30

  • Factors of 20: 1, 2, 4, 5, 10, 20.
  • Factors of 30: 1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 10, 15, 30.
  • Common Factors: 1, 2, 5, 10. HCF: 10.

🎙️ By listing all factors, we can clearly see the common ones and then easily pick out the highest among them, which is 10 in this case. It's the largest number that divides both 20 and 30.

Method 2: HCF by Prime Factorisation

  • Step 1: Write each number as a product of its prime factors.
  • Step 2: Identify common prime factors.
  • Step 3: Multiply these common prime factors (taking the lowest power).

🎙️ This method is very efficient, especially for larger numbers. It breaks down each number into its fundamental prime components, making it easier to spot shared factors.

1Prime Factorise
2Find Common Primes
3Multiply (Lowest Power)

Worked Example: HCF of 48 and 72

  • 48 = 2 x 2 x 2 x 2 x 3 = 2⁴ x 3¹.
  • 72 = 2 x 2 x 2 x 3 x 3 = 2³ x 3².
  • Common prime factors: 2³ and 3¹. HCF = 2³ x 3¹ = 8 x 3 = 24.

🎙️ We find the prime factors for both numbers. Then, we look for the prime factors they share and multiply them, using the lowest power they appear with in either factorisation. This gives us 24.

Introducing Lowest Common Multiple (LCM)

  • The smallest non-zero common multiple of two or more numbers.
  • It's the first number that appears in both (or all) lists of multiples.
  • The smallest number that all given numbers can divide into exactly.

🎙️ Among all the common multiples, the LCM is the smallest one. It's the smallest number that both numbers can divide into without a remainder, a crucial concept for fractions and scheduling.

⬇️
Smallest Shared

Method 1: LCM by Listing Multiples

  • Step 1: List multiples for each number.
  • Step 2: Continue listing until a common multiple is found.
  • Step 3: The first common multiple found is the LCM.

🎙️ This method is great for finding the LCM of smaller numbers. You just keep adding multiples until you hit a number that both lists share, which will be your LCM.

Worked Example: LCM of 8 and 12

  • Multiples of 8: 8, 16, 24, 32, 40...
  • Multiples of 12: 12, 24, 36, 48...
  • The first common multiple is 24. LCM: 24.

🎙️ By carefully listing the multiples, we can quickly identify 24 as the smallest number that is a multiple of both 8 and 12. This is often called the 'least common denominator' for fractions.

Method 2: LCM by Prime Factorisation

  • Step 1: Write each number as a product of its prime factors.
  • Step 2: Identify all unique prime factors from both numbers.
  • Step 3: Multiply these prime factors (taking the highest power).

🎙️ This method is also very powerful for LCM. Instead of the lowest power like HCF, for LCM we use the highest power of each unique prime factor that appears in any of the factorisations.

1Prime Factorise
2Identify All Primes
3Multiply (Highest Power)
22 / 25

Worked Example: LCM of 18 and 24

  • 18 = 2 x 3 x 3 = 2¹ x 3².
  • 24 = 2 x 2 x 2 x 3 = 2³ x 3¹.
  • Unique prime factors: 2 and 3. Highest powers: 2³ and 3². LCM = 2³ x 3² = 8 x 9 = 72.

🎙️ We list all prime factors for each number. Then, for each unique prime factor, we pick the one with the highest exponent and multiply them all together. This results in 72 as the LCM.

Recap: Finding Common Ground

  • Factors divide a number; Multiples are products of a number.
  • HCF is the largest common factor.
  • LCM is the smallest common multiple.
  • Both are fundamental tools in number theory.

🎙️ Today, we've explored how to find common factors and multiples, and understood the significance of HCF and LCM in various situations. These concepts are fundamental in mathematics and have many real-world applications!

What are Geometric Constructions?

  • Drawing geometric figures accurately.
  • Using only a compass and an unmarked ruler (straightedge).
  • No protractor allowed for basic constructions.

🎙️ Geometric constructions are all about drawing shapes with perfect accuracy. We use simple tools like a compass to draw circles and arcs, and a straightedge to draw lines. The challenge is doing it without measuring angles with a protractor!

Constructing a Line Segment of Given Length

  • Draw a ray, mark point A.
  • Open compass to desired length (e.g., 5 cm).
  • Place compass needle at A, draw an arc intersecting the ray at B.

🎙️ This is a fundamental construction. First, draw a starting line. Then, using your ruler, set your compass to the exact length you need. Finally, place the compass point on your starting point and swing an arc to mark the end of your segment.

Constructing a Perpendicular Bisector

  • Draw line segment AB.
  • With A as center, draw arcs above and below AB (radius > half AB).
  • With B as center, draw arcs intersecting previous arcs.
  • Join intersection points to get the bisector.

🎙️ A perpendicular bisector cuts a line segment exactly in half and forms a 90-degree angle with it. This construction is very useful for finding the midpoint of a line or constructing right angles.

Worked Example: Bisecting a Line Segment

  • Given segment PQ of length 6 cm.
  • Open compass to more than 3 cm.
  • Draw arcs from P and Q.
  • Connect arc intersection points.

🎙️ Let's try it with a specific length. If we have a 6 cm segment, we open our compass to more than 3 cm. This ensures our arcs will intersect. Connecting the intersection points gives us the line that cuts PQ exactly in half.

Constructing an Angle Bisector

  • Draw angle ABC.
  • With B as center, draw an arc intersecting BA and BC.
  • From intersection points, draw two more arcs inside the angle.
  • Draw a ray from B through the intersection of these arcs.

🎙️ An angle bisector divides an angle into two equal parts. This construction helps us create symmetrical figures or find the exact middle of an angle.

Worked Example: Bisecting an Angle

  • Given angle XYZ.
  • Place compass at Y, draw an arc cutting YX and YZ.
  • From these intersection points, draw two more arcs.
  • Draw a ray from Y through the new intersection.

🎙️ Imagine you have an angle, say 70 degrees. After this construction, you'll have two perfect 35-degree angles. This is very precise and doesn't require measuring with a protractor.

Constructing an Angle of 60°

  • Draw a ray OA.
  • With O as center, draw an arc cutting OA at P.
  • With P as center and same radius, draw an arc cutting the first arc at Q.
  • Join OQ. Angle AOQ is 60°.

🎙️ This is a fundamental construction. The equilateral triangle formed by O, P, and Q ensures that the angle at O is exactly 60 degrees. This construction is a building block for many other angles.

1Draw Arc from O
2Draw Arc from P
3Join OQ

Constructing Other Angles (30°, 90°, 120°)

  • 30°: Bisect a 60° angle.
  • 90°: Construct a perpendicular bisector, or combine 60° and bisect 30°.
  • 120°: Extend 60° (or two 60° angles).

🎙️ Once you can construct 60 degrees, you can construct many other angles. Bisecting 60 gives 30. A 90-degree angle can be made by constructing a perpendicular, or by adding a 60-degree angle and bisecting the remaining 30 degrees from a straight line.

What are Tilings (Tessellations)?

  • Covering a surface with shapes without gaps or overlaps.
  • The shapes are called 'tiles'.
  • Also known as tessellations.

🎙️ Have you ever seen a tiled floor or a brick wall? That's a tiling! It's about arranging shapes perfectly so they cover an area completely, leaving no empty spaces and not piling on top of each other.

🧩
Fitting Shapes

Properties of Tilings

  • No gaps: The entire surface is covered.
  • No overlaps: Tiles do not lie on top of each other.
  • Shapes fit together at vertices.

🎙️ For a pattern to be a true tiling, these three rules must be followed. It's like a jigsaw puzzle where every piece fits exactly, and there are no missing pieces or pieces that stick out.

Types of Tilings: Regular Tessellations

  • Uses only one type of regular polygon.
  • All sides and angles of the polygon are equal.
  • Only squares, equilateral triangles, and regular hexagons can form regular tessellations.

🎙️ A regular tessellation is the simplest type. You use just one shape, and that shape is a regular polygon. Surprisingly, only three regular polygons can do this on their own: squares, equilateral triangles, and hexagons.

Why do some shapes tile and others don't?

  • It depends on the angles of the shapes.
  • Angles around a point must add up to exactly 360°.
  • This ensures no gaps or overlaps at vertices.

🎙️ This is the key secret behind tessellations! When tiles meet at a point (a vertex), the sum of the angles of the corners that meet there must be exactly 360 degrees. If it's less, there's a gap; if it's more, there's an overlap.

🔄
Angles Around a Point

Angle Sum at a Vertex

  • For a regular polygon with 'n' sides, each interior angle is (n-2) * 180 / n.
  • For a shape to tile, this angle must divide 360° exactly.
  • E.g., Square: 90°, 360/90 = 4 (4 squares meet).

🎙️ Let's look at the math. An equilateral triangle has 60 degrees per angle, and 360/60 = 6, so six triangles meet. A square has 90 degrees, and 360/90 = 4, so four squares meet. A regular hexagon has 120 degrees, and 360/120 = 3, so three hexagons meet.

Worked Example: Can a Regular Pentagon Tile?

  • Interior angle of a regular pentagon = (5-2)*180/5 = 108°.
  • Can 108° divide 360° exactly? 360/108 = 3.33...
  • No, it cannot. Regular pentagons do not tessellate on their own.

🎙️ This is why not all regular polygons can tessellate. A regular pentagon's angle is 108 degrees. If you try to fit them, you'll either have a gap or an overlap because 108 doesn't perfectly divide 360.

Constructing the Perpendicular Bisector of a Line Segment

  • 1. Draw segment AB.
  • 2. With A & B as centers, radius > half AB, draw arcs above & below AB.
  • 3. Join intersection points C & D. CD is the bisector.

🎙️ A perpendicular bisector cuts a line segment exactly in half and forms a 90-degree angle with it. This construction is super useful for finding the center of a line or creating right angles.

1Draw Segment
2Draw Arcs
3Join Intersections

Constructing the Angle Bisector of a Given Angle

  • 1. Draw angle A.
  • 2. With A as center, draw arc intersecting arms at B & C.
  • 3. With B & C as centers, radius > half BC, draw arcs intersecting at D.
  • 4. Join A to D. AD is the bisector.

🎙️ An angle bisector divides an angle into two equal parts. This is very handy when you need to split an angle precisely, perhaps for design or layout purposes.

1Draw Angle
2Mark Arcs on Arms
3Draw Intersecting Arcs
4Join Vertex to Intersection

Worked Example: Constructing a 90-degree Angle

  • 1. Draw a ray OA.
  • 2. With O as center, draw a semicircle intersecting OA at P.
  • 3. With P as center, draw an arc intersecting semicircle at Q.
  • 4. With Q as center, draw another arc intersecting semicircle at R.
  • 5. With Q and R as centers, draw arcs intersecting at S.
  • 6. Join O to S. Angle AOS is 90°.

🎙️ A 90-degree angle, or a right angle, is fundamental. This construction uses the concept of bisecting the angle formed by 60 and 120 degrees to get 90 degrees. Practice this carefully!

1Draw Ray
2Draw Semicircle
3Mark 60° & 120°
4Bisect Angle
5Draw 90° Line

Constructing a Triangle: SSS (Side-Side-Side)

  • Given the lengths of all three sides (a, b, c).
  • 1. Draw base side (e.g., c).
  • 2. With one endpoint as center, radius 'a', draw arc.
  • 3. With other endpoint as center, radius 'b', draw arc.
  • 4. Join intersection point to endpoints.

🎙️ The SSS criterion means you have all three side lengths. You start by drawing one side, then use your compass to 'swing' arcs from each end with the lengths of the other two sides. Where they meet is your third vertex.

1Draw Base
2Arc from End 1
3Arc from End 2
4Join Vertices

Constructing a Triangle: SAS (Side-Angle-Side)

  • Given two sides and the angle *between* them.
  • 1. Draw one side (e.g., AB).
  • 2. At one endpoint (e.g., A), construct the given angle.
  • 3. Measure the second side along the angle's arm (e.g., AC).
  • 4. Join B to C.

🎙️ For SAS, the angle must be *included* between the two given sides. You draw one side, construct the angle at one end, then mark the length of the second side along the newly drawn arm of the angle.

1Draw Side 1
2Construct Angle
3Mark Side 2
4Join Vertices

Constructing a Triangle: ASA (Angle-Side-Angle)

  • Given two angles and the side *between* them.
  • 1. Draw the given side (e.g., AB).
  • 2. At one endpoint (e.g., A), construct one angle.
  • 3. At the other endpoint (e.g., B), construct the second angle.
  • 4. The intersection of the angle arms is the third vertex C.

🎙️ With ASA, the side must be *included* between the two given angles. You draw the side first, then construct each angle at its respective endpoint. The point where the two angle arms meet forms the third vertex.

1Draw Side
2Construct Angle 1
3Construct Angle 2
4Find Vertex

Constructing a Right-angled Triangle: RHS (Right-Hypotenuse-Side)

  • Given a right angle, the hypotenuse, and one other side.
  • 1. Draw one leg (e.g., BC).
  • 2. At one endpoint (e.g., B), construct a 90-degree angle.
  • 3. With the other endpoint (e.g., C) as center, radius = hypotenuse, draw arc intersecting the 90-degree arm at A.
  • 4. Join A to C.

🎙️ RHS is specifically for right-angled triangles. You start with one leg, construct the right angle, then use the hypotenuse length from the other end of the leg to find the third vertex.

1Draw Leg
2Construct 90°
3Arc for Hypotenuse
4Complete Triangle

Worked Example: Constructing an Equilateral Triangle

  • An equilateral triangle has all sides equal and all angles 60°.
  • 1. Draw a line segment AB (side length).
  • 2. With A as center, radius AB, draw an arc.
  • 3. With B as center, radius AB, draw another arc intersecting the first at C.
  • 4. Join A to C and B to C.

🎙️ Constructing an equilateral triangle is a classic example of using the SSS criterion. Since all sides are equal, you just set your compass to the desired side length and draw arcs from both ends of your base.

2Arc from End 1

What is Tiling (Tessellation)?

  • Covering a flat surface with shapes (tiles) without any gaps or overlaps.
  • The shapes fit together perfectly.
  • Creates a repeating pattern.

🎙️ Tiling, or tessellation, is all about covering a surface completely with shapes, just like laying tiles on a floor. The key is that there are no empty spaces and no tiles overlapping each other.

Regular Tilings: Using One Polygon Type

  • Only regular polygons (all sides and angles equal) can tile a plane on their own.
  • Squares, equilateral triangles, and regular hexagons can tile.
  • Pentagons cannot tile regularly.

🎙️ Not all regular polygons can tile a surface perfectly by themselves. Only squares, equilateral triangles, and regular hexagons have angles that allow them to meet at a point without gaps or overlaps.

Key Property of Tilings: Angles at a Vertex

  • For shapes to tile, the sum of the angles around any common vertex must be exactly 360 degrees.
  • If less, there's a gap; if more, there's an overlap.
  • This is why only certain regular polygons tile.

🎙️ The secret to why certain shapes tile and others don't lies in their interior angles. For a perfect fit, the angles of the tiles meeting at any single point must add up to exactly 360 degrees.

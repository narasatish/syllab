Rectilinear Propagation of Light

  • Light always travels in a straight path.
  • This is called rectilinear propagation.

🎙️ One of the most fundamental properties of light is that it always travels in a straight line. You can observe this phenomenon easily in daily life, like sunbeams passing through a small opening.

Activity: Observing Light's Straight Path

  • Look at a candle flame through a straight pipe.
  • Then, look through a bent pipe.
  • You can only see the flame through the straight pipe.

🎙️ This simple activity demonstrates that light cannot bend around corners. When the pipe is straight, light from the candle reaches your eye directly. But a bent pipe blocks the straight path of light.

Shadows: Proof of Straight Path

  • Formed when an opaque object blocks light.
  • Light cannot bend around the object.
  • The shape of the shadow matches the object.

🎙️ Shadows are a perfect everyday example of light traveling in a straight line. When an object that light cannot pass through comes in the way, it creates a dark area where light is blocked, forming a shadow.

Objects and Light: Transparent, Translucent, Opaque

  • Transparent: Light passes through completely (glass).
  • Translucent: Light passes partially (frosted glass).
  • Opaque: Light does not pass through (wall).

🎙️ How much light an object allows to pass through determines its classification. This property is crucial in many applications, from windows to sunglasses, controlling how much light enters a space or reaches our eyes.

Reflection of Light: Bouncing Back

  • Like a ball bouncing off a wall.
  • Mirrors are excellent reflectors.

🎙️ Reflection is a common phenomenon where light rays hit a surface and change direction, essentially bouncing off it. This is how we see our own reflection in a mirror, or how light illuminates a room after hitting the walls.

Types of Reflection: Regular and Diffused

  • Regular reflection: From smooth surfaces (mirror), clear image.
  • Diffused reflection: From rough surfaces (wall), scattered light.

🎙️ The type of surface determines how light reflects. A perfectly smooth surface like a mirror causes regular reflection, producing a clear image, whereas a rough surface scatters light in many directions, leading to diffused reflection.

How We See Objects: The Role of Reflection

  • Light from a source hits an object.
  • The object reflects light into our eyes.
  • Our brain processes this light, allowing us to see.

🎙️ We don't actually see objects directly, but rather the light they reflect. Light from the sun or a bulb illuminates an object, and that reflected light then travels to our eyes, allowing us to perceive its shape and color.

Mirrors: Introducing Plane Mirrors

  • A flat, smooth, highly polished surface.
  • Used daily for seeing our reflection.
  • Forms virtual, erect, and same-sized images.

🎙️ Plane mirrors are the most common type of mirror, having a perfectly flat reflective surface. They are essential in our homes for grooming and also have many scientific applications due to their predictable image formation properties.

Image Formation in a Plane Mirror

  • Image is virtual (cannot be caught on screen).
  • Image is erect (upright).
  • Same size as the object.
  • Laterally inverted (left appears right).
  • Same distance behind as object is in front.

🎙️ When you look into a plane mirror, the image you see has specific characteristics. It appears to be behind the mirror, is the same height as you, and shows your left and right sides swapped, a phenomenon called lateral inversion.

Periscope: Seeing Around Corners

  • Uses two plane mirrors.
  • Allows viewing objects not in direct line of sight.
  • Used in submarines and military tanks.

🎙️ The periscope is a clever device that uses the principle of reflection from plane mirrors to see over obstacles or around corners. This technology is vital in submarines, enabling them to see above the water's surface while remaining submerged.

Beyond Plane: Spherical Mirrors

  • Mirrors with curved reflective surfaces.
  • Part of a hollow sphere.
  • Two main types: Concave and Convex.

🎙️ Unlike flat plane mirrors, spherical mirrors have a curved surface, either bulging outwards or curving inwards. These curves give them unique properties for focusing or spreading light, making them useful in many different applications.

🌐
Curved Mirrors

Concave Mirror: Converging Light

  • Reflecting surface curves inwards.
  • Converges (gathers) parallel light rays.
  • Used by dentists, in torches, and car headlights.

🎙️ Imagine the inside of a spoon – that's like a concave mirror. It collects incoming light rays and focuses them to a point, which is why it's used in applications where a strong, focused beam of light is needed, like in flashlights.

🥄
Inward Curve
16 / 25

Convex Mirror: Diverging Light

  • Reflecting surface curves outwards.
  • Diverges (spreads out) parallel light rays.
  • Used as rear-view mirrors in vehicles, shop security mirrors.

🎙️ Now imagine the outside of a spoon – that's a convex mirror. It spreads out light rays, giving a wider field of view, though the images are smaller. This is why they are perfect for rear-view mirrors in cars, allowing drivers to see more of the road behind them.

🚗

Dispersion of Light: The Rainbow Effect

  • White light is made of seven different colours (VIBGYOR).
  • A prism can split white light into its constituent colours.
  • This splitting is called dispersion.

🎙️ Sir Isaac Newton famously showed that white light, which appears uniform, is actually a mixture of many colours. When passed through a prism, these colours separate, revealing the beautiful spectrum we know as VIBGYOR.

Rainbows: Nature's Own Spectrum

  • Formed by dispersion of sunlight by tiny water droplets.
  • Acts like millions of tiny prisms.
  • Always appears opposite to the sun.

🎙️ Rainbows are a spectacular natural demonstration of light dispersion. After rainfall, sunlight passes through water droplets suspended in the air, each droplet acting like a tiny prism, splitting the white light into its vibrant colours.

What is Light?

  • Light is a form of energy.
  • It helps us see objects around us.
  • Without light, everything would be dark.

🎙️ Think of light as a special kind of energy, just like heat or sound. But unlike heat or sound, light allows us to perceive the world visually. It's the reason we can distinguish colours and shapes.

Sources of Light: Luminous and Non-Luminous

  • Luminous objects produce their own light (e.g., Sun, bulb).
  • Non-luminous objects reflect light from other sources (e.g., Moon, book).
  • Our eyes see non-luminous objects when light bounces off them.

🎙️ The Sun is a natural luminous source, while a torch is an artificial one. The Moon, even though it shines brightly at night, is non-luminous because it only reflects sunlight. We see everything else because light from a source reflects off them and enters our eyes.

How Does Light Travel? Rectilinear Propagation

  • Light always travels in a straight line.
  • This property is called rectilinear propagation.
  • Think of a laser beam or sun rays through a window.

🎙️ Imagine looking at a candle flame through a straight pipe; you can see it. But if you bend the pipe, you can't! This simple experiment proves that light travels in straight lines. This principle is fundamental to understanding how shadows form and how mirrors work.

Reflection of Light: Bouncing Back!

  • When light hits a smooth surface, it bounces back.
  • This phenomenon is called reflection.
  • Think of a ball bouncing off a wall.

🎙️ Just like a rubber ball bounces back after hitting a wall, light also bounces back when it strikes a surface. This 'bouncing back' is what we call reflection, and it's how we see our own image in a mirror.

Laws of Reflection

  • The angle of incidence is equal to the angle of reflection.
  • The incident ray, the reflected ray, and the normal all lie in the same plane.
  • These laws apply to all types of reflecting surfaces.

🎙️ These two laws are crucial for understanding how light behaves when it reflects. The 'normal' is an imaginary line perpendicular to the surface at the point where the light strikes. These laws help scientists and engineers design optical instruments.

Types of Reflection: Regular vs. Diffused

  • Regular reflection: From smooth surfaces (e.g., mirror), forms clear images.
  • Diffused reflection: From rough surfaces (e.g., wall), scatters light, no clear image.
  • Most objects we see reflect light diffusely.

🎙️ Imagine looking at your reflection in a calm pond versus a choppy one. A calm pond shows a clear image (regular reflection), while a choppy one just scatters the light (diffused reflection). This difference explains why you can see your reflection in a mirror but not in a brick wall.

Plane Mirrors: Our Everyday Reflectors

🎙️ Plane mirrors are the most common type of mirror we encounter daily. From checking your appearance before school to seeing behind you in certain vehicles, plane mirrors play a big role. Let's see what kind of images they form.

Image Formation by Plane Mirror

  • Image is virtual (cannot be caught on screen).
  • Image is erect (upright, not upside down).
  • Image is laterally inverted (left appears right).
  • Image is same size as object and same distance behind mirror.

🎙️ When you stand in front of a plane mirror, you see an image that is virtual because it's formed by light rays that appear to diverge from a point, not actually meet. The most interesting part is lateral inversion, where your left hand appears as the right hand of your image!

Spherical Mirrors: Curved Reflectors

  • Mirrors with curved reflecting surfaces are called spherical mirrors.
  • They are part of a hollow sphere.
  • Two main types: Concave and Convex.

🎙️ Imagine cutting a piece out of a shiny, hollow ball. If the inner surface is reflecting, it's a concave mirror. If the outer surface is reflecting, it's a convex mirror. These mirrors have very different properties compared to plane mirrors.

Concave Mirrors: Converging Light

  • A concave mirror curves inwards, like the inside of a spoon.
  • It converges (brings together) parallel rays of light.
  • Can form both real and virtual, inverted or erect images, depending on object position.

🎙️ Concave mirrors are fascinating because they can make parallel light rays meet at a point called the focus. This property makes them useful for concentrating light. They can also produce magnified images, which is quite handy!

Uses of Concave Mirrors

  • Dentists use them to see magnified images of teeth.
  • Used in torch reflectors and car headlights to get powerful parallel beams.
  • Solar furnaces use them to concentrate sunlight.

🎙️ Think about how a dentist uses a small mirror to examine your teeth – that's a concave mirror providing a magnified view. Similarly, the reflector in a torch is concave, gathering light from the bulb and sending it out in a strong, focused beam.

Convex Mirrors: Diverging Light

  • A convex mirror curves outwards, like the back of a spoon.
  • It diverges (spreads out) parallel rays of light.
  • Always forms virtual, erect, and diminished (smaller) images.

🎙️ Unlike concave mirrors, convex mirrors spread out light rays. This means they always form images that are smaller than the actual object. This 'shrinking' effect allows them to cover a wider field of view.

Uses of Convex Mirrors

  • Used as rear-view mirrors in vehicles to see a wider area.
  • Used in shops as security mirrors to monitor large areas.
  • Found at sharp turns on roads to help drivers see oncoming traffic.

🎙️ The wider field of view provided by convex mirrors makes them perfect for vehicle rear-view mirrors. Although the image is smaller, you can see much more of the road behind you, enhancing safety. They are also great for security in stores.

Primary Colours of Light

  • The primary colours of light are Red, Green, and Blue (RGB).
  • These colours cannot be formed by mixing other colours of light.
  • Mixing all three primary colours of light in the right proportion produces white light.

🎙️ These three colours are the building blocks for all other colours of light. Think about your TV or phone screen; they use tiny red, green, and blue lights to create millions of different colours you see. When all three are projected together, they create white light!

Mixing Colours: Secondary Colours

  • Mixing two primary colours of light creates a secondary colour.
  • Red + Green = Yellow
  • Green + Blue = Cyan
  • Blue + Red = Magenta

🎙️ It's important to differentiate between mixing light colours and mixing paint colours. When you mix primary colours of light, you get brighter secondary colours. This is an additive process, adding more light makes it whiter.

Why Objects Appear Coloured

  • An object's colour depends on the light it reflects.
  • A red apple appears red because it reflects red light and absorbs all other colours.
  • A white object reflects all colours; a black object absorbs all colours.

🎙️ The colour you see an object as is not inherent to the object itself, but rather how it interacts with light. If you shine only green light on a red apple, it will appear black because there's no red light for it to reflect!

How the Eye Works: A Simple Journey of Light

  • Light enters through the Cornea and Pupil.
  • The Iris controls the size of the Pupil.
  • The Lens focuses light onto the Retina.
  • The Retina converts light into electrical signals, sent to the brain.

🎙️ It's like a tiny camera inside your head! The cornea and lens work together to focus the light, much like a camera lens. The retina acts like the film or sensor, capturing the image, and then your optic nerve sends that information to your brain for processing.

Care of Eyes: Keep Them Healthy!

  • Read in proper light, not too dim or too bright.
  • Avoid rubbing your eyes; wash them with clean water.
  • Do not look directly at the Sun or bright lights.
  • Get regular eye check-ups.

🎙️ Our eyes are precious, so it's vital to take good care of them. Simple habits like reading in good light and protecting them from direct sunlight can prevent strain and damage. Remember, healthy eyes help you see the world clearly!

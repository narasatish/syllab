What are Acids?

  • Substances that taste sour.
  • Derived from the Latin word 'acere' meaning sour.
  • Can be strong or weak.

🎙️ Acids are chemical substances that we often associate with a sour taste. Think of a lemon or an orange. This sourness is a key characteristic of acids. They can be found naturally or produced in labs.

Common Acids Around Us

  • Acetic acid: Found in vinegar.
  • Citric acid: Found in citrus fruits (lemon, orange).
  • Lactic acid: Found in curd (yogurt).
  • Ascorbic acid: Found in Amla, citrus fruits (Vitamin C).

🎙️ Many foods we eat contain natural acids. Vinegar used in cooking has acetic acid, and the refreshing taste of lemons comes from citric acid. Even curd, or yogurt, contains lactic acid.

Properties of Acids: Corrosive Nature

  • Strong acids can burn skin.
  • They can damage materials like clothes.
  • Always handle acids with care.

🎙️ While some acids are safe to eat, strong acids can be very dangerous. They are corrosive, meaning they can eat away at substances, including our skin. That's why safety precautions are crucial when working with them.

What are Bases?

  • Substances that taste bitter.
  • Feel soapy or slippery to touch.
  • Opposite of acids.

🎙️ Bases are another important group of chemical substances. Unlike acids, they typically taste bitter and feel slippery when you touch them, just like soap. They are, in many ways, the chemical opposite of acids.

Common Bases Around Us

  • Sodium hydroxide: Found in soap.
  • Calcium hydroxide: Found in lime water.
  • Magnesium hydroxide: Found in milk of magnesia (antacid).
  • Ammonium hydroxide: Found in window cleaner.

🎙️ Bases are also common in our homes. Soap contains sodium hydroxide, which gives it that characteristic slippery feel. Milk of magnesia, used for indigestion, contains magnesium hydroxide, a mild base.

How to Tell Them Apart? (Indicators)

  • We cannot taste unknown substances.
  • Indicators help us identify acids and bases.
  • They change colour in acidic or basic solutions.

🎙️ Since tasting unknown chemicals can be dangerous, we need a safe way to distinguish between acids and bases. This is where indicators come in handy. They are special substances that change color depending on whether they are in an acidic or basic solution.

🎙️ Think of indicators as detectives that reveal the nature of a solution. They have a specific color in acidic conditions and a different color in basic conditions. This color change is our clue!

Natural Indicator: Litmus

  • Most common natural indicator.
  • Extracted from lichens.
  • Available as red and blue litmus paper.

🎙️ Litmus is probably the most famous natural indicator. It comes from small plants called lichens and is often used in the form of paper strips. We have both red and blue litmus paper to test substances.

🧪
Litmus Paper
12 / 25

Activity: Using Litmus Paper

  • Blue litmus turns red in acid.
  • Red litmus turns blue in base.
  • No change in neutral solutions.

🎙️ Here's how litmus paper works: if you dip blue litmus paper into an acidic solution, it will turn red. Conversely, if you dip red litmus paper into a basic solution, it will turn blue. If there's no change, the solution is neutral.

1Dip blue litmus into solution
2If it turns red, it's an acid
3Dip red litmus into solution
4If it turns blue, it's a base

Neutralisation - The Balancing Act

  • When an acid reacts with a base.
  • They cancel out each other's effects.
  • Results in a neutral solution.

🎙️ Neutralisation is a very important chemical reaction. It happens when an acid and a base mix together. They essentially 'balance' each other out, reducing their individual acidic or basic properties.

The Neutralisation Reaction: Salt & Water

  • Acid + Base → Salt + Water + Heat
  • A new substance, salt, is formed.
  • Heat is usually produced in this reaction.

🎙️ When an acid reacts with a base, the products are always a salt and water. This reaction also releases energy in the form of heat. This is a fundamental chemical equation to remember.

Real-Life Neutralisation: Indigestion

  • Our stomach produces hydrochloric acid.
  • Too much acid causes indigestion.
  • Antacids (bases) neutralise excess acid.

🎙️ One common example of neutralisation in our daily lives is when we take antacids for indigestion. Our stomach naturally produces hydrochloric acid, but sometimes it produces too much, causing discomfort. Antacids are mild bases that neutralise this excess acid, providing relief.

Real-Life Neutralisation: Ant Sting

  • Ants inject formic acid when they bite.
  • Causes pain and irritation.
  • Rubbing baking soda (base) neutralises it.

🎙️ When an ant stings you, it injects a substance called formic acid, which causes the burning sensation. To soothe it, you can apply a mild base like baking soda solution, which neutralises the acid and reduces the pain.

Real-Life Neutralisation: Soil Treatment

  • Plants need specific soil pH.
  • Acidic soil treated with quicklime (base).
  • Basic soil treated with organic matter (acidic).

🎙️ Farmers use neutralisation to ensure healthy soil for crops. If the soil is too acidic, they add bases like quicklime. If it's too basic, they add organic matter, which releases acidic substances, bringing the soil back to a neutral pH.

What are Salts?

  • Formed during neutralisation reactions.
  • Can be acidic, basic, or neutral.
  • Table salt (sodium chloride) is a common example.

🎙️ Salts are the new compounds formed when acids and bases react. They are not always what we think of as 'table salt'; there are many different types of salts, each with unique properties. They are essential in many industries and in our own bodies.

Recap: Acids, Bases, and Salts

What Are Acids? A Closer Look

  • Acids are chemical substances that taste sour.
  • They turn blue litmus paper red.
  • Found naturally in fruits or made in labs.

🎙️ Chemically speaking, acids release hydrogen ions when dissolved in water. We find natural acids in many food items like citric acid in oranges, or acetic acid in vinegar. There are also strong acids like hydrochloric acid, used in labs.

Examples of Acids Around Us

  • Citric acid: Lemons, oranges
  • Lactic acid: Curd, yogurt
  • Acetic acid: Vinegar (used in pickles)
  • Hydrochloric acid: Stomach acid (helps digestion)

🎙️ From the kitchen to our own bodies, acids play various roles. Citric acid gives citrus fruits their tang, lactic acid makes milk curdle, and acetic acid is a common preservative. Our stomach even produces hydrochloric acid to break down food.

What Are Bases? A Deeper Dive

  • Bases are chemical substances that taste bitter.
  • They feel soapy or slippery to touch.
  • Bases turn red litmus paper blue.

🎙️ Chemically, bases release hydroxide ions when dissolved in water. Common examples include sodium hydroxide, found in soaps, and calcium hydroxide, used in whitewashing. They are also known as alkalis if they are soluble in water.

  • Calcium hydroxide: Limewater, whitewash
  • Magnesium hydroxide: Milk of Magnesia (antacid)
  • Ammonium hydroxide: Window cleaner

🎙️ Bases are essential for cleaning and even for medicines. Sodium hydroxide is a strong base used in making soap. Milk of Magnesia, which contains magnesium hydroxide, is a mild base used to relieve indigestion.

Neutral Substances: Neither Acid Nor Base

  • Some substances are neither acidic nor basic.
  • They don't have a sour or bitter taste.
  • They don't change the color of litmus paper.

🎙️ Not everything is an acid or a base! Pure water, sugar solution, and common salt solution are examples of neutral substances. They don't react like acids or bases and are very important in many applications.

How to Tell Them Apart? Introducing Indicators!

  • We cannot taste or touch unknown substances.
  • Indicators are special substances used to test if a solution is acidic or basic.
  • They change color when mixed with an acid or a base.

🎙️ Since tasting or touching unknown chemicals can be dangerous, scientists use indicators. These are like chemical detectives that show us the nature of a substance by simply changing their color. It's a safe and effective way to identify acids and bases.

Neutralisation: The Balancing Act

  • When an acid and a base react, they neutralise each other.
  • This reaction forms a salt and water.
  • It's like they cancel out each other's strong properties.

🎙️ Neutralisation is a very important chemical reaction. It's where an acid and a base come together and lose their individual acidic and basic properties. The result is something new: a salt and water, which are typically neutral.

⚖️
Balancing Act
22 / 30

The Neutralisation Reaction Explained

  • Acid + Base → Salt + Water (+ Heat)
  • Example: Hydrochloric acid + Sodium hydroxide → Sodium chloride + Water
  • Heat is usually produced during this reaction.

🎙️ This is the fundamental equation for neutralisation. Sodium chloride is common table salt! The release of heat indicates that this reaction is exothermic. This principle is used in many real-world applications.

Real-Life Neutralisation: Soil Treatment

  • Plants need a specific pH range to grow well.
  • If soil is too acidic, farmers add quicklime (calcium oxide) or slaked lime (calcium hydroxide).
  • If soil is too basic, organic matter (which releases acids) is added.

🎙️ For healthy crops, the soil needs to be just right – not too acidic, not too basic. Farmers use neutralisation to balance soil pH. Adding lime to acidic soil or organic matter to basic soil helps ensure plants get the best growing conditions.

🎙️ Industrial waste can be very harmful if not treated properly. Factories use neutralisation reactions to treat their acidic or basic waste materials before releasing them into rivers or drains. This is crucial for protecting our environment and aquatic ecosystems.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Don't taste or touch unknown chemicals to identify them.
  • Confusing litmus paper changes: Blue to Red (Acid), Red to Blue (Base).
  • Forgetting that neutralisation also produces heat.

🎙️ It's easy to make small errors, especially when learning new concepts. Always remember safety first – never taste or touch chemicals. Practice the litmus paper changes, perhaps with a mnemonic, and don't forget the heat produced in neutralisation.

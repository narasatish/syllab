What are Physical Changes?

  • Changes where only the physical properties of a substance are altered.
  • These properties include shape, size, colour, and state.
  • NO new substance is formed during a physical change.

🎙️ Think of a physical change as giving something a 'makeover' without changing what it fundamentally is. The substance itself doesn't become a different substance; it just looks or feels different.

🧊
State Change
7 / 30

Characteristics of Physical Changes

  • Generally reversible: The original substance can often be recovered.
  • No new substance formed: The chemical composition remains the same.
  • Temporary: The change can usually be undone.

🎙️ The key things to remember about physical changes are that they don't create anything new, and you can usually go back to the original form. They are like putting on a different outfit – you're still the same person underneath!

Example: Melting of Ice

  • Ice (solid water) changes to water (liquid water).
  • Only the state of water changes, not its chemical composition.
  • You can freeze the water back into ice.
  • It's a reversible physical change.

🎙️ When ice melts, it's still H2O. It just moves from a solid, rigid structure to a liquid, flowing form. This is a classic example of a physical change because no new substance is created.

Example: Tearing of Paper

  • When you tear a sheet of paper, its size changes.
  • The small pieces are still paper, chemically.
  • No new substance is formed.
  • This is an irreversible physical change (you can't perfectly re-join it).

🎙️ Even though you can't put the paper back together perfectly, it's still paper. The chemical nature of the substance hasn't changed. It's an example of a physical change that is difficult to reverse practically.

What are Chemical Changes?

  • Changes where new substances with different properties are formed.
  • Also known as chemical reactions.
  • The chemical composition of the original substance is altered.

🎙️ In a chemical change, you start with one or more substances, and you end up with completely different substances. It's like taking ingredients and baking a cake – you can't easily turn the cake back into flour, eggs, and sugar.

🧪
New Substance
14 / 30

Characteristics of Chemical Changes

  • Generally irreversible: The original substance cannot be easily recovered.
  • New substance(s) formed: The chemical composition changes.
  • Permanent: The change is lasting.
  • Often accompanied by energy changes (heat, light, sound).

🎙️ Chemical changes are much more fundamental. They rearrange atoms and molecules to form new ones. This is why they are often accompanied by noticeable signs like heat, light, or gas production.

⚛️
Molecular Change

Indicators of Chemical Changes

  • Heat, light, or any other radiation (e.g., sound) may be given off or absorbed.
  • A new smell may be produced.
  • A colour change may take place.
  • A gas may be formed.
  • A precipitate (solid) may be formed.

🎙️ These are the tell-tale signs that a chemical change is happening. If you observe any of these, it's a strong indication that new substances are being created. Think about a firecracker – it shows many of these!

Example: Burning of Magnesium Ribbon

  • When a magnesium ribbon burns, it produces dazzling white light and heat.
  • It forms a white powder called magnesium oxide.
  • Magnesium oxide is a completely new substance.
  • This is an irreversible chemical change.

🎙️ This is a classic experiment. The bright light and heat are clear indicators. The white ash formed is entirely different from the shiny magnesium ribbon you started with, confirming it's a chemical change.

Differentiating Physical and Chemical Changes

  • Physical changes alter form, not composition.
  • Chemical changes alter composition, creating new substances.
  • Physical changes are often reversible; chemical changes are not.
  • Indicators help identify chemical changes.

🎙️ This is the core distinction. If you remember 'no new substance' for physical and 'new substance formed' for chemical, you'll be able to classify most changes correctly. The indicators are your clues for chemical changes.

Special Case: Rusting of Iron

  • When iron objects are left exposed to air and moisture, they acquire a reddish-brown layer.
  • This layer is called rust.
  • Rust is iron oxide, a new substance, different from iron.
  • Therefore, rusting is a chemical change.

🎙️ Rusting is a very common and important chemical change. It causes damage to iron structures. It's a slow process, but it clearly forms a new substance, iron oxide, which has different properties than iron.

Conditions Necessary for Rusting

  • For rusting to occur, both oxygen (from air) and water (or water vapour) are essential.
  • If either is absent, rusting does not happen.
  • This is why iron objects rust faster in humid conditions.

🎙️ It's a chemical reaction involving iron, oxygen, and water. Understanding these conditions helps us prevent rusting. Think about why a car rusts faster near the sea.

Preventing Rusting

  • Applying a coat of paint or grease.
  • Applying a layer of zinc on iron (galvanisation).
  • Chromium plating.
  • Making alloys like stainless steel (iron mixed with carbon, chromium, nickel).

🎙️ Since rusting is a problem, we have various methods to prevent it. All these methods aim to prevent contact between iron, oxygen, and water, thereby stopping the chemical reaction.

Special Case: Crystallisation

  • The process of obtaining pure solid crystals of a substance from its solution.
  • For example, obtaining pure copper sulphate crystals.
  • This is a physical change.

🎙️ Crystallisation is often confused with chemical changes because it looks like a new form is appearing. However, it's merely the purification of an existing substance into a more ordered solid structure, making it a physical change.

💎
Pure Crystals
25 / 30

Process of Crystallisation (Copper Sulphate)

  • Dissolve copper sulphate powder in minimum water.
  • Heat the solution gently and filter it.
  • Evaporate the water until a saturated solution is obtained.
  • Cool the solution slowly without disturbance.

🎙️ The key here is that we are not creating copper sulphate; we are just separating pure copper sulphate from impurities and allowing its molecules to arrange into crystals. The chemical identity remains copper sulphate.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Confusing state changes (melting, boiling) with chemical changes.
  • Assuming all irreversible changes are chemical (e.g., tearing paper).
  • Not looking for indicators like gas, heat, light, or new smell.
  • Thinking physical changes cannot be permanent (e.g., broken glass).

🎙️ It's easy to get confused, especially with concepts like tearing paper or melting wax. Always ask yourself: 'Is a NEW substance formed?' If the answer is no, it's a physical change, even if it's hard to reverse.

Recap: What We've Learned

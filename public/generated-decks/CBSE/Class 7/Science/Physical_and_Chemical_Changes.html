What Does 'Change' Mean?

  • A process where something becomes different.
  • It can affect size, shape, state, or composition.
  • Some changes are easy to reverse, others are not.

🎙️ At its core, a change is simply when something alters from its original state. Think of it like putting on different clothes – you are still you, but your appearance changes. We'll explore how deep these changes go.

Understanding Physical Changes

  • Changes where only physical properties are altered.
  • No new substance is formed.
  • Often temporary and reversible.

🎙️ Let's begin with physical changes. Imagine you're playing with clay. You can squish it, flatten it, make different shapes. But it's still clay, right? That's the essence of a physical change.

↔️
Form Changes

Key Traits of Physical Changes

  • Original substance remains the same.
  • Change in shape, size, colour, or state.
  • Usually reversible.
  • No new chemical bonds are broken or formed.

🎙️ The most important thing to remember about physical changes is that the core identity of the material doesn't change. It's like changing clothes – you're still the same person underneath. You can often go back to the original state.

Reversible

Physical Change in Action: Melting Ice

  • Ice (solid water) changes to water (liquid water).
  • The chemical composition (H₂O) remains the same.
  • It can be reversed by freezing the water.

🎙️ Think about an ice cube melting in your hand. It changes from a solid to a liquid, but it's still water. If you put that water back in the freezer, it turns back into ice. This is a perfect example of a reversible physical change.

Another Physical Change: Tearing Paper

  • A large sheet of paper becomes smaller pieces.
  • The paper's chemical composition doesn't change.
  • It's still paper, just in a different form.

🎙️ When you tear a piece of paper, you're changing its size and shape. You haven't created a new substance; it's still paper. You can't put it back together perfectly, but the material itself is unaltered, making it a physical change.

Activity: Dissolving Salt in Water

  • Mix salt with water.
  • Salt disappears, forming saltwater.
  • You can get salt back by evaporating the water.

🎙️ Let's try an activity. Take some salt and dissolve it in water. The salt seems to vanish, but it's still there, just mixed with water. If you heat the saltwater, the water evaporates, and you'll find the salt crystals left behind. This shows it's a physical change.

Quick Recap: Physical Changes

  • Changes only in physical properties.
  • No new substances are formed.
  • Mostly reversible.
  • Examples: melting, freezing, boiling, tearing, dissolving.

🎙️ So, to summarise physical changes: think of them as surface-level alterations. The material's identity remains intact, and you can often reverse the process to get the original substance back.

Understanding Chemical Changes

  • Changes where new substances are formed.
  • The chemical composition of the material changes.
  • Often permanent and irreversible.

🎙️ Now, let's move to chemical changes. These are more profound. Imagine baking a cake. You mix flour, sugar, eggs, and when you bake it, you get a completely new product – a cake! You can't easily get the raw ingredients back from the cake.

⚛️
New Substance

Key Traits of Chemical Changes

  • One or more new substances are formed.
  • Original substance loses its identity.
  • Usually irreversible.
  • Chemical bonds are broken and formed.

🎙️ The defining feature of a chemical change is the creation of something new. The original material transforms into a different material with different properties. These changes are typically much harder, or impossible, to reverse.

🔥
Irreversible

Chemical Change in Action: Burning Paper

  • Paper burns and turns into ash and smoke.
  • Ash and smoke are completely new substances.
  • You cannot turn ash back into paper.

🎙️ When you burn paper, it doesn't just change shape; it completely transforms. The paper turns into ash, smoke, and releases gases. Ash is a totally different substance from paper, and you can't put it back together to make paper again. This is a clear chemical change.

Another Chemical Change: Rusting of Iron

  • Iron reacts with oxygen and water to form rust.
  • Rust is a new substance (iron oxide).
  • Rust weakens the iron and is irreversible.

🎙️ Have you seen old iron gates or tools covered in a reddish-brown layer? That's rust. Iron reacting with air and moisture creates this new substance called iron oxide. Rust is very different from iron and cannot be easily converted back, making it a chemical change.

How to Spot a Chemical Change

  • Production of heat, light, or sound.
  • Formation of a new gas (bubbles).
  • Change in colour.
  • Formation of a precipitate (solid).

🎙️ Chemical changes often give us clues that they are happening. If you see light being produced, feel heat, hear a fizzing sound, notice bubbles forming, or see a colour change, it's a good indication that a chemical change is taking place.

Physical vs. Chemical: Key Differences

  • Physical: No new substance, reversible.
  • Chemical: New substance formed, irreversible.
  • Physical: Changes shape/state.
  • Chemical: Changes composition.

🎙️ This slide summarises the core distinction. Think of physical changes as surface-level transformations, like changing clothes, while chemical changes are deep transformations, like cooking a meal from raw ingredients. The fundamental nature of the material is different.

Preventing Undesirable Changes: Rusting

  • Rusting is a chemical change that damages iron.
  • We can prevent it by coating iron with paint or grease.
  • Galvanisation (coating with zinc) is another method.

🎙️ Since rusting is a harmful chemical change, we often try to prevent it. By painting iron, applying grease, or galvanising it with zinc, we create a barrier that stops the iron from coming into contact with oxygen and water, thus preventing rust formation.

Lesson Recap: Physical and Chemical Changes

  • Physical Changes: No new substance, reversible (e.g., melting ice).
  • Chemical Changes: New substance formed, irreversible (e.g., burning paper).
  • Look for signs like heat, light, gas, colour change for chemical changes.

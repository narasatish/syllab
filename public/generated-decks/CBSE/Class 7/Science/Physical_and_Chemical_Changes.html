Introducing Physical Changes

  • Only physical properties change
  • No new substance is formed
  • Often reversible

🎙️ A physical change is like giving something a new look without actually changing what it's made of. Imagine tearing a piece of paper – it's still paper, just in smaller pieces. The chemical identity remains the same.

Characteristics of Physical Changes

  • Shape, size, state may change
  • Composition remains the same
  • Generally temporary and reversible

🎙️ In a physical change, you might see a difference in how something looks or feels, but its core material doesn't change. For example, when ice melts into water, it changes state, but it's still H2O. You can freeze it back into ice.

Example: Melting of Ice

  • Ice (solid water) changes to water (liquid)
  • Composition (H2O) remains the same
  • Can be reversed by freezing

🎙️ When you take an ice cube out of the freezer, it slowly turns into water. This is a classic physical change. The water molecules themselves don't change; they just move further apart as the state changes from solid to liquid.

Example: Dissolving Salt in Water

  • Salt seems to disappear in water
  • Salt and water are still present
  • Can get salt back by evaporation

🎙️ When you add salt to water and stir, it dissolves. The salt particles spread throughout the water, but they don't chemically react. You can recover the salt by evaporating the water, proving it was a physical change.

Introducing Chemical Changes

  • A new substance is formed
  • Chemical properties change
  • Often irreversible

🎙️ In a chemical change, things get really exciting! We don't just change the look; we create something entirely new, with different properties. This is like baking a cake – you can't turn it back into flour and eggs.

Characteristics of Chemical Changes

  • Formation of one or more new substances
  • Original substance loses its identity
  • Generally permanent and irreversible

🎙️ Chemical changes are often accompanied by other observations, like heat being given off or taken in, light being produced, or a change in colour. Once a chemical change happens, it's usually very difficult or impossible to reverse.

Signs of Chemical Changes

  • Heat or light produced/absorbed
  • Sound produced
  • Change in colour or smell
  • Formation of a gas or precipitate

🎙️ How do you know if a chemical change has occurred? Look for these clues! If you see bubbles (gas), feel heat, notice a colour change, or hear a hiss, it's a strong indication of a chemical reaction taking place.

Example: Rusting of Iron

  • Iron reacts with oxygen and water
  • Forms a new substance: iron oxide (rust)
  • Rust is brittle and reddish-brown

🎙️ You've probably seen a rusty nail or gate. Rusting is a slow chemical process where iron reacts with oxygen in the presence of moisture to form iron oxide. The original iron is gone, replaced by a new, weaker substance called rust.

Preventing Rusting of Iron

  • Rusting is a chemical change
  • Protection from oxygen and water
  • Methods: painting, oiling, galvanisation

🎙️ Since rusting is a chemical change that damages iron, we often try to prevent it. By applying a layer of paint, grease, or zinc (galvanisation), we create a barrier that prevents iron from coming into contact with oxygen and moisture.

Crystallisation: A Physical Change

  • Obtaining pure solid from a solution
  • Example: getting salt from salty water
  • No new substance formed, just a purer form

🎙️ Crystallisation is a common process used to purify substances. When you let a saturated solution cool down slowly, the dissolved substance forms crystals. This is a physical change because the chemical identity of the substance remains the same, only its state or purity changes.

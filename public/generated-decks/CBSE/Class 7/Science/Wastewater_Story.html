What is Wastewater?

  • Any water that has been used and is no longer fresh.
  • Also known as 'dirty water' or 'sewage'.
  • Contains dissolved and suspended impurities.

🎙️ Wastewater is simply any water that has been used by us and contains impurities. Think of the water that goes down the drain after washing dishes or taking a bath. This used water is no longer clean and needs to be treated.

Where Does Wastewater Come From?

  • Homes (domestic sewage)
  • Industries (industrial effluent)
  • Hospitals and other public buildings

🎙️ Wastewater originates from various places. Our homes are a major source, but factories, hospitals, and even schools also contribute to the wastewater stream. It's a collective output from human activities.

Sources: Domestic Wastewater (Sewage)

  • From kitchens: food particles, oil, detergents.
  • From bathrooms: human waste, soap, hair.
  • From laundries: detergents, dirt from clothes.

🎙️ Domestic wastewater, commonly called sewage, is what flows out of our homes. Every time you wash dishes, flush the toilet, or take a shower, you are contributing to domestic wastewater. It's a mixture of various household wastes.

Sources: Industrial Wastewater

  • Chemicals, heavy metals, toxic substances.
  • High temperatures, oils, greases.
  • Requires specialized treatment before discharge.

🎙️ Industries produce a different type of wastewater, often containing harmful chemicals and heavy metals. For example, a textile factory might discharge water with dyes, while a metal factory could release water with dissolved metals. This industrial wastewater is usually more complex and dangerous than domestic sewage.

Factory Effluent

Sources: Agricultural Runoff & Stormwater

  • Fertilizers and pesticides from farms.
  • Rainwater flowing over roads and land.
  • Carries oil, dirt, and chemicals into water bodies.

🎙️ Even agriculture contributes to wastewater pollution. Rainwater flowing over farms picks up fertilizers and pesticides, carrying them into rivers and lakes. Similarly, stormwater runoff from urban areas can pick up oil, dirt, and litter from roads, polluting water sources.

🚜

Components of Wastewater: Organic Impurities

  • Human and animal faeces (excreta).
  • Urea, oil, vegetable and fruit waste.
  • Food remains and pesticides.

🎙️ Wastewater contains many different things. Organic impurities come from living or once-living matter. This includes human and animal waste, leftover food, and even pesticides used on crops. These substances can decompose and consume oxygen in water.

🍎🍎🍎🍎
Food Waste

Components of Wastewater: Inorganic Impurities & Nutrients

  • Nitrates, phosphates (from detergents/fertilizers).
  • Metals (lead, mercury) from industrial waste.
  • Harmful to aquatic life and human health.

🎙️ Inorganic impurities do not come from living matter. These include salts, metals, and certain chemicals. Nitrates and phosphates, often found in detergents and fertilizers, act as nutrients that can lead to excessive growth of algae in water bodies, harming other aquatic life.

🧪
Chemicals

Components of Wastewater: Bacteria and Other Microbes

  • Disease-causing bacteria (pathogens).
  • Viruses, fungi, protozoa.
  • Can cause cholera, typhoid, dysentery.

🎙️ Perhaps the most dangerous components of untreated wastewater are the harmful microorganisms. These include bacteria, viruses, and other germs that can cause severe diseases like cholera, typhoid, and dysentery if consumed. This is why proper sanitation is so critical.

Harmful Effects of Untreated Wastewater: Health Risks

  • Spreads water-borne diseases like cholera, typhoid.
  • Contaminates drinking water sources.
  • Impacts communities, especially children.

🎙️ Discharging untreated wastewater directly into rivers or lakes is extremely dangerous. It contaminates our water sources, making them unsafe for drinking, bathing, or even recreation. This leads to the spread of many water-borne diseases, affecting public health significantly.

🤢
Sickness

Harmful Effects of Untreated Wastewater: Environmental Damage

  • Kills aquatic plants and animals.
  • Pollutes rivers, lakes, and oceans.
  • Creates foul odours and unhealthy environments.

🎙️ Beyond human health, untreated wastewater devastates the environment. The organic matter consumes oxygen in water, suffocating fish and other aquatic organisms. Toxins can accumulate in the food chain, harming ecosystems and creating unpleasant, unhealthy surroundings.

Why Treat Wastewater? It's a Necessity!

  • Protects public health from diseases.
  • Preserves our natural water bodies.
  • Allows for reuse of treated water.

🎙️ Given all these dangers, treating wastewater isn't just an option; it's a necessity. It's crucial for safeguarding human health, protecting our precious environment, and ensuring that we have clean water resources for future generations. It's a step towards sustainable living.

Wastewater Treatment Plant (WWTP)

  • A facility to remove pollutants from wastewater.
  • Involves physical, chemical, and biological processes.
  • Converts wastewater into treated water (effluent) and sludge.

🎙️ A Wastewater Treatment Plant, or WWTP, is like a big factory designed to clean dirty water. It uses a series of steps to remove all the harmful substances, turning wastewater into water that is much safer to release back into the environment or even reuse.

Wastewater from homes and industries is treated in multiple stages to remove contaminants
14 / 25

Step 1: Preliminary Treatment (Screening)

  • Large physical impurities are removed.
  • Wastewater passes through bar screens.
  • Removes rags, sticks, plastic, stones.

🎙️ The first step is like a giant sieve. Wastewater flows through large bar screens that trap big items like plastic bottles, rags, and sticks. This prevents damage to the machinery in the later stages of treatment.

Step 2: Primary Treatment (Sedimentation)

  • Water flows into a grit and sand removal tank.
  • Heavier solids (grit, sand) settle at the bottom.
  • Organic solids also settle in a large tank (settling tank).

🎙️ After screening, the water enters a grit removal tank where sand and small stones settle down. Then, it moves to a large sedimentation tank where heavier organic solids, called sludge, sink to the bottom, and lighter impurities like oil and grease float to the top, forming scum.

⏳
Settling Solids
16 / 25

Step 3: Secondary Treatment (Biological Process)

  • Aeration tanks pump air into the water.
  • Beneficial bacteria grow and consume organic waste.
  • Microbes break down pollutants into harmless substances.

🎙️ This is where the magic happens with microorganisms! Air is pumped into the water to help beneficial bacteria grow. These bacteria then eat up the remaining organic matter and other pollutants in the water, cleaning it further. This process is called biological treatment.

🌬️
Aeration
17 / 25

Step 4: Sludge Treatment

  • Sludge from primary and secondary treatment is collected.
  • Transferred to a separate tank (digester).
  • Anaerobic bacteria break down sludge, producing biogas.

🎙️ The settled sludge from the primary and secondary treatment stages is not wasted. It's transferred to a special tank called a digester. Here, different types of bacteria, which don't need oxygen, break down the sludge and produce biogas, which can be used as fuel.

💩
Digesting Sludge
18 / 25

Step 5: Disinfection and Release

  • Treated water (effluent) is disinfected.
  • Chlorine or ozone is used to kill remaining germs.
  • Cleaned water is released into rivers or used for irrigation.

🎙️ After biological treatment, the water is much cleaner, but it might still contain some harmful germs. So, it undergoes disinfection, usually with chlorine or UV light, to kill any remaining bacteria. This treated water, called effluent, is then safe enough to be discharged into natural water bodies or used for purposes like irrigation.

✨
Clean Water

The Sewerage System: Our Underground Network

  • Network of pipes that carry wastewater.
  • Connects homes, hospitals, industries to WWTPs.
  • Manholes provide access for inspection and cleaning.

🎙️ The sewerage system is the hidden network of pipes beneath our cities that collects all the wastewater and transports it to the treatment plants. Think of it as the veins and arteries of a city, carrying away the used water to be cleaned. Manholes are important access points for maintenance.

Sanitation and Disease: A Direct Link

  • Poor sanitation leads to contaminated water.
  • Causes widespread diseases like cholera, dysentery, hepatitis.
  • Open defecation is a major health risk.

🎙️ There's a very direct link between sanitation and health. Poor sanitation, like open defecation or improper disposal of sewage, directly contaminates water sources and soil. This leads to a higher incidence of severe water-borne diseases, especially in rural areas or slums.

Better Housekeeping Practices: What YOU Can Do!

  • Do not throw oil and fats down the drain.
  • Do not throw chemicals like paint, medicines down the drain.
  • Use dustbins for solid waste, not drains.

🎙️ We all have a role to play in preventing wastewater pollution. Simple actions at home, like not pouring oil and grease down the sink, or disposing of chemicals and solid waste in dustbins instead of drains, can make a huge difference in keeping our pipes clear and reducing the load on treatment plants.

🗑️
Use Dustbin

Alternative Arrangements for Sewage Disposal

  • Septic tanks for individual homes in rural areas.
  • Composting pits for converting human waste into manure.
  • On-site sewage disposal systems where no sewerage exists.

🎙️ In places where there isn't a proper sewerage system, like many rural areas, alternative methods are used. Septic tanks are common, where sewage is collected and decomposed by bacteria. Composting pits are another eco-friendly option that turns waste into useful manure.

What is Heat?

  • A form of energy.
  • Makes things feel hot.
  • Can be transferred from one object to another.

🎙️ Heat is a type of energy that makes objects feel hot. It's not a substance, but rather the internal energy of particles vibrating. When an object gains heat energy, its particles move faster, and it feels hotter.

Measuring Temperature Accurately

  • Temperature is the degree of hotness or coldness of an object.
  • It's a precise measurement, unlike our sense of touch.
  • We use a special device to measure it.

🎙️ Temperature gives us an exact number for how hot or cold something is. It's a fundamental physical quantity. This allows scientists and doctors to communicate precise conditions.

Thermometers: Our Heat Detectors

  • Devices used to measure temperature.
  • Most common type uses mercury or alcohol.
  • Works on the principle of thermal expansion.

🎙️ A thermometer is essentially a tube with a liquid that expands when heated and contracts when cooled. This change in volume is then measured against a calibrated scale to give us a temperature reading.

📏
Thermometer
6 / 25

The Clinical Thermometer

  • Used to measure body temperature.
  • Has a short range, usually 35°C to 42°C.
  • Contains a kink to prevent mercury from falling back quickly.

🎙️ This is the thermometer you'd use at home or see at a doctor's clinic. The kink, or constriction, is very important as it holds the mercury in place after you take it out, allowing you to read the temperature accurately.

1Wash thermometer
2Shake down mercury
3Place under tongue/armpit
4Read temperature
7 / 25

Handle Clinical Thermometers with Care

  • Wash before and after use.
  • Shake mercury below 35°C.
  • Do not hold by the bulb.
  • Read at eye level.

🎙️ It's crucial to follow these precautions for accurate readings and safety. Never put a clinical thermometer in hot water, as its limited range can cause it to burst.

⚠️
Safety First

The Laboratory Thermometer

  • Used to measure temperatures of objects in labs.
  • Has a wider range, typically -10°C to 110°C.
  • No kink, so mercury falls back immediately.

🎙️ This type of thermometer is designed for experiments and measuring the temperature of various substances. Because it doesn't have a kink, you need to read the temperature while the bulb is still in contact with the substance being measured.

🧪
Lab Use
9 / 25

Using a Laboratory Thermometer Safely

  • Keep the bulb immersed in the substance.
  • Bulb should not touch the bottom or sides of the container.
  • Read while immersed, at eye level.

🎙️ Unlike the clinical thermometer, the laboratory thermometer's reading changes as soon as it's removed from the substance. So, ensure it remains in contact with the substance throughout the measurement.

🔬
Accurate Readings

Clinical vs. Laboratory Thermometer

  • Clinical: measures body temp (35-42°C), has kink.
  • Laboratory: measures other objects (-10-110°C), no kink.

🎙️ The key differences lie in their temperature range, the presence or absence of a kink, and their specific applications. Understanding these helps us choose the right tool for the job.

How Does Heat Travel?

  • Heat always flows from a hotter object to a colder object.
  • This transfer continues until both objects reach the same temperature.
  • There are three main ways heat can travel.

🎙️ Imagine holding an ice cube. Heat from your hand flows to the ice cube, making it melt and your hand feel cold. This natural flow from hot to cold is fundamental to understanding heat transfer.

↔️

Conduction: Heat Through Solids

  • Heat transfer in solids, where particles are closely packed.
  • Heat passes from particle to particle without actual movement of particles.
  • Common in metals.

🎙️ In conduction, the hot end of a metal rod makes its particles vibrate faster. These vibrations are passed on to neighboring particles, and so on, transferring heat along the rod.

Heat transfers from hot to cold regions through three methods: conduction (direct contact)
13 / 25

Good Conductors of Heat

  • Materials that allow heat to pass through them easily.
  • Examples: metals like iron, copper, aluminum.
  • Used in cooking utensils and radiators.

🎙️ Metals are excellent conductors because their free electrons can quickly transfer heat energy. This is why our cooking pans are made of metal – to efficiently transfer heat from the stove to the food.

🍳🍳🍳🍳
Metal Cookware
14 / 25

Poor Conductors (Insulators)

  • Materials that do not allow heat to pass through them easily.
  • Examples: wood, plastic, air, wool.
  • Used to keep things warm or cold.

🎙️ Insulators have tightly bound electrons and loosely packed particles (like air), which makes it difficult for heat to transfer. This property is useful for making handles of hot utensils or insulating our homes.

🧤🧤🧤🧤
Warm & Safe

Convection: Heat in Fluids

  • Heat transfer in liquids and gases (fluids).
  • Hotter, less dense fluid rises; colder, denser fluid sinks.
  • Creates a convection current.

🎙️ Convection involves the actual movement of the fluid particles. When water is heated from below, the warmer water becomes lighter and moves up, while cooler, heavier water moves down to take its place, creating a continuous current.

1Heat source warms fluid
2Warm fluid rises
3Cool fluid sinks
4Cycle repeats

Convection Around Us

  • Boiling water in a pot.
  • Sea and land breezes.
  • Room heaters warming a room.

🎙️ Sea breezes occur because land heats up faster than water during the day. The hot air above the land rises, and cooler air from the sea rushes in to replace it, creating a breeze.

Radiation: Heat Without Contact

  • Heat transfer that does not require any medium.
  • Travels as electromagnetic waves.
  • Can occur even in a vacuum.

🎙️ Radiation is how the sun's heat reaches Earth, traveling through the vast emptiness of space. You feel radiation when you stand near a bonfire or a hot electric heater, even without touching them.

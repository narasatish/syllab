What is Disease? When the Body Malfunctions

  • Analogy: A car with a flat tire or engine trouble.
  • Any condition that impairs normal functioning of the body.
  • Causes discomfort, dysfunction, or distress.

🎙️ Just as a car cannot perform its function with a flat tire, a diseased body cannot function optimally. Disease is essentially a deviation from the normal healthy state, leading to various signs and symptoms. It can affect any part of the body and manifest in many ways, from a simple cold to a complex chronic condition.

Types of Diseases: Infectious vs. Non-Infectious

  • Infectious: Transmitted from one person to another.
  • Non-infectious: Not transmitted, often lifestyle-related.
  • Understanding transmission is key for prevention.

🎙️ This is a fundamental classification. Infectious diseases, like the common cold, spread easily, while non-infectious ones, like diabetes or cancer, do not. Recognizing this difference is crucial for public health strategies and personal preventative measures. We will focus primarily on infectious diseases in this chapter.

Common Infectious Diseases: Bacterial (Typhoid, Pneumonia)

  • Typhoid: Salmonella typhi, contaminated food/water.
  • Symptoms: High fever, weakness, stomach pain, constipation.
  • Pneumonia: Streptococcus pneumoniae/Haemophilus influenzae, droplets.
  • Symptoms: Fever, chills, cough, headache; alveoli fill with fluid.

🎙️ Bacterial diseases are widespread and can be quite severe. Typhoid is a classic example transmitted through poor sanitation, while pneumonia affects the respiratory system, often spread via respiratory droplets. Remember the causative agents and key symptoms for exam purposes, as these are frequently asked.

Common Infectious Diseases: Viral (Common Cold, Dengue)

  • Common Cold: Rhinoviruses, droplet infection.
  • Symptoms: Nasal congestion, sore throat, cough, headache.
  • Dengue: Dengue virus (DENV), Aedes mosquito vector.
  • Symptoms: High fever, severe headache, muscle/joint pain, rash.

🎙️ Viral diseases are also very common. The common cold is a mild but widespread respiratory infection. Dengue, on the other hand, is a serious mosquito-borne viral disease prevalent in tropical and subtropical regions, including India. Pay attention to the vectors involved in diseases like Dengue.

Common Infectious Diseases: Protozoan (Malaria, Amoebiasis)

  • Malaria: Plasmodium species (P. vivax, P. falciparum), Anopheles mosquito.
  • Symptoms: Chills, recurrent high fever, sweating; affects RBCs.
  • Amoebiasis: Entamoeba histolytica, contaminated food/water.
  • Symptoms: Constipation, abdominal pain, cramps, blood/mucus in stools.

🎙️ Protozoan diseases like Malaria are a major public health concern globally. Understanding the life cycle of Plasmodium is critical for your exams. Amoebiasis is an intestinal infection spread through contaminated food and water, highlighting the importance of hygiene. Both are significant topics for your board examinations.

Common Infectious Diseases: Fungal (Ringworm)

  • Ringworm: Fungi like Microsporum, Epidermophyton, Trichophyton.
  • Transmission: Direct contact, contaminated clothes/towels.
  • Symptoms: Dry, scaly lesions on skin, nails, scalp; intense itching.

🎙️ Fungal infections, though often less severe than bacterial or viral ones, can be persistent and uncomfortable. Ringworm is a common example that thrives in warm, moist conditions. Maintaining personal hygiene and avoiding sharing personal items are important preventive measures. Remember the characteristic symptoms of ringworm.

Common Infectious Diseases: Helminthic (Ascariasis, Filariasis)

  • Ascariasis: Ascaris lumbricoides (roundworm), contaminated water/vegetables.
  • Symptoms: Internal bleeding, muscular pain, fever, anaemia, intestinal blockage.
  • Filariasis (Elephantiasis): Wuchereria bancrofti/malayi (filarial worm), mosquito vector.
  • Symptoms: Chronic inflammation of lymphatic vessels, swelling of limbs/genitals.

🎙️ Helminthic diseases are caused by parasitic worms and can lead to chronic debilitating conditions. Ascariasis is an intestinal parasite, while filariasis causes severe swelling due to lymphatic blockage. Both highlight the impact of environmental sanitation and vector control on public health. The life cycle of Ascaris is an important diagram-based question.

Innate Immunity: Our First Line of Defence

  • Non-specific, present from birth.
  • Provides different types of barriers.
  • Four main types of barriers.

🎙️ Innate immunity is our body's built-in, ready-to-go defence system. It doesn't discriminate between pathogens; it just acts. Think of it as the general security measures of a country. These barriers are crucial in preventing most pathogens from even entering or establishing an infection in our body.

Acquired Immunity: The Specific Response

  • Pathogen-specific, developed after exposure.
  • Characterized by memory and specificity.
  • Mediated by B-lymphocytes and T-lymphocytes.

🎙️ Unlike innate immunity, acquired immunity is highly specialized and 'learns' from past encounters. It's like a special forces unit that trains to fight specific enemies. This memory allows for a faster and stronger response upon re-exposure to the same pathogen, a concept vital for vaccination.

Primary vs. Secondary Immune Response

  • Primary: First encounter, slow and low intensity.
  • Secondary: Subsequent encounter, rapid and high intensity.
  • Memory cells are key to the secondary response.

🎙️ Imagine meeting someone for the first time versus meeting an old friend. The primary response is like a cautious first meeting, while the secondary response is like instantly recognizing and reacting to a familiar face. This difference in speed and strength is fundamental to how vaccines protect us, by eliciting a primary response without causing disease.

Humoral and Cell-Mediated Immunity (CMI)

  • Humoral (Antibody-mediated): B-cells produce antibodies in blood.
  • CMI: T-cells directly attack infected cells/cancer cells.
  • Both are crucial for comprehensive protection.

🎙️ Acquired immunity has two main arms. Humoral immunity uses antibodies circulating in our body fluids to neutralize pathogens. Cell-mediated immunity, on the other hand, involves T-cells directly destroying infected cells. Both systems work in tandem to provide robust protection against a wide range of threats.

Active and Passive Immunity: How We Acquire It

  • Active Immunity: Body produces its own antibodies.
  • Passive Immunity: Ready-made antibodies are given.
  • Both can be natural or artificial.

🎙️ Think of active immunity as learning to ride a bicycle yourself – you develop the skill. Passive immunity is like being given a ride – you get the benefit without having to learn. Understanding these distinctions is important for comprehending how vaccines and antitoxins work. Natural active immunity is after infection, while artificial active is vaccination.

Vaccination and Immunisation: Building Protection

  • Principle: Introduce weakened/inactivated pathogen or antigen.
  • Body produces primary immune response and memory cells.
  • Protects against future infections by the same pathogen.

🎙️ Vaccination is a cornerstone of public health, saving millions of lives annually. It cleverly exploits the memory property of acquired immunity. By exposing the body to a harmless form of the pathogen, we 'teach' our immune system to recognize and rapidly fight off the real threat later. This is a critical concept for your exams.

Autoimmunity: When the Body Attacks Itself

  • Immune system attacks 'self' cells by mistake.
  • Loss of ability to differentiate between self and non-self.
  • Example: Rheumatoid Arthritis, affecting joints.

🎙️ Sometimes, the immune system, designed to protect, makes a critical error. In autoimmunity, it loses its tolerance for self-components and mounts an attack on the body's own tissues. This can lead to chronic inflammatory conditions, like rheumatoid arthritis, which can be very debilitating. This is a fascinating but complex area of immunology.

Allergy: An Exaggerated Immune Response

  • Exaggerated reaction to certain environmental antigens (allergens).
  • Examples: Pollen, dust mites, animal dander.
  • Symptoms: Sneezing, watery eyes, breathing difficulty.
  • Mediated by IgE antibodies and mast cells (histamine release).

🎙️ Allergy is essentially an overreaction by our immune system to substances that are usually harmless. While most people tolerate pollen, an allergic individual's body perceives it as a threat. The release of histamine is what causes those familiar symptoms like sneezing and runny nose. Antihistamines are commonly used to manage these symptoms.

AIDS: Acquired ImmunoDeficiency Syndrome

  • Caused by Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV).
  • HIV attacks helper T-cells, severely weakening immunity.
  • Transmission: Sexual contact, contaminated blood, mother to child.
  • Prevention: Safe practices, awareness, no cure but manageable.

🎙️ AIDS is a devastating condition where the immune system is progressively destroyed, leaving the body vulnerable to opportunistic infections. HIV specifically targets helper T-cells, which are crucial for coordinating immune responses. Understanding its transmission routes is vital for prevention, as there is currently no cure, though treatments can manage the disease.

Cancer: Uncontrolled Cell Growth

  • Abnormal and uncontrolled proliferation of cells.
  • Benign tumors (localized) vs. Malignant tumors (metastasis).
  • Causes: Carcinogens (physical, chemical, biological).
  • Detection: Biopsy, radiography, MRI, CT scan. Treatment: Surgery, chemo, radio.

🎙️ Cancer is a complex disease characterized by cells that lose their normal growth control mechanisms. Malignant tumors are particularly dangerous because they can spread to other parts of the body, a process called metastasis. Early detection and a combination of treatments are crucial for improving patient outcomes. This is a significant topic for both exams and general awareness.

Common Mistakes in Human Health and Disease

What is Health?

  • WHO Definition: A state of complete physical, mental, and social well-being.
  • Not merely absence of disease or infirmity.
  • Essential for productivity and happiness.
  • Impacts economic prosperity and social progress.

🎙️ Health is much more than just not being sick. The World Health Organization defines it broadly, encompassing physical fitness, mental peace, and good social interactions. A healthy individual is more productive, contributing positively to society and the economy.

Factors Affecting Health

  • Genetic Disorders: Inherited predispositions to certain diseases.
  • Infections: Caused by pathogens like bacteria, viruses, fungi.
  • Lifestyle: Diet, exercise, hygiene, habits (e.g., smoking, alcohol).
  • Environmental Factors: Pollution, sanitation, access to clean water.

🎙️ Our health is a complex interplay of various factors. Genetic makeup can make us susceptible to certain conditions. Infections are a major cause of illness globally. Importantly, our lifestyle choices and the environment we live in play a significant role in maintaining or deteriorating our health.

Disease Classification

  • Infectious Diseases: Transmissible from one person to another.
  • Non-infectious Diseases: Not transmitted, often lifestyle or genetic.
  • Examples of infectious: Typhoid, Common Cold, Malaria.
  • Examples of non-infectious: Cancer, Diabetes, Hypertension.

🎙️ Diseases are broadly classified into infectious and non-infectious. Infectious diseases, as the name suggests, can spread. Non-infectious diseases, like many chronic conditions, do not spread but are often linked to genetics or lifestyle. This distinction is crucial for understanding prevention strategies.

Pathogens: The Disease-Causing Agents

  • Organisms causing disease in a host organism.
  • Include bacteria, viruses, fungi, protozoans, helminths.
  • Enter the body through various routes (air, water, food, vectors).
  • Adapt to survive within the host environment.

🎙️ Pathogens are the culprits behind infectious diseases. They are diverse, ranging from microscopic bacteria and viruses to larger parasitic worms. These agents have evolved mechanisms to enter our bodies, multiply, and cause damage, leading to symptoms of illness. Understanding them is key to combating disease.

Bacterial Disease: Typhoid

  • Causative Agent: Salmonella typhi (bacterium).
  • Transmission: Contaminated food and water.
  • Symptoms: Sustained high fever (39° to 40°C), weakness, stomach pain, constipation, headache.
  • Diagnosis: Widal test.

🎙️ Typhoid is a severe bacterial infection. It's often associated with poor sanitation. The Widal test is a common diagnostic tool. Untreated, it can lead to serious complications like intestinal perforation. Prevention focuses on hygiene and safe drinking water.

Bacterial Disease: Pneumonia

  • Causative Agents: Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae (bacteria).
  • Affects: Alveoli of the lungs fill with fluid.
  • Symptoms: Fever, chills, cough, headache, difficulty breathing.
  • Transmission: Droplets from infected person (sneezing, coughing).

🎙️ Pneumonia is a serious respiratory infection where the air sacs in the lungs become inflamed and filled with fluid, impairing oxygen exchange. It can be particularly dangerous for the very young and the elderly. Good hygiene and avoiding close contact with infected individuals can help prevent its spread.

Viral Disease: Common Cold

  • Causative Agent: Rhinoviruses (group of viruses).
  • Affects: Nose and respiratory passage, not lungs.
  • Symptoms: Nasal congestion, sore throat, cough, headache, fatigue.
  • Transmission: Droplets (cough, sneeze) and contaminated objects.

🎙️ The common cold is a very prevalent viral infection, distinct from pneumonia as it primarily affects the upper respiratory tract. It's highly contagious and spreads easily. While generally mild, it can be disruptive. There's no cure, but symptoms can be managed. Good hand hygiene is key to prevention.

Protozoan Disease: Malaria

  • Causative Agent: Plasmodium (protozoan parasite).
  • Species: P. vivax, P. falciparum (most severe), P. malariae, P. ovale.
  • Vector: Female Anopheles mosquito.
  • Symptoms: Recurring high fever, chills, headache, nausea (cyclic).

🎙️ Malaria is a life-threatening disease caused by Plasmodium parasites, transmitted through the bite of infected female Anopheles mosquitoes. The characteristic cyclic fever and chills are due to the rupture of red blood cells. P. falciparum causes the most severe form, often fatal. Mosquito control is vital for prevention.

Malaria Life Cycle (Human Phase)

  • 1. Sporozoites (infectious form) enter human blood via mosquito bite.
  • 2. Travel to liver, multiply asexually (schizogony).
  • 3. Released merozoites infect Red Blood Cells (RBCs).
  • 4. Merozoites multiply in RBCs, causing their rupture and symptoms.

🎙️ Understanding the life cycle is crucial for malaria control. In the human host, sporozoites first invade liver cells, then release merozoites that target red blood cells. The rupture of RBCs releases toxins, causing the characteristic fever and chills. Some merozoites develop into gametocytes, ready for the mosquito phase.

Malaria Life Cycle (Mosquito Phase)

  • 1. Infected mosquito ingests gametocytes during blood meal.
  • 2. Gametocytes develop into gametes, fertilization occurs in mosquito gut.
  • 3. Zygote develops into oocyst, then sporozoites.
  • 4. Sporozoites migrate to mosquito's salivary glands, ready for new host.

🎙️ The mosquito is the definitive host where sexual reproduction of Plasmodium occurs. When an infected mosquito bites a human, the cycle continues. Breaking this cycle, for example, by controlling mosquito populations, is a key strategy in malaria eradication.

Protozoan Disease: Amoebiasis

  • Causative Agent: Entamoeba histolytica (protozoan parasite).
  • Transmission: Contaminated food and water (faecal-oral route).
  • Symptoms: Constipation, abdominal pain, cramps, stools with mucus and blood.
  • Vector: Houseflies can act as mechanical carriers.

🎙️ Amoebiasis is an intestinal infection common in areas with poor sanitation. The parasite lives in the large intestine. Houseflies play a role in spreading the cysts. Maintaining good personal hygiene and consuming safe food and water are crucial preventive measures.

Helminthic Disease: Ascariasis

  • Causative Agent: Ascaris lumbricoides (roundworm).
  • Transmission: Contaminated water, vegetables, fruits (eggs).
  • Symptoms: Internal bleeding, muscular pain, fever, anaemia, blockage of intestinal passage.
  • High prevalence in children.

🎙️ Ascariasis is a common parasitic infection, especially in children, caused by the human roundworm. The eggs are ingested and hatch in the intestine, where the worms mature. Severe infections can lead to malnutrition and even intestinal blockage. Hand washing and proper cooking of food are essential for prevention.

Helminthic Disease: Filariasis (Elephantiasis)

  • Causative Agent: Wuchereria bancrofti, W. malayi (filarial worms).
  • Transmission: Bite of infected female mosquito (Culex).
  • Affects: Lymphatic vessels of lower limbs, causing chronic inflammation.
  • Symptoms: Gross deformities, swelling (elephantiasis), genital organs affected.

🎙️ Filariasis is a chronic disease that can lead to severe disfigurement. The adult worms live in the lymphatic system, blocking lymph flow and causing swelling. It develops over many years. Mosquito control is the primary method of prevention, similar to malaria.

Fungal Disease: Ringworm

  • Causative Agents: Fungi of genera Microsporum, Trichophyton, Epidermophyton.
  • Affects: Skin, nails, scalp (dry, scaly lesions).
  • Symptoms: Itchy lesions, often appearing as a ring-like rash.
  • Transmission: Close contact with infected persons, soil, fomites (towels, clothes).

🎙️ Ringworm is a common fungal infection of the skin. It's highly contagious and thrives in warm, moist conditions. Good personal hygiene, avoiding sharing personal items, and keeping skin dry are important preventive measures. It's treatable with antifungal medications.

Prevention and Control of Infectious Diseases

  • Maintain personal and public hygiene (clean food, water, sanitation).
  • Control vectors (mosquito nets, insecticides, biological control).
  • Vaccination/Immunisation: Protects against specific pathogens.
  • Education and awareness about disease transmission.

🎙️ Preventing infectious diseases is a multi-pronged approach. Good hygiene prevents many diseases transmitted via the faecal-oral route. Vector control is crucial for diseases like malaria and filariasis. Vaccination offers specific protection, and public awareness empowers individuals to take preventive actions.

What is Immunity?

  • The ability of the host to fight against disease-causing organisms.
  • Protects against pathogens and their toxic products.
  • Two main types: Innate Immunity and Acquired Immunity.
  • Our body's defence mechanism.

🎙️ Immunity is our body's incredible ability to defend itself against invaders. It's what keeps us healthy and resilient. We'll explore two major categories: innate, which is present from birth, and acquired, which develops over our lifetime through exposure or vaccination.

Innate Immunity: Non-specific Defence

  • Present from birth, non-specific defence.
  • Physical Barriers: Skin, mucous membranes.
  • Physiological Barriers: Acid in stomach, saliva, tears.
  • Cellular Barriers: Phagocytes (e.g., neutrophils, macrophages).
  • Cytokine Barriers: Interferons (protect non-infected cells from viruses).

🎙️ Innate immunity is our first line of defence. It's like a general security system that doesn't target specific pathogens. Our skin acts as a physical wall, while stomach acid and tears are chemical deterrents. Specialised cells like macrophages engulf invaders, and interferons signal against viral threats.

Acquired Immunity: Specific Defence

  • Pathogen-specific, characterised by memory.
  • Develops after exposure to a pathogen.
  • Primary response: First encounter, slow and weak.
  • Secondary response: Subsequent encounter, rapid and strong.

🎙️ Acquired immunity is highly specific and has a 'memory'. It learns from past encounters. The primary response is when your body first sees a pathogen, leading to a mild illness. The secondary response is much faster and more potent, often preventing illness entirely due to memory cells.

Types of Acquired Immunity: Humoral vs. Cell-Mediated

  • Humoral Immunity (Antibody-mediated): B-lymphocytes produce antibodies.
  • Antibodies are proteins that circulate in blood and lymph to fight pathogens.
  • Cell-Mediated Immunity (CMI): T-lymphocytes directly kill infected cells.
  • CMI is responsible for graft rejection and fighting intracellular pathogens.

🎙️ Acquired immunity has two main branches. Humoral immunity involves B-cells making antibodies that neutralise pathogens in body fluids. Cell-mediated immunity involves T-cells directly destroying infected cells or cancer cells. Both are crucial for a robust immune response.

Active vs. Passive Immunity

  • Active Immunity: Body produces its own antibodies (slow but long-lasting).
  • Natural Active: Infection (e.g., chickenpox).
  • Artificial Active: Vaccination (e.g., polio vaccine).
  • Passive Immunity: Ready-made antibodies given (fast but short-lived).
  • Natural Passive: Antibodies from mother to foetus/infant (placenta, colostrum).
  • Artificial Passive: Antitoxin injections (e.g., tetanus antitoxin).

🎙️ This distinction is vital for understanding how we gain protection. Active immunity is when your body does the work, leading to long-term memory. Passive immunity is a quick fix, providing immediate protection but without long-term memory. Both have their specific uses in medical practice.

Vaccination and Immunisation

  • Principle: Introduce weakened/inactivated pathogen or antigens.
  • Triggers primary immune response, generating memory B and T cells.
  • Future exposure leads to rapid secondary response, preventing disease.
  • Herd Immunity: Protects populations when most individuals are vaccinated.

🎙️ Vaccination is one of the greatest achievements in medicine. By safely exposing the immune system to parts of a pathogen, it prepares the body to fight off a real infection effectively. This not only protects the individual but also contributes to 'herd immunity', safeguarding the community.

Allergies: Exaggerated Immune Response

  • Exaggerated response of the immune system to harmless substances (allergens).
  • Allergens: Pollen, dust mites, animal dander, certain foods, drugs.
  • Symptoms: Sneezing, watery eyes, runny nose, difficulty breathing (asthma).
  • Mediated by IgE antibodies and chemicals like histamine from mast cells.

🎙️ Allergies are essentially an overreaction of our immune system to things that are normally harmless. The body mistakenly identifies allergens as threats, leading to uncomfortable symptoms. Antihistamines are commonly used to alleviate these symptoms by blocking histamine action.

Autoimmunity: When Self Attacks Self

  • Immune system fails to distinguish between self and non-self antigens.
  • Attacks body's own cells and tissues.
  • Causes: Unknown, genetic predisposition and environmental factors suggested.
  • Examples: Rheumatoid Arthritis, Type 1 Diabetes, Multiple Sclerosis.

🎙️ Autoimmunity is a serious condition where the immune system, designed to protect, mistakenly turns against the body's own tissues. This leads to chronic inflammation and damage. The exact causes are often complex, involving both genetic and environmental triggers. There's no cure, but treatments aim to manage symptoms and suppress the immune response.

Immune System Organs (Lymphoid Organs)

  • Primary Lymphoid Organs: Bone Marrow (B-cell maturation), Thymus (T-cell maturation).
  • Secondary Lymphoid Organs: Spleen, Lymph Nodes, Tonsils, Peyer's Patches.
  • Secondary organs are sites where lymphocytes interact with antigens.
  • These organs provide the environment for lymphocyte activation and proliferation.

🎙️ The immune system isn't just a collection of cells; it's an intricate network of organs. Primary lymphoid organs are where immune cells are born and mature. Secondary lymphoid organs are like meeting points where these mature cells encounter pathogens and launch an immune response. Knowing these locations helps understand immune function.

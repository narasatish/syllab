What is Genetic Material?

  • Substance that carries genetic information from parent to offspring.
  • Controls development and functioning of an organism.
  • Must be stable, capable of replication, and allow for variation.
  • DNA is the primary genetic material in most organisms.

🎙️ The genetic material is essentially the blueprint for life. It dictates everything from our eye colour to our susceptibility to certain diseases. For a molecule to be considered genetic material, it must meet several criteria, including stability, the ability to make copies of itself, and the capacity for small changes that drive evolution.

DNA Structure: The Double Helix

  • Proposed by Watson and Crick in 1953.
  • Composed of two polynucleotide chains.
  • Chains are antiparallel (5'→3' and 3'→5').
  • Sugar-phosphate backbone on the outside, nitrogenous bases on the inside.

🎙️ The iconic double helix structure of DNA, discovered by Watson and Crick, revolutionized biology. Remember, the two strands run in opposite directions, which is crucial for replication and transcription. The backbone gives structural integrity, while the bases carry the genetic code.

Chargaff's Rules and Base Pairing

  • Erwin Chargaff's observations (1950) laid the foundation.
  • Adenine (A) always pairs with Thymine (T) via two hydrogen bonds.
  • Guanine (G) always pairs with Cytosine (C) via three hydrogen bonds.
  • A+G = T+C (Purines = Pyrimidines).

🎙️ Chargaff's rules are fundamental to understanding DNA structure and stability. The specific base pairing (A with T, G with C) ensures the two strands are complementary, making DNA replication accurate. The number of hydrogen bonds also contributes to the stability of the helix.

Packaging of DNA in Eukaryotes

  • Human DNA is ~2.2 meters long, needs extensive packaging.
  • DNA wraps around positively charged histone proteins (H2A, H2B, H3, H4).
  • Forms a nucleosome, the 'beads-on-string' structure.
  • Nucleosomes further coil to form chromatin fibers, then chromosomes.

🎙️ Imagine trying to fit a 2.2-meter-long thread into a tiny cell nucleus! This is where DNA packaging comes in. Histones, being positively charged, attract the negatively charged DNA, forming nucleosomes. This hierarchical coiling allows DNA to be compact and organized within the nucleus.

Griffith's Transforming Principle (1928)

  • Frederick Griffith's experiment with Streptococcus pneumoniae.
  • Rough (R) strain (non-virulent) and Smooth (S) strain (virulent).
  • Heat-killed S strain + live R strain = mice died.
  • Concluded a 'transforming principle' transferred from S to R strain.

🎙️ Griffith's experiment was a landmark in genetics, even though he didn't identify the transforming substance. The key takeaway is that some genetic material could be transferred between bacterial strains, altering their characteristics. This hinted at DNA's role, though not directly.

Avery, MacLeod, McCarty Experiment (1944)

  • Aimed to identify Griffith's 'transforming principle'.
  • Purified biochemicals (DNA, RNA, proteins) from heat-killed S cells.
  • Only DNA caused transformation of R cells into S cells.
  • Digesting DNA with DNase prevented transformation.

🎙️ This experiment provided strong evidence that DNA, not protein or RNA, was the genetic material. By systematically eliminating other macromolecules, Avery and his colleagues demonstrated DNA's unique ability to confer hereditary traits. This was a crucial step, though still met with some skepticism.

Hershey-Chase Experiment (1952)

  • Used bacteriophages (viruses that infect bacteria).
  • Labeled DNA with 32P (phosphorus) and proteins with 35S (sulfur).
  • Infected E. coli bacteria, then blended and centrifuged.
  • Found 32P (DNA) entered the bacterial cells, 35S (protein) did not.

🎙️ The Hershey-Chase experiment provided definitive and unambiguous proof that DNA is the genetic material. Their elegant use of radioactive isotopes clearly showed that only the genetic material, DNA, was transferred from the virus to the bacteria to direct new viral synthesis. This experiment silenced most of the remaining doubts.

DNA Replication: The Semiconservative Model

  • Proposed by Watson and Crick, experimentally proven by Meselson and Stahl.
  • Each new DNA molecule consists of one original strand and one newly synthesized strand.
  • Ensures genetic continuity and accurate inheritance.
  • Occurs during the S-phase of the cell cycle.

🎙️ DNA replication is a fundamental process, ensuring that every new cell receives a complete set of genetic instructions. The semiconservative model is key here: it means that the original DNA molecule is not destroyed but serves as a template for new synthesis, maintaining genetic fidelity.

Mechanism of DNA Replication: Key Enzymes

  • DNA Helicase: Unwinds the double helix.
  • DNA Polymerase III: Synthesizes new DNA strands (5'→3' direction).
  • DNA Ligase: Joins Okazaki fragments on the lagging strand.
  • Primase: Synthesizes RNA primers to initiate synthesis.

🎙️ DNA replication is a highly coordinated process involving many enzymes. Each enzyme has a specific role, from unwinding the helix to synthesizing new strands and sealing gaps. Understanding the function of each enzyme is crucial for explaining the overall mechanism.

Replication Fork and Strand Synthesis

  • Y-shaped structure where DNA unwinds and replication occurs.
  • Leading strand: Synthesized continuously in 5'→3' direction.
  • Lagging strand: Synthesized discontinuously as Okazaki fragments.
  • Okazaki fragments are later joined by DNA ligase.

🎙️ The replication fork is the active site of DNA synthesis. Due to the antiparallel nature of DNA and the 5' to 3' synthesis direction of DNA polymerase, one strand is synthesized continuously (leading), while the other is synthesized in short pieces (lagging strand), which are then joined together.

The Central Dogma of Molecular Biology

  • Proposed by Francis Crick.
  • Information flow: DNA → RNA → Protein.
  • DNA replication: DNA makes more DNA.
  • Reverse Transcription: RNA can make DNA (e.g., retroviruses).

🎙️ The Central Dogma describes the fundamental flow of genetic information. It's a cornerstone of molecular biology. While the primary flow is DNA to RNA to protein, remember the exceptions like reverse transcription, which highlights the versatility of nucleic acids.

Transcription: DNA to Messenger RNA (mRNA)

  • Process of synthesizing RNA from a DNA template.
  • Catalyzed by RNA polymerase.
  • Occurs in the nucleus in eukaryotes, cytoplasm in prokaryotes.
  • Only one strand of DNA (template strand) is transcribed.

🎙️ Transcription is the first step in gene expression, where the genetic information from DNA is copied into an RNA molecule. Unlike replication, only a specific segment of DNA (a gene) is transcribed, and only one of the two DNA strands acts as a template.

Types of RNA and Their Roles

  • Messenger RNA (mRNA): Carries genetic code from DNA to ribosome.
  • Ribosomal RNA (rRNA): Structural and catalytic component of ribosomes.
  • Transfer RNA (tRNA): Carries specific amino acids to the ribosome.
  • Small nuclear RNA (snRNA): Involved in splicing in eukaryotes.

🎙️ RNA comes in various forms, each with a crucial role in gene expression. mRNA acts as the direct messenger, tRNA is the adaptor molecule, and rRNA is the workhorse of the ribosome. Understanding their distinct functions is key to understanding protein synthesis.

The Genetic Code: Language of Life

  • Set of rules by which information encoded in mRNA is translated into protein.
  • Consists of codons (triplets of nucleotides).
  • 64 possible codons, 61 code for amino acids, 3 are stop codons.
  • AUG is the start codon (codes for Methionine).

🎙️ The genetic code is universal, dictating how a sequence of nucleotides translates into a sequence of amino acids. The triplet nature of codons ensures enough combinations to specify all 20 amino acids. Memorize the start and stop codons, as they are frequently tested.

Features of Genetic Code & Wobble Hypothesis

  • Degenerate: Multiple codons can specify the same amino acid.
  • Unambiguous: Each codon specifies only one amino acid.
  • Universal: Code is nearly identical across all life forms.
  • Wobble Hypothesis: Pairing between tRNA and mRNA is flexible at the third base of the codon.

🎙️ The genetic code has several important features. Degeneracy is a protective mechanism, reducing the impact of mutations. The wobble hypothesis explains why fewer tRNAs are needed than the 61 sense codons, adding efficiency to translation. Understand these features for exam questions.

Translation: Protein Synthesis

  • Process of synthesizing proteins from mRNA template.
  • Occurs on ribosomes in the cytoplasm.
  • Involves mRNA, tRNA, rRNA, amino acids, and various protein factors.
  • Proceeds in three steps: Initiation, Elongation, Termination.

🎙️ Translation is the final step in the central dogma, where the genetic message is converted into a functional protein. Ribosomes act as the protein synthesis factories, with tRNA molecules bringing the correct amino acids based on the mRNA codons. Each step is tightly regulated.

Regulation of Gene Expression: The Lac Operon

  • An operon is a unit of gene expression and regulation in prokaryotes.
  • Lac operon controls metabolism of lactose in E. coli.
  • Consists of a promoter, operator, structural genes (lac Z, Y, A), and a regulator gene (lac i).
  • An example of negative control and inducible operon.

🎙️ Gene regulation is crucial for cells to adapt to changing environments and conserve energy. The lac operon is the classic example of how genes can be turned 'on' or 'off' in response to nutrient availability. Understanding its components and their interactions is vital.

Lac Operon Mechanism: Induction by Lactose

  • In absence of lactose: Repressor protein binds to operator, preventing transcription.
  • In presence of lactose: Lactose (inducer) binds to repressor, inactivating it.
  • Inactive repressor cannot bind to operator; RNA polymerase transcribes structural genes.
  • Enzymes for lactose metabolism (β-galactosidase, permease, transacetylase) are produced.

🎙️ The lac operon demonstrates a brilliant regulatory mechanism. Lactose acts as an inducer, essentially removing the 'brake' on gene expression. This ensures that the enzymes for lactose breakdown are only produced when lactose is actually available, saving the cell energy.

Human Genome Project (HGP): A Megaproject

  • Launched in 1990, completed in 2003.
  • Goal: Sequence the entire human genome (all ~3 billion base pairs).
  • Methodology: Sequencing fragments, then assembling (BACs and YACs).
  • Provided a comprehensive map of human genetic information.

🎙️ The Human Genome Project was an unprecedented scientific endeavor, akin to sending a man to the moon in terms of its ambition and impact. It provided the complete genetic blueprint of a human, opening new avenues for understanding diseases and developing personalized medicine.

Salient Features of Human Genome

  • Human genome has ~3164.7 million base pairs.
  • Average gene has 3000 bases, largest known is Dystrophin (~2.4 million bases).
  • Less than 2% of the genome codes for proteins.
  • Repetitive sequences make up a large portion, with unknown functions.

🎙️ The HGP revealed surprising facts about our genome. For instance, only a tiny fraction actually codes for proteins. The vast majority consists of non-coding and repetitive DNA, whose functions are still being actively researched. This highlights the complexity beyond just protein-coding genes.

DNA Fingerprinting: Identity and Forensics

  • Technique to identify individuals based on unique DNA patterns.
  • Relies on Variable Number Tandem Repeats (VNTRs) – short repetitive sequences.
  • Steps: DNA extraction, PCR amplification, restriction digestion, gel electrophoresis, blotting, hybridization, autoradiography.
  • Applications: Forensic science, paternity testing, biodiversity conservation.

🎙️ DNA fingerprinting is a powerful tool with immense practical applications, especially in forensics. It leverages the unique variations in repetitive DNA sequences among individuals. Understand the steps involved, as they often appear in exam questions.

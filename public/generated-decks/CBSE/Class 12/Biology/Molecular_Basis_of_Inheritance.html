The Search for Genetic Material

  • Early belief: proteins were genetic material.
  • Frederick Griffith's 'Transforming Principle' (1928).
  • Avery, MacLeod, McCarty's biochemical characterization (1944).
  • DNA confirmed as the genetic material.

🎙️ For a long time, scientists debated what carried genetic information. Proteins seemed like strong candidates due to their diversity. However, a series of groundbreaking experiments gradually pointed towards DNA as the true genetic material, culminating in definitive proof.

Hershey-Chase Experiment (1952)

  • Used bacteriophages (viruses) and E. coli.
  • Labeled DNA with P-32 and protein with S-35.
  • Only P-32 (DNA) entered the bacterial cells.
  • Conclusively proved DNA is the genetic material.

🎙️ This experiment was pivotal. By cleverly labeling DNA and protein components of a virus, Hershey and Chase showed that only the genetic material, DNA, was transferred into the host cell to direct the synthesis of new viruses. This provided irrefutable evidence.

Structure of DNA: Watson & Crick Model

  • Double helix structure proposed in 1953.
  • Two polynucleotide strands, antiparallel.
  • Sugar-phosphate backbone, nitrogenous bases inside.
  • Specific base pairing: A=T, G≡C (Chargaff's Rule).

🎙️ The double helix model, proposed by Watson and Crick, revolutionized biology. It elegantly explained how genetic information could be stored and replicated. Remember the antiparallel orientation and the specific hydrogen bonding between complementary bases.

Packaging of DNA Helix

  • DNA is extremely long in eukaryotic cells.
  • Wrapped around histone proteins to form nucleosomes.
  • Nucleosomes coil further into chromatin fibers.
  • Chromatin condenses to form chromosomes during cell division.

🎙️ Imagine fitting a 2.2-meter long DNA molecule into a tiny nucleus! This incredible feat is achieved through elaborate packaging. Histone proteins, being positively charged, attract the negatively charged DNA, forming compact structures like nucleosomes and ultimately chromosomes.

RNA: The Versatile Molecule

🎙️ RNA is often seen as DNA's helper, but it's a versatile molecule with diverse functions. While DNA stores information, RNA is involved in expressing that information, carrying messages, and building proteins. Each type of RNA has a unique structure and role.

DNA vs. RNA: Key Differences

  • Sugar: Deoxyribose (DNA) vs. Ribose (RNA).
  • Bases: Thymine (DNA) vs. Uracil (RNA).
  • Strandedness: Double helix (DNA) vs. Single strand (RNA).
  • Stability: DNA more stable, RNA less stable and reactive.

🎙️ Understanding the differences between DNA and RNA is fundamental. These structural distinctions dictate their respective roles: DNA for stable information storage, and RNA for dynamic information transfer and catalytic functions. The 2'-OH group in ribose makes RNA more reactive.

Meselson-Stahl Experiment (1958)

  • Grew E. coli in N-15 (heavy isotope) medium.
  • Transferred to N-14 (light isotope) medium.
  • Analyzed DNA density after generations using CsCl gradient.
  • Confirmed semiconservative replication.

🎙️ This elegant experiment provided the experimental proof for semiconservative replication. By using heavy and light isotopes of nitrogen, Meselson and Stahl could distinguish between parental and newly synthesized DNA strands, showing the intermediate density after one generation.

Mechanism of DNA Replication

  • Origin of replication (Ori) site.
  • DNA helicase unwinds the double helix.
  • DNA polymerase synthesizes new strands.
  • Leading strand (continuous), Lagging strand (discontinuous - Okazaki fragments).

🎙️ Replication is a complex, enzyme-driven process. DNA helicase separates the strands, forming a replication fork. DNA polymerase then adds new nucleotides, always in the 5' to 3' direction. This leads to continuous synthesis on one strand and discontinuous synthesis on the other, creating Okazaki fragments.

Transcription Unit & RNA Polymerase

  • Composed of a promoter, structural gene, and terminator.
  • Promoter: binding site for RNA polymerase.
  • Structural gene: encodes the RNA sequence.
  • Terminator: signals end of transcription.

🎙️ A transcription unit defines the segment of DNA that is transcribed into RNA. RNA polymerase is the key enzyme; it binds to the promoter region, unwinds the DNA, and synthesizes RNA by reading the template strand. The terminator sequence then signals the enzyme to detach.

The Genetic Code: Language of Life

  • Relationship between nucleotide sequence and amino acid sequence.
  • Triplet code: three nucleotides (codon) specify one amino acid.
  • Discovered by Nirenberg, Khorana, and Holley.
  • Universal, degenerate, non-overlapping, unambiguous.

🎙️ The genetic code is like a dictionary that translates the language of nucleic acids into the language of proteins. Each three-nucleotide codon specifies a particular amino acid, forming the building blocks of proteins. Its universal nature is a testament to the common ancestry of life.

Features of the Genetic Code

  • Triplet: 64 codons (61 sense, 3 stop).
  • Degenerate: most amino acids specified by more than one codon.
  • Unambiguous: one codon codes for only one amino acid.
  • Universal: same code for all organisms (with minor exceptions).

🎙️ Understanding these features is crucial. The degeneracy provides some robustness against mutations, while the unambiguous nature ensures correct protein synthesis. The universality allows for genetic engineering across species. Remember AUG is the start codon and codes for methionine.

Wobble Hypothesis

  • Proposed by Francis Crick.
  • The first two bases of a codon pair strictly with anticodon.
  • The third base (wobble position) allows for flexibility.
  • Explains why fewer tRNAs are needed than codons.

🎙️ The wobble hypothesis explains the degeneracy of the genetic code and how a single tRNA can recognize multiple codons. This flexibility at the third base pair allows for efficient translation without requiring a unique tRNA for every single codon. It's a clever biological shortcut.

Translation: Protein Synthesis

  • Process of synthesizing proteins from mRNA template.
  • Occurs on ribosomes in the cytoplasm.
  • Involves mRNA, tRNA, rRNA, and various factors.
  • Converts genetic information into functional proteins.

🎙️ Translation is the ultimate step in gene expression, where the genetic message in mRNA is decoded into a sequence of amino acids, forming a polypeptide chain. Ribosomes act as the protein synthesis factories, reading the mRNA codons and facilitating the addition of amino acids by tRNAs.

Components of Translation

  • mRNA: carries genetic message from DNA.
  • tRNA: brings specific amino acids to the ribosome.
  • Ribosomes (rRNA + proteins): sites of protein synthesis.
  • Aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases: charge tRNAs with amino acids.

🎙️ Each component plays a vital role. mRNA provides the instructions, tRNA acts as the adapter molecule, and ribosomes are the catalytic machinery. The accurate 'charging' of tRNA with the correct amino acid by synthetases is critical for fidelity of protein synthesis.

Regulation of Gene Expression: Lac Operon

  • Model for gene regulation in prokaryotes (E. coli).
  • Regulates genes for lactose metabolism.
  • Inducible operon: switched ON in presence of lactose.
  • Negative control by repressor protein.

🎙️ Organisms need to regulate gene expression to conserve energy and respond to environmental changes. The Lac Operon is a classic example of how bacteria control the expression of genes involved in breaking down lactose. It's a crucial concept for understanding gene regulation.

The lac operon is a gene regulation system in bacteria that controls production of lactose

Lac Operon Mechanism

  • Repressor protein binds to operator, blocking transcription.
  • Lactose (inducer) binds to repressor, inactivating it.
  • RNA polymerase can then access promoter and transcribe genes.
  • Genes (Z, Y, A) produce enzymes for lactose breakdown.

🎙️ In the absence of lactose, the repressor protein actively blocks transcription. When lactose is present, it acts as an inducer, binding to the repressor and causing a conformational change that prevents it from binding to the operator. This allows the structural genes to be transcribed and translated.

DNA Fingerprinting

  • Technique to identify individuals based on DNA variations.
  • Developed by Alec Jeffreys.
  • Uses Variable Number Tandem Repeats (VNTRs).
  • Highly specific, used in forensics and paternity testing.

🎙️ DNA fingerprinting is a powerful tool leveraging the unique pattern of repetitive DNA sequences in each individual. Since these VNTRs are highly polymorphic, they create a distinctive 'genetic fingerprint' that can be used for identification in various applications.

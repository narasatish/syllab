Levels of Ecological Organisation

  • Ecology studies interactions at various levels.
  • Individual organism is the basic unit.
  • Hierarchy: Organism → Population → Community → Biome → Biosphere.

🎙️ Ecology isn't just about big forests; it starts with a single organism. We study how these organisms group together into populations, then form communities, which exist within larger biomes, ultimately making up our entire biosphere. Understanding this hierarchy is key.

Organism & Environment: Habitat and Niche

  • Habitat: The physical place an organism lives.
  • Niche: The functional role and resources an organism utilizes.
  • No two species can have exactly the same niche in a habitat (Competitive Exclusion Principle).

🎙️ Think of habitat as an organism's address, where it lives. Niche, on the other hand, is its profession or role within that address, including its diet, reproductive patterns, and interactions. A common mistake is confusing these two distinct concepts.

Abiotic Factors Affecting Organisms

  • Non-living physical and chemical components of an ecosystem.
  • Temperature: Most ecologically relevant factor.
  • Water: Essential for life, its availability affects adaptations.
  • Light: Crucial for photosynthesis, photoperiodism, and vision.
  • Soil: Composition, pH, mineral content determine vegetation.

🎙️ Abiotic factors are the non-living elements that profoundly shape an organism's life. Temperature and water are paramount, dictating where species can live and how they adapt. Remember, these factors aren't independent but interact complexly.

Responses to Abiotic Factors

  • Organisms develop strategies to cope with environmental changes.
  • Regulate: Maintain constant internal environment (homeostasis).
  • Conform: Body temperature/osmotic concentration changes with environment.
  • Migrate: Temporarily move to a more hospitable area.
  • Suspend: Enter a state of dormancy (e.g., hibernation, aestivation, diapause).

🎙️ When facing environmental challenges, organisms have evolved diverse responses. Regulators are like warm-blooded animals maintaining a constant body temperature, while conformers simply 'go with the flow.' Migration and suspension are temporary escape strategies.

Adaptations in Organisms

  • Any attribute that enables an organism to survive and reproduce.
  • Morphological: Structural changes (e.g., desert plants with thick cuticles).
  • Physiological: Internal functional changes (e.g., high altitude sickness adaptation).
  • Behavioural: Actions (e.g., desert lizards basking in sun).

🎙️ Adaptations are the evolutionary tools organisms use to thrive in their specific niches. From the physical structure of a cactus to the internal chemistry of a fish in cold water, or the sun-basking behavior of a lizard, adaptations are everywhere and crucial for survival.

Population Attributes: Introduction

  • A group of individuals of the same species living in a well-defined geographical area.
  • Share or compete for similar resources.
  • Attributes unique to populations, not individuals (e.g., birth rate, death rate).

🎙️ Moving from individual organisms, we now focus on populations. A population is more than just a collection of individuals; it has emergent properties like birth rates and death rates that an individual cannot possess. Understanding these attributes is foundational to population ecology.

Population Density: Counting Individuals

  • Number of individuals per unit area or volume.
  • Can be estimated indirectly (e.g., pug marks, fecal pellets).
  • Important for resource management and conservation efforts.

🎙️ Population density tells us how crowded a population is. While simply counting individuals is ideal, it's often impractical. Indirect methods, like counting animal tracks, provide valuable estimates, especially for elusive species. High density can mean increased competition.

Natality and Mortality: Birth and Death Rates

  • Natality (Birth Rate): Number of births per unit population per unit time.
  • Mortality (Death Rate): Number of deaths per unit population per unit time.
  • These rates significantly influence population size.

🎙️ Natality and mortality are the primary biological drivers of population change. A high birth rate combined with a low death rate leads to population growth, while the opposite leads to decline. These are expressed as per capita rates, making them comparable across different population sizes.

Immigration and Emigration: Movement of Individuals

  • Immigration: Individuals joining a population from elsewhere.
  • Emigration: Individuals leaving a population to go elsewhere.
  • These movements also impact population size and genetic diversity.

🎙️ Besides births and deaths, the movement of individuals into (immigration) and out of (emigration) a population can also significantly alter its size and structure. Think of birds migrating or people moving to new cities; these are examples of such movements.

Age Pyramids: Visualizing Population Structure

  • Graphical representation of the proportion of individuals in different age groups.
  • Typically divided into pre-reproductive, reproductive, and post-reproductive.
  • Shape indicates growth status: expanding (triangular), stable (bell-shaped), declining (urn-shaped).

🎙️ Age pyramids are powerful tools to understand the demographic structure of a population. A broad base indicates a high proportion of young individuals, suggesting future growth. A narrow base, conversely, suggests a declining population. This is crucial for planning and conservation.

Population Growth Models: Exponential Growth

  • Occurs when resources are unlimited and there are no limiting factors.
  • Population grows at an accelerating rate, forming a J-shaped curve.
  • Equation: dN/dt = rN, where r is intrinsic rate of natural increase.

🎙️ Exponential growth is what happens when a population has everything it needs – food, space, no predators. It grows very fast, but this model is rarely sustained in nature because resources are always finite. It's a theoretical maximum growth rate.

📈
Unlimited Growth

Worked Example: Exponential Growth Calculation

  • Problem: A population of 100 individuals has an intrinsic rate of natural increase (r) of 0.2 per year. Calculate the population size after 1 year if growth is exponential.
  • Formula: Nt = N0 * e^(rt)
  • Solution: N1 = 100 * e^(0.2*1) = 100 * 1.221 = 122.1 individuals.

🎙️ Let's apply the exponential growth formula. Here, N0 is the initial population, 'e' is Euler's number (approx 2.718), 'r' is the intrinsic rate, and 't' is time. This calculation shows the rapid increase under ideal conditions, though fractions of individuals are theoretical.

15 / 25

Population Growth Models: Logistic Growth

  • More realistic model, considering limited resources and competition.
  • Population growth slows down as it approaches carrying capacity (K).
  • Forms an S-shaped (sigmoid) curve. Equation: dN/dt = rN(K-N)/K.

🎙️ Logistic growth is what we typically observe in nature. As resources become scarce, competition increases, and the growth rate decelerates. The population eventually stabilizes around the carrying capacity, which is the maximum number of individuals an environment can sustain.

🔄
Limited Growth
16 / 25

Worked Example: Logistic Growth and Carrying Capacity (K)

  • Problem: A population has a carrying capacity (K) of 500 individuals and an intrinsic rate (r) of 0.3. If the current population (N) is 200, what is the growth rate (dN/dt)?
  • Formula: dN/dt = rN(K-N)/K
  • Solution: dN/dt = 0.3 * 200 * (500-200)/500 = 60 * (300/500) = 60 * 0.6 = 36 individuals/time unit.

🎙️ In this example, we see how the (K-N)/K factor in the logistic equation acts as a braking mechanism. When N is far from K, the growth is high. As N approaches K, this factor becomes smaller, reducing the growth rate, reflecting resource limitation.

Life History Variations: r- and K-selected Species

  • r-selected species: Produce many small offspring, short lifespan, rapid development (e.g., insects, bacteria).
  • K-selected species: Produce few large offspring, long lifespan, slow development, parental care (e.g., humans, elephants).
  • These strategies reflect adaptations to different environmental conditions.

🎙️ Organisms adopt different life history strategies based on their environment. r-selected species thrive in unstable environments by reproducing quickly, while K-selected species invest heavily in fewer offspring, suitable for stable environments near carrying capacity. Population Interactions: Introduction

  • Species rarely exist in isolation; they interact in various ways.
  • Interactions can be beneficial (+), detrimental (-), or neutral (0).
  • Types: Predation, Competition, Parasitism, Commensalism, Mutualism.

🎙️ No organism is an island. All species interact with others, and these interactions are fundamental to ecosystem function. We'll explore the major types of interactions, understanding their impact on the interacting species. Pay close attention to the symbols used to denote benefit or harm.

🤝

Predation: The Hunter and the Hunted (+/-)

  • One species (predator) kills and consumes another species (prey).
  • Important for transferring energy across trophic levels.
  • Keeps prey populations under control, maintains species diversity.

🎙️ Predation is a direct interaction where one benefits at the expense of another. It's not just about lions and deer; herbivores eating plants are also predators. This interaction is vital for energy flow and preventing overpopulation of prey species, thus maintaining ecological balance.

🐺🦌

Competition: The Struggle for Resources (-/-)

  • Occurs when two or more species (interspecific) or individuals of the same species (intraspecific) vie for limited resources.
  • Can lead to competitive exclusion (one species outcompetes another) or resource partitioning.
  • Both interacting species are negatively affected.

🎙️ Competition is a universal interaction, especially when resources are scarce. It's a lose-lose situation for both parties involved, as they expend energy vying for the same limited resource. This can drive evolutionary adaptations and shape community structure.

Resource Scarcity

Parasitism: Living Off a Host (+/-)

  • One species (parasite) lives on or in another species (host), deriving nourishment.
  • Parasite benefits, host is harmed (but usually not killed immediately).
  • Examples: Ticks on dogs, Cuscuta on plants, malarial parasite in humans.

🎙️ Parasitism is a highly evolved interaction where the parasite depends on the host for survival. Unlike predators, parasites typically don't kill their hosts quickly, as their survival depends on the host's continued existence. This relationship can be complex and co-evolutionary.

Commensalism and Mutualism: Positive Interactions

  • Commensalism (+/0): One species benefits, the other is unaffected (e.g., orchid on a tree).
  • Mutualism (+/+): Both interacting species benefit (e.g., lichen = alga + fungus).
  • These interactions highlight cooperation in ecosystems.

🎙️ Not all interactions are negative. Commensalism shows a one-sided benefit without harm, while mutualism is a win-win scenario, often leading to co-evolution. Understanding these positive interactions is crucial for appreciating the intricate web of life.

Ecology: The Study of Interactions

  • Branch of biology studying interactions among organisms and between organisms and their physical environment.
  • Term 'Ecology' coined by Ernst Haeckel.
  • Focuses on distribution, abundance, and relationships of organisms.

🎙️ Ecology is more than just nature study; it's a scientific discipline that explains why organisms live where they do and how they survive. It's about the intricate web of life on our planet.

Hierarchy of Ecology: From Organism to Biosphere

  • Organism: Individual living being.
  • Population: Group of individuals of same species.
  • Community: Different populations interacting.
  • Biome: Large regional unit with major vegetation.

🎙️ Ecology studies life at various levels, starting from a single organism and expanding to the entire biosphere. Understanding this hierarchy is fundamental to grasping ecological concepts.

Abiotic Factors: Temperature - The Master Factor

  • Most ecologically relevant environmental factor.
  • Affects enzyme kinetics, metabolic rate, and physiological functions.
  • Organisms are Eurythermal (wide range) or Stenothermal (narrow range).

🎙️ Temperature is a critical abiotic factor as it directly influences biological processes. Remember the terms Eurythermal and Stenothermal, as they describe an organism's tolerance to temperature fluctuations.

Abiotic Factors: Water - The Elixir of Life

  • Desert animals and plants have special adaptations for water conservation.

🎙️ Water is fundamental for survival. Its availability and quality, especially salinity in aquatic environments, dictate where organisms can live and how they adapt to conserve or manage water.

Coping with Stress: Regulators vs. Conformers

  • Regulate: Maintain constant internal body temperature/osmolarity (homeostasis). E.g., Mammals.
  • Conform: Body temperature/osmolarity changes with ambient conditions. E.g., Most plants, simple animals.
  • Regulation is energetically expensive.

🎙️ Organisms respond to environmental stress in various ways. Regulators actively maintain internal stability, while conformers allow their internal conditions to fluctuate with the external environment.

Coping with Stress: Migration & Suspension

  • Migrate: Temporarily move to a more hospitable area. E.g., Siberian cranes.
  • Suspend: Go into a state of reduced metabolic activity. E.g., Hibernation, aestivation, diapause (zooplankton).
  • These are temporary, short-term responses.

🎙️ When conditions become too harsh, some organisms opt for temporary solutions. Migration involves moving away, while suspension involves entering a dormant state to conserve energy until conditions improve.

Adaptations: Evolving for Survival

  • Any attribute of an organism (morphological, physiological, behavioral) that enables it to survive and reproduce.

🎙️ Adaptations are key to an organism's success in its specific niche. They are inherited traits that have evolved over generations, allowing species to thrive in challenging environments.

Population Attributes: Dynamics of Numbers

  • Birth Rate (Natality): Number of births per unit population per unit time.
  • Death Rate (Mortality): Number of deaths per unit population per unit time.
  • These rates determine population size changes.

🎙️ When studying populations, we look beyond individuals. Birth and death rates are crucial metrics that tell us whether a population is growing, shrinking, or remaining stable.

Population Growth: Unlimited Resources (Exponential Model)

  • Occurs when resources are unlimited and population grows rapidly.
  • Equation: dN/dt = rN (N=population size, r=intrinsic rate of natural increase).
  • Produces a J-shaped curve.
  • Rare in nature for sustained periods.

🎙️ Exponential growth is theoretical, assuming no environmental limits. While it can occur initially, no population can sustain this growth indefinitely due to resource constraints.

Population Growth: Limited Resources (Logistic Model)

  • More realistic model, considering limited resources.
  • Equation: dN/dt = rN (K-N)/K (K=carrying capacity).
  • Produces an S-shaped (Sigmoid) curve.
  • Population eventually stabilizes at carrying capacity (K).

🎙️ Logistic growth is what we typically observe in nature. As a population approaches its carrying capacity – the maximum number an environment can sustain – its growth rate slows down.

Problem Solving: Applying Population Growth Formulas

  • Question: A population of 500 bacteria has an intrinsic rate of natural increase (r) of 0.5 per hour. What is the population size after 2 hours, assuming exponential growth?
  • Solution: N(t) = N(0) * e^(rt). N(2) = 500 * e^(0.5*2) = 500 * e^1 ≈ 500 * 2.718 = 1359 bacteria.
  • Common Mistake: Forgetting to use 'e' for continuous growth.

🎙️ These formulas are important for exam questions. Pay close attention to whether the question implies exponential or logistic growth, and ensure you use the correct variables and constants.

Population Interactions: The Web of Life

  • No species lives in isolation; they interact in various ways.
  • Can be beneficial (+), detrimental (-), or neutral (0).
  • Types: Mutualism, Competition, Predation, Parasitism, Commensalism, Amensalism.

🎙️ The interactions between different species are fundamental to ecosystem function. Understanding these relationships helps us predict how changes in one species might affect others.

Mutualism: A Win-Win Relationship (+/+)

  • Both interacting species benefit.
  • Often obligatory and co-evolved.
  • Examples: Lichens (fungi + algae), Mycorrhizae (fungi + plant roots), Pollination (plant + insect).

🎙️ Mutualism is a beautiful example of cooperation in nature, where two species help each other thrive. These relationships are often highly specialized and essential for the survival of both partners.

Competition: The Struggle for Resources (-/-)

  • Both species are harmed due to shared limited resources.
  • Can be interspecific (between different species) or intraspecific (within same species).
  • Gause's Competitive Exclusion Principle: Two species cannot coexist indefinitely if they compete for the same limiting resources.

🎙️ Competition is a common interaction, especially when resources are scarce. Gause's Principle highlights that strong competition can lead to the exclusion of one species by another.

Predation & Parasitism: Exploitative Relationships (+/-)

  • Predation: Predator kills and consumes prey. E.g., Tiger and deer. Crucial for energy transfer and population control.
  • Parasitism: Parasite lives on or in host, deriving nourishment without immediately killing it. E.g., Lice on humans, Cuscuta on plants.
  • Host is harmed, parasite benefits.

🎙️ Predation and parasitism are 'plus-minus' interactions where one species benefits at the expense of another. Predators are essential for maintaining ecosystem balance, and parasites often have complex life cycles.

Commensalism & Amensalism: Mixed Outcomes

  • Commensalism (+/0): One species benefits, the other is unaffected. E.g., Orchid on a mango tree, cattle egret and grazing cattle.
  • Amensalism (-/0): One species is harmed, the other is unaffected. E.g., Penicillium inhibiting bacterial growth, black walnut tree releasing juglone.

🎙️ These interactions show that not all relationships are strictly beneficial or detrimental to both parties. Commensalism is often about finding shelter or food without impacting the host, while amensalism is often an accidental negative impact.

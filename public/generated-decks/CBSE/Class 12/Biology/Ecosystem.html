What is an Ecosystem?

  • A functional unit of nature.
  • Includes living organisms and non-living environment.
  • Interactions lead to nutrient cycling and energy flow.

🎙️ An ecosystem is essentially a community of living organisms interacting with each other and with their physical surroundings as a single, integrated unit. Think of it as a self-sustaining system where everything is connected and influences one another. This interaction is key to its definition.

Types of Ecosystems: Terrestrial & Aquatic

  • Terrestrial: Land-based, e.g., forests, grasslands, deserts.
  • Aquatic: Water-based, e.g., ponds, lakes, rivers, oceans.
  • Vary greatly in size, from a small pond to a vast ocean.

🎙️ Ecosystems are broadly categorized based on their physical environment. Terrestrial ecosystems are found on land, while aquatic ecosystems are found in water. Both types exhibit unique characteristics and house diverse flora and fauna adapted to their specific conditions.

Components of an Ecosystem: Biotic Factors

  • Living organisms within the ecosystem.
  • Producers: Autotrophs (e.g., plants, algae).
  • Consumers: Heterotrophs (e.g., animals).
  • Decomposers: Saprotrophs (e.g., bacteria, fungi).

🎙️ Biotic components are the living parts of an ecosystem, playing distinct roles. Producers form the base by creating their own food, consumers feed on others, and decomposers break down dead organic matter, recycling nutrients back into the system. These interactions are fundamental for energy flow.

Components of an Ecosystem: Abiotic Factors

  • Non-living physical and chemical components.
  • Examples: Temperature, water, light, soil, pH, minerals.
  • Crucial for the survival and distribution of biotic components.

🎙️ Abiotic factors are the non-living environmental conditions that significantly influence the living organisms. These include physical factors like temperature and light, and chemical factors like water availability and soil composition. Organisms must adapt to these factors to thrive in their specific ecosystem.

Ecosystem Structure: Stratification

  • Vertical distribution of different species.
  • Occupying different levels in an ecosystem.
  • Example: Trees form top layer, shrubs middle, herbs bottom.

🎙️ Stratification refers to the layering of different species at various vertical levels within an ecosystem, especially in terrestrial ones like forests. This arrangement allows different species to coexist by utilizing resources at different heights, reducing competition and maximizing resource use within the habitat.

Ecosystem Functions: Productivity

  • Rate of biomass production by producers.
  • Gross Primary Productivity (GPP): Total photosynthesis.
  • Net Primary Productivity (NPP): GPP minus respiration (available biomass).
  • Secondary Productivity: Rate of biomass formation by consumers.

🎙️ Productivity is a key function, representing the rate at which biomass is generated. GPP is the total energy captured by producers, while NPP is what's left after their own metabolic needs, making it available to heterotrophs. Secondary productivity measures biomass increase in consumers.

Ecosystem Functions: Decomposition

  • Breakdown of complex organic matter into inorganic substances.
  • Key processes: Fragmentation, Leaching, Catabolism, Humification, Mineralization.
  • Decomposers (bacteria, fungi) are vital for nutrient recycling.

🎙️ Decomposition is the essential process by which detritus is broken down, returning nutrients to the soil. It involves several distinct steps, each carried out by different organisms or physical processes. This cycle is critical for maintaining soil fertility and nutrient availability for producers.

Worked Example: Factors Affecting Decomposition Rate

  • Temperature: Higher temperature generally increases decomposition.
  • Moisture: Adequate moisture promotes decomposer activity.
  • Aeration: Oxygen-rich environments favor aerobic decomposition.
  • Chemical composition of detritus: Lignin/chitin slow down, nitrogen/sugar speed up.

🎙️ Decomposition rates are not constant; they are influenced by several environmental factors. For instance, in tropical rainforests, decomposition is rapid due to high temperatures and humidity. Conversely, in polar regions or deserts, decomposition is much slower. Understanding these factors is important for explaining nutrient cycling.

Ecosystem Functions: Energy Flow

  • Movement of energy through an ecosystem.
  • Starts with solar energy captured by producers.
  • Transferred sequentially through trophic levels.
  • Unidirectional and non-cyclic.

🎙️ Energy flow is a fundamental process, illustrating how energy enters an ecosystem and moves through its various components. Unlike nutrients, energy does not cycle; it flows in one direction, from the sun to producers, then to consumers, with significant loss at each step as heat. This makes it a one-way street.

Trophic Levels: Feeding Positions

  • Specific feeding position an organism occupies.
  • Producers (T1): Autotrophs, base of the food chain.
  • Primary Consumers (T2): Herbivores, feed on producers.
  • Secondary Consumers (T3): Carnivores, feed on primary consumers.
  • Tertiary Consumers (T4): Top carnivores, feed on secondary consumers.

🎙️ Trophic levels categorize organisms based on their feeding relationships. Each level represents a step in the food chain, showing how energy is transferred. Understanding these levels is essential for analyzing the structure and dynamics of an ecosystem and how energy is utilized.

The 10% Law of Energy Transfer

  • Proposed by Raymond Lindeman in 1942.
  • Only about 10% of energy is transferred from one trophic level to the next.
  • The remaining 90% is lost as heat during metabolic activities.
  • Limits the number of trophic levels in an ecosystem.

🎙️ The 10% Law is a crucial concept in ecology, explaining why food chains are typically short. This massive energy loss at each step means that higher trophic levels receive significantly less energy, limiting their biomass and population size. This principle governs the structure of ecological pyramids.

Ecological Pyramids: Graphical Representation

  • Graphical representation of trophic structure.
  • Base represents producers, successive levels represent consumers.
  • Pyramid of Number, Pyramid of Biomass, Pyramid of Energy.
  • Illustrate relationships between trophic levels.

🎙️ Ecological pyramids are visual tools that simplify complex trophic relationships. They help us understand the quantitative relationships between different trophic levels in terms of the number of individuals, biomass, or energy content. Each type of pyramid offers a unique perspective on ecosystem structure.

Pyramid of Number: Individuals per Trophic Level

  • Shows the number of individual organisms at each trophic level.
  • Can be upright (e.g., grassland ecosystem).
  • Can be inverted (e.g., single large tree supporting many insects).
  • Can be spindle-shaped (e.g., forest ecosystem with fewer trees than herbivores).

🎙️ The Pyramid of Number counts the individual organisms at each level. While it's often upright, it can be inverted, as seen with a single large producer supporting many primary consumers. This pyramid can sometimes be misleading due to the varying sizes of individual organisms.

Pyramid of Biomass: Total Mass at Each Level

  • Represents the total dry weight (biomass) of organisms.
  • Usually upright (e.g., most terrestrial ecosystems).
  • Can be inverted in aquatic ecosystems (e.g., pond/ocean).
  • Producers (phytoplankton) have less biomass than consumers (zooplankton).

🎙️ The Pyramid of Biomass measures the total mass of living organisms. Inverted pyramids of biomass are common in aquatic environments, where producers (phytoplankton) reproduce rapidly but have a short lifespan, leading to a smaller standing crop biomass than the longer-lived zooplankton and fish.

Pyramid of Energy: Energy Flow at Each Level

  • Shows the total energy content at each trophic level.
  • Always upright, never inverted.
  • Reflects the 10% law of energy transfer.
  • The most fundamental and accurate ecological pyramid.

🎙️ The Pyramid of Energy is always upright because energy is lost as heat at each successive trophic level, according to the 10% law. It represents the actual energy flow, making it the most consistent and ecologically sound pyramid. No ecosystem can have more energy at a higher trophic level than at a lower one.

Ecological Succession: Change Over Time

  • Gradual and predictable change in species composition.
  • Occurs in an area over ecological time.
  • Pioneer species: First to colonize a barren area.
  • Climax community: Stable, mature, self-perpetuating community.

🎙️ Ecological succession describes the process of change in the species structure of an ecological community over time. It's a natural process where one community replaces another, driven by environmental modifications. This journey from barren land to a stable climax community can take hundreds or thousands of years.

Types of Succession: Primary & Secondary

  • Primary Succession: Starts in an area where no living organisms existed.
  • Example: Newly cooled lava, bare rock, newly formed sand dunes.
  • Secondary Succession: Starts in areas where existing communities have been removed.
  • Example: Abandoned farmlands, deforested areas, areas after a fire.

🎙️ The distinction between primary and secondary succession is critical. Primary succession is a much slower process because it begins on completely barren land without any pre-existing soil. Secondary succession is faster as it occurs in areas where soil and some life forms already exist, providing a head start.

Hydrarch and Xerarch Succession

  • Hydrarch Succession: Takes place in wetter areas.
  • Progresses from hydric to mesic conditions.
  • Xerarch Succession: Takes place in dry areas.
  • Progresses from xeric to mesic conditions.
  • Both eventually lead to a mesic (medium water condition) climax community.

🎙️ These terms describe succession based on the initial water conditions. Hydrarch succession starts in water and gradually fills in, becoming drier. Xerarch succession starts in dry conditions and gradually becomes wetter. Interestingly, both pathways converge towards a moderate, or mesic, climax community, demonstrating a common endpoint for succession.

The Carbon Cycle: Life's Building Block

  • Carbon is a major constituent of all organic compounds.
  • Atmospheric CO2 is primary reservoir.
  • Photosynthesis fixes carbon, respiration releases it.
  • Burning of fossil fuels significantly impacts the cycle.

🎙️ The carbon cycle is fundamental to life, demonstrating how carbon moves between the atmosphere, oceans, land, and living organisms. Human activities like burning fossil fuels have significantly altered this natural balance, leading to increased atmospheric CO2 and climate change. Understanding this cycle is crucial for environmental awareness.

The Phosphorus Cycle: A Sedimentary Cycle

  • Phosphorus is a component of DNA, RNA, ATP, and bones.
  • Main reservoir: Earth's crust (rocks).
  • Released by weathering of rocks.
  • No significant atmospheric gaseous phase.

🎙️ Unlike the carbon and nitrogen cycles, the phosphorus cycle is primarily sedimentary, meaning its main reservoir is in rocks. It's a much slower cycle, relying on geological processes like weathering to release phosphorus into the ecosystem. This element is vital for many biological processes, from energy transfer to genetic material.

Defining an Ecosystem

  • Structural and functional unit of nature.
  • Comprises biotic (living) and abiotic (non-living) components.
  • These components interact and exchange materials.

🎙️ Formally, an ecosystem is the basic unit of nature where living organisms interact among themselves and with their surrounding physical environment. It's a dynamic system with continuous flow of energy and nutrients.

Biotic Components: The Living Players

  • Producers: Autotrophs (plants, algae).
  • Consumers: Heterotrophs (herbivores, carnivores, omnivores).
  • Decomposers: Saprotrophs (bacteria, fungi).

🎙️ Biotic components are all the living organisms. Producers make their own food, consumers eat others, and decomposers break down dead organic matter, recycling nutrients back into the environment.

Types of Ecosystems: Natural vs. Artificial

  • Natural: Function without human interference (forest, ocean).
  • Artificial (Man-made): Created and maintained by humans (crop field, aquarium).
  • Both have biotic and abiotic components.

🎙️ Ecosystems can be broadly categorized based on their origin. Natural ones are self-sustaining, while artificial ones require human input and management to maintain their balance.

Ecosystem Function: Primary Productivity

  • Rate of biomass production by producers (plants).
  • Gross Primary Productivity (GPP): Total photosynthesis.
  • Net Primary Productivity (NPP): GPP - Respiration (biomass available to consumers).

🎙️ Primary productivity is all about how much organic matter plants can create. NPP is particularly important as it represents the energy available at the base of the food chain for all other organisms.

🌱
Plant Growth

Ecosystem Function: Secondary Productivity

  • Rate of biomass production by consumers.
  • Involves assimilation of energy from consuming other organisms.
  • Lower than primary productivity due to energy loss.

🎙️ Secondary productivity is how much biomass is accumulated by heterotrophs – animals. It's always less than primary productivity because a lot of energy is lost as heat at each trophic level.

Ecosystem Function: Decomposition Process

  • Fragmentation: Detritivores break down detritus.
  • Leaching: Water-soluble nutrients seep into soil.
  • Catabolism: Bacteria/fungi release enzymes.
  • Humification: Formation of humus.
  • Mineralization: Release of inorganic nutrients.

🎙️ Decomposition is a vital process that recycles nutrients. It's a multi-step process, starting with physical breakdown and ending with the release of simple inorganic substances back into the ecosystem.

Factors Affecting Decomposition

🎙️ The rate of decomposition isn't constant; it's influenced by several factors. Optimal temperature and moisture are crucial for the microbes involved, and the type of organic matter also plays a big role.

Pyramid of Number: Counting Organisms

  • Represents the number of individuals at each trophic level.
  • Can be upright (grassland) or inverted (tree ecosystem).
  • Upright: Producers > Primary Consumers > Secondary Consumers.
  • Inverted: A single tree supporting many insects.

🎙️ The pyramid of number shows how many organisms are present at each level. It's not always upright; sometimes, a large producer can support many smaller consumers, leading to an inverted shape.

Pyramid of Biomass: Weighing the Levels

  • Represents total dry weight of organisms at each trophic level.
  • Can be upright (terrestrial) or inverted (aquatic).
  • Upright: Large biomass of plants supporting less animal biomass.
  • Inverted: Small biomass of phytoplankton supporting large fish biomass (due to rapid turnover).

🎙️ Biomass pyramids measure the total mass of living matter. In aquatic ecosystems, due to the fast reproduction and short lifespan of phytoplankton, the biomass pyramid can appear inverted.

Pyramid of Energy: The Energy Flow

  • Represents the total energy at each trophic level.
  • ALWAYS upright, as energy decreases at successive levels.
  • Only 10% energy transferred to the next trophic level (10% Law).

🎙️ The pyramid of energy is the most fundamental and universally upright pyramid. Energy is lost as heat at each transfer, so there's always less energy available at higher trophic levels, following the 10% law.

Ecological Succession: Change Over Time

  • Gradual and predictable change in species composition.
  • From pioneer species to climax community.
  • Primary Succession: Bare land (volcanic rock).
  • Secondary Succession: Disturbed area (abandoned field).

🎙️ Ecosystems are not static; they change over time. Ecological succession describes this process, leading to a more stable and diverse community. Primary and secondary succession differ in their starting conditions.

Hydrarch and Xerarch Succession

  • Hydrarch: Succession in wet areas (e.g., pond to forest).
  • Xerarch: Succession in dry areas (e.g., bare rock to forest).
  • Both lead to a mesic (medium water condition) climax community.

🎙️ These are two specific types of succession based on the initial water conditions. Interestingly, regardless of whether it starts wet or dry, the ultimate stable community tends to be one that thrives in moderate moisture.

Nutrient Cycling: Recycling Life's Elements

  • Movement of nutrient elements through ecosystem components.
  • Gaseous cycles: Carbon, Nitrogen (reservoirs in atmosphere).
  • Sedimentary cycles: Phosphorus, Sulfur (reservoirs in Earth's crust).

🎙️ Nutrient cycling, also known as biogeochemical cycles, ensures that essential elements like carbon and nitrogen are continuously reused. Understanding their reservoirs is key to comprehending these cycles.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Confusing food chain with food web.
  • Incorrectly drawing inverted pyramids (e.g., energy).
  • Mixing up primary and secondary succession.
  • Not understanding the 10% law of energy transfer.

🎙️ Students often make these errors. Remember, a food web is many interconnected chains. The pyramid of energy is *always* upright. Primary succession starts from scratch, secondary from existing soil. And the 10% law is fundamental to energy flow.

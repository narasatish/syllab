Understanding an Ecosystem

  • A functional unit of nature.
  • Organisms interact with each other and their physical environment.
  • Vary in size from a small pond to a vast forest.

🎙️ An ecosystem is a functional unit where living organisms interact among themselves and with their surrounding physical environment. It can be as small as a pond or as large as an ocean, demonstrating complex interdependencies.

🎙️ Ecosystems are made up of two main interacting components: abiotic, which are the non-living physical and chemical factors, and biotic, which include all living organisms. Their interaction drives all ecosystem processes.

Key Abiotic Factors

  • Light and Temperature: Energy source and metabolic rates.
  • Water: Essential for all life processes.
  • Soil and Nutrients: Support plant growth.
  • pH: Influences enzyme activity and solubility.

🎙️ Abiotic factors like sunlight, temperature, water, soil, and pH critically influence the types of organisms that can thrive in an ecosystem. They determine resource availability and environmental conditions.

Key Biotic Factors

  • Producers: Autotrophs (e.g., plants, algae).
  • Consumers: Heterotrophs (e.g., animals).
  • Decomposers: Saprotrophs (e.g., bacteria, fungi).

🎙️ Biotic components are categorised into producers, consumers, and decomposers. Producers make their own food, consumers feed on others, and decomposers break down dead organic matter, recycling nutrients.

Gross vs. Net Primary Productivity

  • GPP (Gross Primary Productivity): Total photosynthesis rate.
  • NPP (Net Primary Productivity): GPP minus respiration (R).
  • NPP = GPP - R; biomass available to consumers.

🎙️ Gross Primary Productivity (GPP) is the total rate of photosynthesis. Net Primary Productivity (NPP) is GPP minus the energy used by producers for respiration (R), so NPP = GPP - R. NPP is the biomass available to consumers.

Decomposition: Nature's Recycling Process

  • Breakdown of complex organic matter (detritus).
  • By decomposers (bacteria, fungi).
  • Releases simpler inorganic substances.

🎙️ Decomposition is the process by which decomposers break down complex organic matter (detritus) into simpler inorganic substances like CO2, water, and nutrients. It's vital for nutrient cycling.

♻️
Nutrient Recycling

Stages of Decomposition

  • Fragmentation: Detritivores break down detritus.
  • Leaching: Water-soluble nutrients seep into soil.
  • Catabolism: Enzymes break down complex compounds.
  • Humification: Formation of dark, amorphous humus.
  • Mineralisation: Release of inorganic nutrients.

🎙️ Decomposition involves five key steps: fragmentation by detritivores, leaching of water-soluble nutrients, catabolism by bacterial and fungal enzymes, humification forming humus, and mineralisation releasing inorganic nutrients.

Understanding Trophic Levels

  • Position an organism occupies in a food chain.
  • Producers: First trophic level (autotrophs).
  • Primary Consumers: Second level (herbivores).
  • Secondary Consumers: Third level (carnivores eating herbivores).
  • Tertiary Consumers: Fourth level (carnivores eating other carnivores).

🎙️ Trophic levels represent the position an organism occupies in a food chain. Producers form the base, followed by herbivores (primary consumers), carnivores (secondary consumers), and top carnivores (tertiary consumers).

Food Chain vs. Food Web

  • Food Chain: Simple, linear pathway of energy transfer.
  • Food Web: Complex network of interconnected food chains.
  • Food webs provide more stability to an ecosystem.

🎙️ A food chain illustrates a single linear pathway of energy transfer. A food web, however, shows multiple interconnected food chains, representing the complex feeding relationships within an ecosystem.

Pyramid of Energy: Always Upright

  • Represents energy flow at each trophic level.
  • Always upright due to the 10% Law.
  • Energy decreases by ~90% at each successive level.

🎙️ The pyramid of energy is always upright because only about 10% of the energy is transferred from one trophic level to the next (10% Law). The remaining 90% is lost as heat or used for metabolic activities.

Ecological Succession: Ecosystem Change

  • Gradual and predictable change in species composition.
  • Leads to a stable climax community.
  • Can be primary or secondary.

🎙️ Ecological succession is the gradual and predictable change in the species composition of an area over time. It leads to a stable climax community adapted to the local environment.

Types of Ecological Succession

  • Primary Succession: Starts in barren areas (e.g., volcanic rock).
  • Secondary Succession: Occurs in disturbed areas (e.g., after a fire).
  • Pioneer species initiate change, climax community stabilizes.

🎙️ Primary succession occurs in barren areas where no life existed before, like newly cooled lava. Secondary succession occurs in areas where life was present but has been disturbed, like a forest fire.

What is Genetics?

  • Branch of biology studying heredity and variation.
  • Deals with how traits are passed from parents to offspring.
  • Explains similarities and differences within species.

🎙️ Genetics is the core of understanding why we resemble our parents but are not identical copies. It explores the mechanisms and patterns of trait transmission.

Heredity vs. Variation

  • Heredity: Transmission of characters from parents to offspring.
  • Variation: Differences among individuals of the same species.
  • Both are fundamental to evolution and adaptation.

🎙️ Heredity ensures continuity of species, while variation provides the raw material for evolutionary change. Think of a family – everyone shares traits (heredity) but also has unique features (variation).

Gregor Mendel: Father of Genetics

  • Austrian monk, performed experiments on pea plants (1856-1863).
  • Proposed fundamental laws of inheritance.
  • His work was rediscovered in 1900.

🎙️ Mendel's meticulous experiments and mathematical approach were revolutionary. His work laid the groundwork for modern genetics, though it went unrecognized for decades.

Why Pea Plant (Pisum sativum)?

🎙️ Mendel chose the pea plant wisely. Its characteristics made it perfect for studying inheritance patterns, allowing him to observe multiple generations quickly.

Monohybrid Cross: Concept

  • Cross involving a single pair of contrasting characters.
  • Example: Tall plant x Dwarf plant.
  • Used to study the inheritance of one gene at a time.

🎙️ A monohybrid cross is the simplest type of genetic cross, focusing on how one specific trait, like height, is passed down through generations.

Monohybrid Cross: Steps (Tall x Dwarf)

  • 1. Parental (P) generation: Pure Tall (TT) x Pure Dwarf (tt)
  • 2. Gamete formation: T and t
  • 3. F1 generation: All Tall (Tt)
  • 4. F1 self-pollination: Tt x Tt

🎙️ Let's trace the steps. Starting with pure parents, we observe the F1 generation, then self-pollinate F1 to get the F2 generation. This systematic approach reveals inheritance patterns.

Mendel's First Law: Law of Dominance

  • In a pair of contrasting factors (alleles), one is dominant, the other recessive.
  • Dominant character expresses itself in F1 generation.
  • Recessive character is masked in F1, appears in F2.

🎙️ This law explains why, in the F1 generation of a monohybrid cross, only one parental trait is expressed. The dominant allele masks the effect of the recessive allele.

👑
One Trait Rules
10 / 25

Mendel's Second Law: Law of Segregation

  • Alleles for a trait separate during gamete formation.
  • Each gamete receives only one allele.
  • The alleles reunite randomly during fertilization.

🎙️ The Law of Segregation is universal and states that alleles do not blend. They remain discrete units and separate cleanly during gamete formation, ensuring variation.

Monohybrid Cross: F2 Ratios

  • Cross: Tt x Tt (F1 self-cross)
  • F2 Genotypes: TT, Tt, tt
  • F2 Phenotypes: Tall, Dwarf
  • Phenotypic Ratio: 3 Tall : 1 Dwarf; Genotypic Ratio: 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt

🎙️ After an F1 self-cross, the F2 generation shows both dominant and recessive traits. Remember the classic 3:1 phenotypic and 1:2:1 genotypic ratios for monohybrid crosses.

Common Mistake: Phenotypic vs. Genotypic Ratios

  • Phenotypic ratio: Based on observable traits (e.g., 3 Tall : 1 Dwarf).
  • Genotypic ratio: Based on genetic makeup (e.g., 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt).
  • Always specify which ratio is asked in the exam.

🎙️ A frequent error is mixing these two up. Phenotype is what you see, genotype is the underlying genetic code. Understand the difference clearly for accurate answers.

Dihybrid Cross: Concept

  • Cross involving two pairs of contrasting characters.
  • Example: Seed shape (Round/Wrinkled) and Seed colour (Yellow/Green).
  • Studies independent inheritance of two genes.

🎙️ Moving on from one trait, a dihybrid cross allows us to observe how two different traits are inherited simultaneously. This reveals a more complex pattern.

Mendel's Third Law: Law of Independent Assortment

  • When two pairs of traits are combined in a hybrid, segregation of one pair of characters is independent of the other pair.
  • Alleles for different traits assort independently during gamete formation.
  • Leads to new combinations of traits in offspring.

🎙️ This law is a cornerstone of genetics. It explains why traits like seed shape and seed color don't always travel together, leading to diverse offspring combinations.

Dihybrid Cross: F2 Phenotypic Ratio

  • Cross: RrYy x RrYy (F1 self-cross)
  • F2 Phenotypes: Round Yellow, Round Green, Wrinkled Yellow, Wrinkled Green
  • Phenotypic Ratio: 9 : 3 : 3 : 1

🎙️ The 9:3:3:1 phenotypic ratio is characteristic of a dihybrid cross where both genes assort independently. Make sure you can derive this ratio using a Punnett square.

Common Mistake: Incorrect Gamete Formation

  • For RrYy, gametes are RY, Ry, rY, ry (four types).
  • Do not write Rr, Yy as gametes.
  • Each gamete must receive one allele for each gene.

🎙️ This is a critical step in dihybrid crosses. If gametes are formed incorrectly, the entire Punnett square and resulting ratios will be wrong. Practice forming gametes carefully.

Beyond Mendel: Incomplete Dominance

  • F1 hybrid shows an intermediate phenotype.
  • Neither allele is completely dominant.
  • Example: Snapdragon flower color (Red x White -> Pink F1).

🎙️ Not all traits follow strict dominant-recessive patterns. In incomplete dominance, the heterozygote expresses a phenotype that is a blend of the two homozygous parents.

🌸
Blended Traits
21 / 25

Beyond Mendel: Co-dominance

  • Both alleles express themselves fully in the heterozygote.
  • No blending or intermediate phenotype.
  • Example: ABO blood groups (A and B alleles both expressed in AB blood type).

🎙️ Co-dominance is different from incomplete dominance. Here, both alleles contribute equally and distinctly to the phenotype, rather than blending.

🤝
Both Expressed

Beyond Mendel: Multiple Allelism

  • More than two alleles exist for a single gene in a population.
  • An individual can still only have two alleles.
  • Example: ABO blood groups (I^A, I^B, i alleles).

🎙️ While an individual inherits only two alleles for a gene, the population can have many. ABO blood groups are a classic example, involving three alleles for blood type.

Chromosomal Theory of Inheritance

  • Proposed by Sutton and Boveri (1902).
  • Mendel's 'factors' (genes) are located on chromosomes.
  • Homologous chromosomes carry alleles and segregate during meiosis.

🎙️ This theory was a major breakthrough, linking Mendel's abstract factors to physical structures within the cell. It provided a physical basis for the laws of inheritance.

🔬

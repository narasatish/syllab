Fundamental Vocabulary - Part 1

  • Gene: Unit of inheritance, codes for a specific trait
  • Allele: Alternative forms of a gene (e.g., Tall or Dwarf)
  • Homozygous: Having identical alleles for a trait (e.g., TT or tt)

🎙️ Before diving into Mendel's laws, it's crucial to understand these basic terms. Genes are the instructions, and alleles are the different versions of those instructions that an organism can inherit.

Fundamental Vocabulary - Part 2

  • Heterozygous: Having different alleles for a trait (e.g., Tt)
  • Dominant allele: Expresses itself phenotypically even in heterozygous state
  • Recessive allele: Expresses only in homozygous state, masked by dominant allele

🎙️ These terms describe how alleles combine and interact to determine an organism's characteristics. Understanding dominant and recessive relationships is key to predicting inheritance patterns.

Phenotype vs. Genotype

  • Phenotype: Observable physical characteristics (e.g., tall, dwarf, red)
  • Genotype: Genetic constitution of an organism (e.g., TT, Tt, tt)
  • Genotype determines phenotype, but not always directly visible

🎙️ It's vital to distinguish between what an organism looks like (phenotype) and its underlying genetic makeup (genotype). They are related but distinct concepts, crucial for problem-solving.

Mendel's First Law: Law of Dominance

  • In a pair of contrasting traits, one allele is dominant and the other is recessive
  • Dominant allele expresses itself in the F1 (first filial) generation
  • Recessive allele remains masked in the presence of a dominant allele

🎙️ This law explains why, when you cross two pure-breeding parents with contrasting traits, only one trait appears in the first filial (F1) generation. The dominant trait 'wins out'.

👑
Dominant Rule

Monohybrid Cross - Introduction

  • A genetic cross involving a single pair of contrasting characters
  • Example: Crossing a pure tall pea plant with a pure dwarf pea plant
  • Helps study the inheritance pattern of one gene

🎙️ A monohybrid cross simplifies the study by focusing on just one trait at a time. Mendel started with these simple crosses to establish his foundational principles.

Monohybrid and dihybrid crosses demonstrate Mendel's laws through Punnett squares showing

Worked Example: Monohybrid Cross (F1)

  • Parental (P) Generation: TT (Tall) x tt (Dwarf)
  • Gametes produced: T from Tall parent, t from Dwarf parent
  • F1 Generation: All Tt (Heterozygous Tall)

🎙️ Here, we see the first step of a monohybrid cross. All offspring in the F1 generation are heterozygous and express the dominant tall phenotype, illustrating the Law of Dominance.

Worked Example: Monohybrid Cross (F2 Phenotypic Ratio)

  • F1 self-cross: Tt (Tall) x Tt (Tall)
  • F2 Phenotypes: 3 Tall : 1 Dwarf
  • The phenotypic ratio is always 3:1 for complete dominance in F2

🎙️ When F1 individuals self-pollinate, the recessive trait reappears in the F2 generation. This 3:1 phenotypic ratio is characteristic of a monohybrid cross with complete dominance.

Worked Example: Monohybrid Cross (F2 Genotypic Ratio)

  • F1 self-cross: Tt (Tall) x Tt (Tall)
  • F2 Genotypes: 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt
  • The genotypic ratio is always 1:2:1 for complete dominance in F2

🎙️ While the phenotype is 3:1, the underlying genetic makeup shows a different ratio: one homozygous dominant, two heterozygous, and one homozygous recessive. Understand both!

Common Mistake: Confusing Ratios

  • Always distinguish between phenotypic and genotypic ratios
  • Phenotypic ratio (e.g., 3:1) describes observable traits
  • Genotypic ratio (e.g., 1:2:1) describes the genetic makeup

🎙️ This is a very common error in exams. Always read the question carefully to determine if it asks for phenotypic or genotypic ratios. They are distinct and tested frequently.

Mendel's Second Law: Law of Segregation

  • Alleles for a trait separate (segregate) during gamete formation
  • Each gamete receives only one of the two alleles
  • Ensures the purity of gametes (Law of Purity of Gametes)

🎙️ This law states that during meiosis, the two alleles for a character separate from each other, so that each gamete gets only one allele. This separation is random and complete.

✂️
Allele Separation

The Test Cross

  • Cross an individual with unknown genotype (but dominant phenotype) with a homozygous recessive parent
  • Purpose: To determine if the unknown individual is homozygous dominant or heterozygous
  • Results indicate the genotype of the unknown parent

🎙️ A test cross is a powerful tool to determine the genotype of an individual showing a dominant phenotype. It helps us distinguish between TT and Tt by observing the offspring ratios.

❓
Uncovering Genotype

  • Analyzes the simultaneous inheritance of two different genes

🎙️ A dihybrid cross takes us a step further, examining how two different traits are inherited together. It's more complex but reveals another important principle of inheritance.

Mendel's Third Law: Law of Independent Assortment

  • When two pairs of traits are combined in a hybrid, segregation of one pair of characters is independent of the other pair
  • Alleles for different traits sort independently into gametes
  • Leads to new combinations of traits in the offspring

🎙️ This law states that the alleles for different genes segregate independently of one another during gamete formation. This leads to new combinations of traits in the offspring, increasing variation.

🎲
Random Sorting
17 / 25

Worked Example: Dihybrid Cross (F1)

  • Parental (P) Generation: RRYY (Round Yellow) x rryy (Wrinkled Green)
  • Gametes produced: RY from RRYY, ry from rryy
  • F1 Generation: All RrYy (Heterozygous Round Yellow)

🎙️ Similar to the monohybrid cross, the F1 generation of a dihybrid cross shows only the dominant phenotypes for both traits, being heterozygous for both. This confirms the Law of Dominance for two traits.

1Parents (RRYY x rryy)
2Gametes (RY, ry)
3F1 Progeny (RrYy)

Worked Example: Dihybrid Cross (F2 Phenotypic Ratio)

  • F1 self-cross: RrYy x RrYy
  • F2 Phenotypes: 9 Round Yellow : 3 Round Green : 3 Wrinkled Yellow : 1 Wrinkled Green
  • The phenotypic ratio is always 9:3:3:1 for independent assortment in F2

🎙️ The 9:3:3:1 phenotypic ratio is the hallmark of a dihybrid cross where both genes show complete dominance and assort independently. Memorize this ratio, it's frequently tested!

Beyond Mendelian Genetics: Incomplete Dominance

  • F1 hybrid shows an intermediate phenotype, a blend of parental traits
  • Neither allele is completely dominant over the other
  • Example: Flower color in Mirabilis jalapa (snapdragon) - Red x White -> Pink

🎙️ Not all traits follow simple Mendelian dominance. In incomplete dominance, the heterozygous phenotype is a blend of the two homozygous phenotypes, like mixing paint colors.

🌸
Blended Traits
20 / 25

Beyond Mendelian Genetics: Co-dominance

  • Both alleles express themselves fully and simultaneously in the F1 hybrid
  • No blending; both traits are distinctly visible
  • Example: ABO blood groups (IAIB expresses both A and B antigens)

🎙️ In co-dominance, both alleles contribute to the phenotype without blending. A classic example is the AB blood type, where both A and B antigens are present on the red blood cells.

🤝
Both Expressed

Beyond Mendelian Genetics: Multiple Alleles

  • More than two alleles exist for a single gene within a population
  • An individual still possesses only two alleles for that gene
  • Example: ABO blood group system (IA, IB, and i alleles)

🎙️ While an individual carries only two alleles for a gene, a population can have many more. The ABO blood group system is a prime example of multiple allelism, leading to four blood types.

What are Microbes? The Invisible Allies

  • Microscopic organisms: bacteria, fungi, protozoa, viruses.
  • Ubiquitous: found everywhere – soil, water, air, inside us.
  • Diverse forms: spherical, rod-shaped, helical.
  • Mostly beneficial, some pathogenic.

🎙️ Microbes are organisms so small they can only be seen with a microscope. They include bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and even viruses. These tiny entities are truly everywhere, from the deepest oceans to the highest mountains, and even within our own bodies. While some are disease-causing, a vast majority are our invisible allies, performing essential functions.

Microbes in Household Products: Curd Formation

  • Lactic Acid Bacteria (LAB) like Lactobacillus.
  • Convert milk sugar (lactose) into lactic acid.
  • Coagulates and partially digests milk proteins.
  • Increases Vitamin B12 content and checks pathogens.

🎙️ One of the most common applications of microbes in our daily lives is curd formation. Lactic Acid Bacteria, or LAB, are responsible for this transformation. They ferment the lactose in milk, producing lactic acid which coagulates the milk proteins, giving curd its characteristic texture. This process also enhances its nutritional value by increasing Vitamin B12.

Microbes in Household Products: Bread Making

  • Saccharomyces cerevisiae: 'Baker's Yeast'.
  • Ferments sugars in dough, producing CO2.
  • CO2 gas causes dough to rise (puff up).
  • Essential for soft, porous texture of bread.

🎙️ Another delicious example is bread making. The familiar 'puffiness' of bread comes from the action of a specific yeast, Saccharomyces cerevisiae, also known as baker's yeast. This microbe ferments the carbohydrates in the dough, releasing carbon dioxide gas. These gas bubbles get trapped in the dough, causing it to rise and giving bread its light texture.

Microbes in Household Products: Cheese Production

  • Aged cheese varieties involve specific microbes.
  • Swiss cheese: large holes due to Propionibacterium shermanii (CO2).
  • Roquefort cheese: distinctive flavour from Penicillium roqueforti.
  • Microbial action impacts texture, flavour, and aroma.

🎙️ Cheese making is an ancient microbial art. Different types of cheese get their unique characteristics from specific microbes. For instance, the large holes in Swiss cheese are due to the significant amount of carbon dioxide produced by Propionibacterium shermanii. On the other hand, the distinct flavour and blue-green veins in Roquefort cheese are a result of the fungus Penicillium roqueforti.

Microbes in Industrial Products: Fermented Beverages

  • Saccharomyces cerevisiae: 'Brewer's Yeast'.
  • Ferments malted cereals and fruit juices to ethanol.
  • Wine and beer are produced without distillation.
  • Whisky, brandy, rum are distilled alcoholic beverages.

🎙️ Moving to industrial applications, microbes are central to producing alcoholic beverages. Saccharomyces cerevisiae, here known as 'brewer's yeast', ferments sugars present in malted cereals and fruit juices to produce ethanol. Depending on whether distillation is involved, we get different types of beverages like wine and beer (without distillation) or whisky and rum (with distillation).

Microbes in Industrial Products: Antibiotics

  • Chemical substances produced by microbes.
  • Can kill or retard the growth of other microbes (pathogens).
  • First antibiotic: Penicillin, discovered by Alexander Fleming.
  • Penicillin produced by fungus Penicillium notatum.

🎙️ Antibiotics are perhaps the most significant microbial discovery for human health. These are chemicals produced by certain microbes that can inhibit or destroy other disease-causing microbes. The accidental discovery of Penicillin by Alexander Fleming, produced by the fungus Penicillium notatum, revolutionised medicine and saved millions of lives during World War II.

Worked Example: Fleming's Serendipity

  • Fleming observed Penicillium mould contaminating a Staphylococci culture plate.
  • Noticed a clear 'zone of inhibition' around the mould.
  • Mould produced a chemical inhibiting bacterial growth.
  • This chemical was later identified as Penicillin.

🎙️ Let's walk through Fleming's famous discovery. He noticed that a Penicillium mould contaminating his bacterial culture plates created a clear area where no bacteria grew. This 'zone of inhibition' indicated that the mould was producing something inhibitory. This accidental observation, a true serendipitous event, led to the isolation of Penicillin, changing the course of medical history.

Common Mistake: Antibiotic Resistance

  • Improper use of antibiotics leads to antibiotic resistance.
  • Bacteria evolve and become immune to the drug's effects.
  • Always complete the full course of antibiotics.
  • Over-prescription and misuse are major global health concerns.

🎙️ A critical point for exams and real life: the misuse of antibiotics is a serious problem. Not completing the prescribed course or using them unnecessarily can lead to bacteria developing resistance. This means the antibiotics become ineffective against those strains, making infections much harder to treat. Always use antibiotics responsibly and only under medical advice.

Industrial Products: Chemicals, Enzymes & Bioactive Molecules

  • Microbes produce organic acids: Citric acid (Aspergillus niger).
  • Acetic acid (Acetobacter aceti), Butyric acid (Clostridium butylicum).
  • Enzymes: Lipases (detergent formulations), Pectinases & Proteases (juice clarification).
  • Bioactive molecules: Cyclosporin A (immunosuppressant), Statins (cholesterol lowering).

🎙️ Microbes are also invaluable as tiny factories producing a range of chemicals, enzymes, and bioactive molecules. For example, Aspergillus niger produces citric acid, and Acetobacter aceti gives us acetic acid. Enzymes like lipases are used in detergents, while pectinases and proteases clarify fruit juices. Beyond these, microbes also yield powerful bioactive molecules like Cyclosporin A, an immunosuppressant, and Statins, which lower blood cholesterol.

Industrial Products: Bioactive Molecules Deep Dive

  • Streptokinase: produced by Streptococcus, a 'clot buster'.
  • Cyclosporin A: produced by fungus Trichoderma polysporum, immunosuppressant for organ transplants.
  • Statins: produced by yeast Monascus purpureus, lower blood cholesterol by inhibiting enzyme.
  • These molecules have significant therapeutic applications.

🎙️ Let's elaborate on some key bioactive molecules. Streptokinase, from the bacterium Streptococcus, is used to dissolve blood clots in patients who have had a myocardial infarction. Cyclosporin A, from the fungus Trichoderma polysporum, is a crucial immunosuppressant used to prevent rejection in organ transplant patients. And statins, produced by the yeast Monascus purpureus, are widely used to lower blood cholesterol levels by inhibiting a key enzyme involved in cholesterol synthesis. These are high-yield topics for exams.

Microbes in Sewage Treatment: The Challenge

  • Wastewater contains large amounts of organic matter and pathogens.
  • Direct discharge into natural water bodies causes pollution.
  • Sewage Treatment Plants (STPs) use microbes to treat wastewater.
  • Reduces Biological Oxygen Demand (BOD).

🎙️ Now, let's explore how microbes help us manage waste. Urban wastewater, or sewage, contains a high concentration of organic matter and disease-causing microbes. Releasing untreated sewage into rivers or oceans would severely pollute them. Sewage Treatment Plants, or STPs, harness the power of microbes to clean this water, making it safe for discharge. A key indicator of pollution here is BOD.

Wastewater from homes and industries is treated in multiple stages to remove contaminants

Sewage Treatment: Primary Treatment

  • Physical removal of large and small particles.
  • Sequential filtration removes floating debris.
  • Grit (soil and small pebbles) removed by sedimentation.
  • Solids settle to form primary sludge; supernatant is primary effluent.

🎙️ Sewage treatment begins with primary treatment, a physical process. Large debris is removed by filtration, followed by sedimentation to remove grit like soil and small stones. The solids that settle form the primary sludge, while the liquid above is called the primary effluent. This effluent then moves to the next stage.

Sewage Treatment: Secondary Treatment (Biological)

  • Primary effluent pumped into large aeration tanks.
  • Vigorous agitation and air supply encourage aerobic microbial growth.
  • Microbes (flocs) consume organic matter, reducing BOD.
  • Treated effluent is passed into settling tanks, forming activated sludge.

🎙️ Secondary treatment is where microbes truly shine. The primary effluent is transferred to large aeration tanks where it's constantly agitated and supplied with air. This promotes the rapid growth of aerobic microbes, forming 'flocs' – masses of bacteria associated with fungal filaments. These flocs consume the organic matter, significantly reducing the Biological Oxygen Demand (BOD) of the water. Once BOD is reduced, the effluent is moved to settling tanks.

Sewage Treatment: Anaerobic Sludge Digestion

  • Activated sludge (sedimented flocs) pumped into anaerobic digesters.
  • Anaerobic bacteria digest organic matter and flocs.
  • Produce a mixture of gases: methane, H2S, CO2 (biogas).
  • Digested sludge is safely disposed of.

🎙️ The activated sludge from the secondary treatment, which contains a large population of aerobic microbes, is then transferred to anaerobic sludge digesters. Here, anaerobic bacteria break down the organic matter and the flocs themselves. This anaerobic digestion produces a mixture of gases, primarily methane, along with hydrogen sulphide and carbon dioxide, collectively known as biogas. This biogas can be used as an energy source.

Microbes in Biogas Production: The Energy Source

  • Biogas: a mixture of gases (methane, CO2, H2S).
  • Produced by microbial activity on animal dung and agricultural waste.
  • Methanogens (e.g., Methanobacterium) are key anaerobic bacteria.
  • Used as cooking fuel and for lighting in rural areas.

🎙️ Biogas is a renewable energy source generated through the anaerobic digestion of organic waste. Methanogens, a group of anaerobic bacteria like Methanobacterium, are the primary producers of methane, which is the main component of biogas. These bacteria thrive in the guts of cattle and in anaerobic sludge digesters. Biogas is a clean and efficient fuel, widely used in rural India for cooking and lighting.

Microbes as Biocontrol Agents: Natural Pest Control

  • Using biological methods to control plant diseases and pests.
  • Reduces reliance on harmful chemical pesticides and insecticides.
  • Environmentally friendly and sustainable approach.
  • Targets specific pests without harming beneficial organisms.

🎙️ Moving to agriculture, microbes offer a fantastic alternative to chemical pesticides. Biocontrol involves using natural enemies or microbial agents to control pests and diseases. This approach is environmentally friendly, reducing chemical pollution and protecting beneficial insects. It's a sustainable way to manage agricultural pests.

Biocontrol Agents: Key Examples

  • Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt): controls insect larvae (caterpillars).
  • Trichoderma: free-living fungi, effective against various plant pathogens.
  • Baculoviruses (Genus Nucleopolyhedrovirus): species-specific, narrow spectrum insecticidal applications.
  • Ladybird and Dragonflies: control aphids and mosquitoes respectively.

🎙️ Let's look at specific biocontrol agents. Bacillus thuringiensis, or Bt, is widely used to control insect larvae; its spores are ingested by caterpillars, killing them. Trichoderma is a common fungus that protects plant roots against various pathogens. Baculoviruses, particularly the genus Nucleopolyhedrovirus, are excellent for species-specific insect pest management, causing no harm to plants, mammals, or other non-target organisms. Remember, even insects like ladybirds and dragonflies are biocontrol agents.

Microbes as Biofertilisers: Enhancing Soil Fertility

  • Organisms that enrich the nutrient quality of the soil.
  • Reduce the need for chemical fertilisers, promoting organic farming.
  • Main types: bacteria, fungi, cyanobacteria.
  • Enhance plant growth and productivity.

🎙️ Finally, microbes play a crucial role as biofertilisers. These are living organisms that, when applied to seeds, plant surfaces, or soil, colonise the rhizosphere or the interior of the plant and promote growth by increasing the supply or availability of primary nutrients to the host plant. They are a cornerstone of organic farming, reducing our dependence on synthetic chemical fertilisers and improving soil health.

Biofertilisers: Key Examples

  • Bacteria: Rhizobium (symbiotic N-fixer in legumes), Azotobacter & Azospirillum (free-living N-fixers).
  • Fungi: Mycorrhiza (e.g., Glomus) forms symbiotic association with plants for phosphorus uptake.
  • Cyanobacteria (Blue-green algae): Anabaena, Nostoc (autotrophic N-fixers in paddy fields).
  • Each type has specific roles in nutrient cycling.

🎙️ Let's look at the main types of biofertilisers. Bacteria like Rhizobium form symbiotic associations with legumes, fixing atmospheric nitrogen. Free-living bacteria like Azotobacter and Azospirillum also fix nitrogen in the soil. Fungi, particularly mycorrhizal fungi like Glomus, enhance phosphorus uptake by plants. And finally, cyanobacteria, or blue-green algae, like Anabaena and Nostoc, are important nitrogen-fixers, especially in paddy fields. Understanding these specific roles is vital for your exams.

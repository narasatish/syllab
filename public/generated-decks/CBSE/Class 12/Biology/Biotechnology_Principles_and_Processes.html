Understanding Biotechnology

  • Definition: Technology using living organisms or their products.
  • Aims to modify products, plants, animals, or microorganisms.
  • Applications in health, agriculture, and industry.

🎙️ Biotechnology is essentially technology based on biology. It uses living systems and organisms to develop or make products for specific uses, from medicines to better crops.

Two Core Principles of Biotechnology

  • Genetic Engineering: Altering genetic material (DNA/RNA).
  • Bioprocess Engineering: Maintaining sterile conditions for product synthesis.
  • Both are essential for successful biotechnological applications.

🎙️ These two principles are the pillars. Genetic engineering allows us to change an organism's DNA, while bioprocess engineering ensures the desired products are made efficiently without contamination.

Genetic Engineering: The Foundation

  • Technology to alter the chemistry of genetic material (DNA/RNA).
  • Introduces this into host organisms.
  • Changes the phenotype of the host organism.

🎙️ This is the technology that permits us to cut, paste, and move genes. It's about directly manipulating an organism's genes to achieve a desired trait or product.

Constructing Recombinant DNA (rDNA)

  • Desired gene (foreign DNA) is isolated.
  • Vector DNA (e.g., plasmid) is also isolated.
  • Both are cut and joined using DNA ligase to form rDNA.

🎙️ The key idea is combining DNA from two different sources. A piece of foreign DNA, carrying a desired gene, is inserted into a carrier DNA molecule, called a vector.

Origin of Replication (ori)

  • A specific DNA sequence in a vector.
  • Replication of any piece of DNA linked to it initiates here.
  • Controls the copy number of the linked DNA in the host cell.

🎙️ The 'ori' site is crucial for any DNA piece to replicate within a host cell. It dictates how many copies of the recombinant DNA will be made, which is vital for gene cloning.

Cloning Vectors: Delivering Genes

  • DNA molecules that can carry a foreign DNA segment.
  • Plasmids are the most commonly used cloning vectors.
  • Must possess an 'ori' site, selectable marker, and cloning sites.

🎙️ Vectors act like vehicles, transporting our desired gene into the host cell. Plasmids, small circular DNA found in bacteria, are excellent natural vectors due to their independent replication.

Restriction Enzymes: Molecular Scissors

  • Enzymes that cut DNA at specific recognition sequences.
  • These sequences are usually palindromic.
  • Known as endonucleases, they cut within the DNA strand.

🎙️ These enzymes are the backbone of genetic engineering. They allow us to precisely cut DNA at specific sites, creating fragments that can then be joined with other DNA pieces.

✂️

Naming Restriction Enzymes

  • First letter: genus of the prokaryotic cell.
  • Next two letters: species of the prokaryotic cell.
  • Fourth letter: strain of the prokaryotic cell.
  • Roman numeral: order of discovery from that strain.

🎙️ Understanding the naming helps identify the enzyme's origin. For example, EcoRI comes from *Escherichia coli* strain RY13, and it was the first restriction enzyme isolated from this strain.

Types of Cuts: Sticky vs. Blunt Ends

  • Sticky ends: Staggered cuts, producing overhangs. Easily join with complementary sticky ends.
  • Blunt ends: Straight cuts, producing no overhangs. Harder to join with other blunt ends.
  • Sticky ends are preferred for efficient ligation in rDNA technology.

🎙️ Sticky ends are preferred in genetic engineering because their complementary overhangs allow easier and more stable joining with other DNA fragments cut by the same enzyme.

DNA Ligase: The Molecular Glue

  • Enzyme that joins DNA fragments together.
  • Forms phosphodiester bonds between adjacent nucleotides.
  • Essential for creating a stable recombinant DNA molecule.

🎙️ Once restriction enzymes have cut the DNA, DNA ligase acts as the glue. It seals the nicks in the sugar-phosphate backbone, permanently joining the foreign DNA into the vector.

Competent Host: Ready for Transformation

  • Host cells must be made capable of taking up foreign DNA.
  • Treated with divalent cations (e.g., calcium chloride) to increase permeability.
  • Heat shock (brief heating) further facilitates DNA uptake.

🎙️ Bacterial cells don't naturally take up large DNA molecules. We make them 'competent' by treating them with chemicals and temperature changes, which increases their permeability to DNA.

  • Isolation of the genetic material (DNA).

  • Amplification of gene of interest (PCR).
  • Ligation of DNA fragments into a vector.

Isolating Pure DNA

  • Cells are lysed (broken open) to release DNA.
  • RNA is removed using Ribonuclease (RNase).
  • Proteins and other macromolecules are removed using Protease.
  • DNA is precipitated with chilled ethanol, forming fine threads.

🎙️ The first step is always to obtain pure DNA. We use enzymes to break down other cellular components, leaving us with a clean sample of DNA for further manipulation.

🔬

Precision Cutting with Restriction Enzymes

  • The isolated DNA and vector DNA are cut with the same restriction enzyme.
  • This ensures compatible sticky ends for efficient ligation.
  • The reaction is performed under optimal conditions (temperature, buffer).

🎙️ After isolation, the desired gene and the vector DNA are cut using the *same* restriction enzyme. This ensures that the ends are complementary and can be joined together.

Separation and Isolation of DNA Fragments: Gel Electrophoresis

  • Separates DNA fragments based on their size and charge.
  • DNA (negatively charged) moves towards the positive electrode.
  • Agarose gel acts as a sieve; smaller fragments move faster.
  • Fragments are visualized using Ethidium Bromide under UV light.

🎙️ Gel electrophoresis is like a molecular sieve. Smaller DNA fragments move faster and further through the gel, allowing us to separate and then extract our gene of interest. This process is called elution.

Ligation: Joining the Pieces

  • The desired DNA fragment (insert) and the cut vector DNA are mixed.
  • DNA ligase enzyme is added to catalyze the joining.
  • Forms phosphodiester bonds, creating the recombinant DNA molecule.

🎙️ Now we bring the cut gene and the cut vector together. DNA ligase acts to permanently join them, forming the complete recombinant DNA molecule, ready for insertion into a host.

Transformation: Introducing rDNA into Host Cell

  • The recombinant DNA is introduced into a competent host cell.
  • Common methods: heat shock, microinjection, biolistics (gene gun).
  • Host cell takes up the foreign DNA, becoming 'transformed'.

🎙️ This is the critical step where the host cell acquires the new genetic material. The chosen method depends on the host organism and the type of vector used.

Selection of Recombinants

  • Not all host cells successfully take up the recombinant DNA.
  • Selectable markers (e.g., antibiotic resistance genes) on the vector help identify transformed cells.
  • Non-recombinants or non-transformed cells are eliminated.

🎙️ This is like finding a needle in a haystack. Selectable markers on the vector allow us to grow only the cells that have successfully taken up the recombinant DNA, while eliminating others.

Bioreactors: Large-Scale Production

  • Large vessels (typically 100-1000 litres capacity).
  • Provide optimal conditions (temperature, pH, nutrients, oxygen) for cell growth.
  • Used to produce large quantities of the desired protein/product.

🎙️ Once we have a successful recombinant cell, we need to produce the desired protein on a large scale. Bioreactors provide the ideal environment for cells to multiply and synthesize the product efficiently.

Downstream Processing: From Product to Pure

  • Includes filtration, centrifugation, chromatography, etc.
  • Formulation with preservatives and rigorous quality control testing.

🎙️ This is the final stage, ensuring the product is pure, safe, and ready for use. It involves several purification steps and rigorous quality checks before the product can be marketed.

Real-Life Impact: Recombinant Insulin

  • First therapeutic product made by recombinant DNA technology.
  • Gene for human insulin was inserted into *E. coli* bacteria.
  • Enabled mass production of safe, effective insulin for diabetics.
  • Eliminated issues with animal-sourced insulin (allergies).

🎙️ This is a landmark achievement, transforming the lives of millions. Before this, insulin came from animal sources, which often caused allergic reactions. Recombinant human insulin is identical to natural human insulin.

Chapter Recap & Key Takeaways

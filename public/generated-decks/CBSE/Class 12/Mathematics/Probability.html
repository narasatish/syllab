Conditional Probability: The 'Given That' Scenario

  • Probability of an event occurring given that another event has already occurred.
  • Changes the sample space to the event that has occurred.
  • Represented as P(A|B) – Probability of A given B.

🎙️ Imagine you're drawing cards. What's the probability of drawing a King, given that you've already drawn a face card? This 'given that' aspect is the essence of conditional probability. It restricts our universe of possibilities.

Conditional Probability: The Formula

  • P(A|B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B), provided P(B) ≠ 0.
  • P(B|A) = P(A ∩ B) / P(A), provided P(A) ≠ 0.
  • P(A ∩ B) is the probability of both A and B occurring.

🎙️ This formula is fundamental. It tells us that the probability of A happening, given B has happened, is the probability of both A and B happening, divided by the probability of B happening. Remember the condition P(B) cannot be zero.

Worked Example 1: Conditional Probability

  • A family has two children. What is the probability that both children are boys, given that at least one of them is a boy?
  • S = {BB, BG, GB, GG}. Let A = {BB} and B = {BB, BG, GB}.
  • A ∩ B = {BB}. P(A ∩ B) = 1/4. P(B) = 3/4.
  • P(A|B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B) = (1/4) / (3/4) = 1/3.

🎙️ This is a classic problem. Notice how the 'given that' condition effectively reduces our sample space from four possibilities to just three. We are no longer considering the case of two girls.

Common Mistake 1: Confusing P(A|B) and P(B|A)

  • P(A|B) is NOT necessarily equal to P(B|A).
  • The event given as known changes the denominator.
  • Always clearly identify which event is 'given'.

🎙️ This is a very common trap! Students often interchange P(A|B) and P(B|A) without realizing they represent different scenarios. Always read the question carefully to determine which event is the condition.

Properties of Conditional Probability

  • P(S|F) = 1, P(F|F) = 1 (where S is sample space, F is event).
  • P(A'|F) = 1 - P(A|F).
  • If A and B are mutually exclusive, P((A ∪ B)|F) = P(A|F) + P(B|F).

🎙️ These properties are intuitive and follow directly from the definition. For instance, the probability of the entire sample space occurring, given that F has occurred, must be 1, because F is part of S.

Multiplication Theorem on Probability

  • Used to find the probability of the intersection of two events.
  • P(A ∩ B) = P(B)P(A|B) or P(A ∩ B) = P(A)P(B|A).
  • Derived directly from the conditional probability formula.

🎙️ This theorem is simply a rearrangement of the conditional probability formula. It's incredibly useful when you know the conditional probability and want to find the probability of both events happening together.

✖️

Worked Example 2: Multiplication Theorem

  • A bag contains 5 red and 3 black balls. Two balls are drawn without replacement.
  • What is the probability that both balls are red?
  • Let R1 = first ball is red, R2 = second ball is red.
  • P(R1 ∩ R2) = P(R1) * P(R2|R1) = (5/8) * (4/7) = 20/56 = 5/14.

🎙️ In this example, drawing the first red ball changes the total number of balls and the number of red balls remaining, hence the conditional probability for the second draw. This is a classic 'without replacement' problem.

Independent Events: No Influence

  • Two events A and B are independent if the occurrence of one does not affect the probability of the other.
  • Mathematically: P(A|B) = P(A) and P(B|A) = P(B).
  • If independent, P(A ∩ B) = P(A) * P(B).

🎙️ Think of flipping a coin twice. The outcome of the first flip does not influence the outcome of the second flip. These are independent events. The simplified multiplication rule applies here.

Independent Events: Conditions and Properties

  • If A and B are independent, then A and B' are also independent.
  • A' and B are independent, and A' and B' are independent.
  • This property is useful for proving independence of complements.

🎙️ These properties are quite powerful. If two events are independent, then their complements are also independent. This often simplifies calculations in problems involving 'at least one' or 'neither' scenarios.

Worked Example 3: Checking Independence

  • A die is thrown. Let A be the event 'an even number appears' and B be the event 'a number greater than 4 appears'.
  • P(A) = 3/6 = 1/2. P(B) = 2/6 = 1/3.
  • A ∩ B = {6}. P(A ∩ B) = 1/6.
  • Since P(A)P(B) = (1/2)(1/3) = 1/6 = P(A ∩ B), events A and B are independent.

🎙️ To check for independence, always calculate P(A), P(B), and P(A ∩ B). If P(A ∩ B) equals the product of P(A) and P(B), then they are independent. Otherwise, they are dependent.

Common Mistake 2: Independent vs. Mutually Exclusive

  • Independent: Occurrence of one does NOT affect the other. P(A ∩ B) = P(A)P(B).
  • Mutually Exclusive: Occurrence of one PREVENTS the other. P(A ∩ B) = 0.
  • These are distinct concepts; an event cannot generally be both (unless P(A) or P(B) is 0).

🎙️ This is another crucial distinction. Mutually exclusive events cannot happen at the same time, while independent events simply don't influence each other. Don't confuse them during problem-solving.

Quick Check 1: Conditional Probability

  • If P(A) = 0.6, P(B) = 0.3, and P(A ∩ B) = 0.2, what is P(A|B)?
  • P(A|B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B) = 0.2 / 0.3 = 2/3.
  • Answer: Approximately 0.66.

🎙️ Take a moment to apply the conditional probability formula we just learned. The correct calculation is P(A|B) = 0.2 / 0.3, which is 2/3 or approximately 0.66.

Quick Check 2: Independent Events

  • True or False: If two events A and B are independent, they must also be mutually exclusive.
  • Answer: False.

Bayes' Theorem: Reversing the Condition

  • Used when we know P(A|E) but want to find P(E|A).
  • Often involves 'prior' probabilities and 'posterior' probabilities.
  • Crucial for problems involving cause and effect, or diagnosis.

🎙️ Bayes' Theorem is a powerful tool that allows us to update our beliefs about an event based on new evidence. It's about 'reversing' the conditional probability – finding the probability of the cause given the effect.

🔄
Reversing Roles

Bayes' Theorem: The Formula

  • Let E₁, E₂, ..., E_n be a partition of the sample space.
  • Let A be any event with P(A) ≠ 0.
  • P(E_i|A) = [P(E_i)P(A|E_i)] / Σ[P(E_j)P(A|E_j)] for j=1 to n.
  • Denominator is P(A) using Total Probability Theorem.

🎙️ This formula might look daunting, but it's logical. The numerator is the probability of the specific cause and effect occurring together. The denominator is the total probability of the effect occurring across all possible causes.

1Prior P(E_i)
2Likelihood P(A|E_i)
3Evidence P(A)
4Posterior P(E_i|A)

Worked Example 4 (Part 1): Bayes' Theorem Setup

  • Bag 1: 2 red, 3 black balls. Bag 2: 4 red, 1 black ball.
  • A bag is chosen at random, and a red ball is drawn.
  • What is the probability that the ball came from Bag 1?
  • E1 = Bag 1 chosen, E2 = Bag 2 chosen. A = Red ball drawn.

🎙️ First, we define our events. E1 and E2 form a partition because we must choose either Bag 1 or Bag 2. A is the event we observed. We want to find P(E1|A).

👜👜

Worked Example 4 (Part 2): Calculating Probabilities

  • P(E1) = 1/2, P(E2) = 1/2 (since a bag is chosen at random).
  • P(A|E1) = 2/5 (prob of red from Bag 1).
  • P(A|E2) = 4/5 (prob of red from Bag 2).
  • P(A) = P(A|E1)P(E1) + P(A|E2)P(E2) = (2/5)(1/2) + (4/5)(1/2) = 1/5 + 2/5 = 3/5.

🎙️ Here, we calculate the prior probabilities of choosing each bag and the likelihoods of drawing a red ball from each bag. Then, we use the Total Probability Theorem to find the overall probability of drawing a red ball.

➕
Summing Probabilities
20 / 25

Worked Example 4 (Part 3): Applying Bayes' Formula

  • P(E1|A) = [P(E1)P(A|E1)] / P(A).
  • P(E1|A) = [(1/2)(2/5)] / (3/5).
  • P(E1|A) = (1/5) / (3/5) = 1/3.
  • So, the probability the red ball came from Bag 1 is 1/3.

🎙️ Finally, we plug all our calculated values into Bayes' formula. This gives us the 'posterior' probability – the updated probability that the ball came from Bag 1, now that we know a red ball was drawn.

Random Variables: Quantifying Outcomes

  • A random variable (X) is a real-valued function whose domain is the sample space of a random experiment.
  • It assigns a numerical value to each outcome.
  • Can be discrete (countable values) or continuous (any value in an interval).

🎙️ Until now, events were descriptive. Random variables allow us to attach a numerical value to the outcomes of an experiment, making them amenable to mathematical analysis. For Class 12, we primarily focus on discrete random variables.

Probability Distribution of a Random Variable

  • A table or function that gives the probabilities for each possible value of a discrete random variable.
  • Requirements: P(X=x_i) ≥ 0 for all x_i, and ΣP(X=x_i) = 1.
  • Example: Number of heads in two coin tosses (X=0,1,2).

🎙️ The probability distribution is essentially a map that tells us how likely each possible value of our random variable is. The sum of all these probabilities must always be 1, covering all possible outcomes.

Mean (Expectation) of a Random Variable

  • The average value of a random variable over many trials.
  • Also known as the Expected Value, E(X) or μ (mu).
  • E(X) = Σ [x_i * P(X=x_i)].
  • Represents the 'center' of the probability distribution.

🎙️ The expectation is what you would 'expect' to get, on average, if you repeated the experiment many times. It's a weighted average, where each value is weighted by its probability of occurrence.

Variance of a Random Variable: Spread

  • Measures the spread or dispersion of the random variable's values around its mean.
  • Var(X) = E(X²) - [E(X)]².
  • E(X²) = Σ [x_i² * P(X=x_i)].
  • Standard Deviation (σ) = √Var(X).

🎙️ While the mean tells us the center, the variance tells us how much the values typically deviate from that center. A higher variance means greater spread. The standard deviation is often preferred as it's in the same units as X.

Recap & Exam Tips for Probability

  • Conditional Probability: P(A|B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B).
  • Multiplication Theorem: P(A ∩ B) = P(A)P(B|A).
  • Independent Events: P(A ∩ B) = P(A)P(B).
  • Bayes' Theorem: P(E_i|A) = [P(E_i)P(A|E_i)] / P(A).
  • Random Variables: E(X) = Σx_i P(X=x_i), Var(X) = E(X²) - [E(X)]².

Revisiting Basics: Sample Space & Events

  • Sample Space (S): Set of all possible outcomes of an experiment.
  • Event (E): A subset of the sample space.
  • P(E) = (Number of favorable outcomes) / (Total number of outcomes).
  • 0 ≤ P(E) ≤ 1 for any event E.

🎙️ Before we jump into new topics, let's quickly recap what you learned in Class 11. Remember, probability is fundamentally about quantifying uncertainty. The sample space lists every possible result, and an event is a specific collection of those results.

Conditional Probability: The 'Given That' Scenario

  • Probability of an event occurring, given that another event has already occurred.
  • It narrows down the sample space to the outcomes where the given event happened.
  • Denoted as P(A|B), read as 'Probability of A given B'.

🎙️ Conditional probability is a core concept for Class 12. Think of it as adjusting our probability calculation based on new information. When we say 'given that', we are effectively changing our universe of possible outcomes.

The Formula: P(A|B)

  • P(A|B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B), provided P(B) ≠ 0.
  • P(A ∩ B) is the probability of both A and B occurring.
  • The denominator P(B) acts as the new reduced sample space.

🎙️ This formula is fundamental. Always remember that the probability of the 'given' event, P(B), must not be zero. If P(B) is zero, it means event B can never happen, so it cannot be 'given'.

1Identify events A and B
2Find P(A ∩ B)
3Find P(B)
4Calculate P(A ∩ B) / P(B)

Worked Example 1: Drawing Cards

  • A card is drawn from a well-shuffled deck of 52 cards.
  • Event A: The card drawn is a King.
  • Event B: The card drawn is a Face Card.
  • Find P(A|B).

🎙️ Let's apply the formula to a classic card problem. First, identify the total number of Kings and Face Cards. Then, find the intersection – how many cards are both a King AND a Face Card. This will lead us to the conditional probability.

🃏🃏🃏
Cards from a Deck
6 / 30

Solution: Example 1 (Cards)

  • P(A ∩ B) = P(King and Face Card) = P(King) = 4/52.
  • P(B) = P(Face Card) = 12/52 (4 Kings, 4 Queens, 4 Jacks).
  • P(A|B) = (4/52) / (12/52) = 4/12 = 1/3.
  • Alternatively, out of 12 face cards, 4 are Kings.

🎙️ Notice how the conditional probability is higher than the simple probability of drawing a King (4/52). This is because knowing it's a face card reduces our sample space, making a King more likely within that reduced set.

Avoid These Errors in Conditional Probability

  • Confusing P(A|B) with P(B|A). They are generally not equal.
  • Using P(A) or P(B) in the numerator instead of P(A ∩ B).
  • Forgetting that P(B) must be non-zero in the denominator.
  • Not clearly defining events A and B before calculation.

🎙️ Students often swap A and B in the conditional probability formula. Remember, the 'given' event is always in the denominator. Clearly label your events to avoid confusion, especially in complex problems.

Multiplication Theorem on Probability: P(A ∩ B)

  • Rearrangement of the conditional probability formula.
  • P(A ∩ B) = P(B) * P(A|B) or P(A ∩ B) = P(A) * P(B|A).
  • Used to find the probability of two events both occurring.
  • Especially useful when events are dependent.

🎙️ The multiplication theorem is essentially the conditional probability formula rearranged. It allows us to calculate the probability of the intersection of two events, which means both events happening, by considering their dependence.

Applying the Multiplication Theorem

  • Step 1: Define the two events whose joint probability is needed.
  • Step 2: Calculate the probability of the first event.
  • Step 3: Calculate the conditional probability of the second event, given the first.
  • Step 4: Multiply these two probabilities.

🎙️ This theorem is very practical for 'without replacement' scenarios, where the outcome of the first draw affects the probabilities for the second draw. Follow these steps carefully to ensure accuracy.

1Define events
2Find P(First)
3Find P(Second | First)
4Multiply: P(First) * P(Second | First)

Worked Example 2: Balls in Bags

  • Bag 1: 4 red, 3 black balls. Bag 2: 3 red, 5 black balls.
  • A ball is drawn from Bag 1 and transferred to Bag 2.
  • Then, a ball is drawn from Bag 2.
  • What is the probability that the ball drawn from Bag 2 is red?

🎙️ This problem involves sequential events and dependence. The composition of Bag 2 changes based on the ball transferred from Bag 1. We need to consider two cases for the transfer: a red ball or a black ball.

🔴🔴
Two Bags of Balls
11 / 30

Solution: Example 2 (Balls in Bags)

  • Let R1 be Red from Bag 1, B1 be Black from Bag 1.
  • Let R2 be Red from Bag 2.
  • P(R2) = P(R1 ∩ R2) + P(B1 ∩ R2).
  • P(R2) = P(R1)P(R2|R1) + P(B1)P(R2|B1) = (4/7)*(4/9) + (3/7)*(3/9) = 16/63 + 9/63 = 25/63.

🎙️ This example beautifully demonstrates how the multiplication theorem is used in conjunction with the law of total probability. We consider all possible ways to reach the desired outcome and sum their probabilities.

Independent Events: No Influence

  • Two events A and B are independent if the occurrence of one does not affect the probability of the other.
  • Mathematically: P(A|B) = P(A) and P(B|A) = P(B).
  • This means the conditional probability is just the simple probability.
  • Examples: Tossing two coins, rolling two dice.

🎙️ Independent events are much simpler to deal with because you don't need to consider how one event impacts the other. If knowing B happened doesn't change the likelihood of A, then A and B are independent.

Condition for Independence: P(A ∩ B) = P(A)P(B)

  • This is the most common and useful test for independence.
  • If A and B are independent, then P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B).
  • This is derived directly from the multiplication theorem.
  • Converse is also true: if P(A ∩ B) = P(A)P(B), then A and B are independent.

🎙️ This formula is your go-to for checking independence. Remember, this applies *only* if they are independent. If they are dependent, you must use the conditional probability form of the multiplication theorem.

Worked Example 3: Independent Events (Coin Tosses)

  • A fair coin is tossed twice.
  • Event A: First toss is a Head.
  • Event B: Second toss is a Head.
  • Are A and B independent?

🎙️ Consider the sample space for two coin tosses. Calculate the individual probabilities of A and B, and the probability of their intersection. Then, use the independence condition to verify.

🪙🪙
Two Coin Tosses
15 / 30

Solution: Example 3 (Coin Tosses)

  • Sample space S = {HH, HT, TH, TT}.
  • P(A) = P(First is H) = P({HH, HT}) = 2/4 = 1/2.
  • P(B) = P(Second is H) = P({HH, TH}) = 2/4 = 1/2.
  • P(A ∩ B) = P(Both are H) = P({HH}) = 1/4.
  • Since P(A)P(B) = (1/2)*(1/2) = 1/4 = P(A ∩ B), events A and B are independent.

🎙️ This example confirms our intuition: the outcome of one coin toss does not affect the outcome of the next. This is a classic example of independent events. Make sure to clearly write out your sample space and events for clarity.

Bayes' Theorem: Updating Beliefs with New Evidence

  • Used to calculate conditional probability when prior probabilities are known.
  • Allows us to update the probability of an event based on new information.
  • Crucial in fields like medical diagnosis, machine learning, and finance.
  • Often involves 'inverse probability': P(Cause|Effect).

🎙️ Bayes' Theorem is a powerful tool that helps us reverse the conditional probability. Instead of P(Effect|Cause), we can find P(Cause|Effect). It's about how new evidence changes our initial belief about something.

🔄

The Bayes' Theorem Formula

  • P(E₁|A) = [P(E₁)P(A|E₁)] / Σ [P(Eᵢ)P(A|Eᵢ)]
  • E₁, E₂, ..., Eₙ are mutually exclusive and exhaustive events (partitions of sample space).
  • A is any event that occurs with Eᵢ.
  • The denominator is P(A) by the Law of Total Probability.

🎙️ This formula might look complex, but it's very systematic. The key is to correctly identify your 'causes' (Eᵢ) and the 'effect' (A). The denominator sums up the probability of event A happening through each of the possible causes.

1Identify partitions (Eᵢ)
2Identify event A
3Find P(Eᵢ) and P(A|Eᵢ)
4Apply formula

Worked Example 4: Medical Diagnosis

  • Disease D affects 1% of the population.
  • Test T is 90% accurate (P(T|D)=0.9) and 9% false positive (P(T|D')=0.09).
  • If a person tests positive (T), what is the probability they actually have the disease (D)? Find P(D|T).

🎙️ This is a classic real-world application of Bayes' Theorem. We want to find the probability of having the disease given a positive test, which is often surprisingly different from the test's accuracy itself. Let D' be 'not having the disease'.

🩺
Medical Test
20 / 30

Solution: Example 4 (Medical Diagnosis)

  • P(D) = 0.01, P(D') = 0.99.
  • P(T|D) = 0.90, P(T|D') = 0.09.
  • P(D|T) = [P(D)P(T|D)] / [P(D)P(T|D) + P(D')P(T|D')]
  • P(D|T) = [0.01 * 0.90] / [0.01 * 0.90 + 0.99 * 0.09] = 0.009 / (0.009 + 0.0891) = 0.009 / 0.0981 ≈ 0.0917.

🎙️ Even with a 90% accurate test, the probability of actually having the disease given a positive result is only about 9.17%! This highlights the importance of prior probability and false positives, especially for rare diseases.

Random Variables: Quantifying Outcomes

  • A random variable (X) is a real-valued function whose domain is the sample space of a random experiment.
  • It assigns a numerical value to each outcome of the experiment.
  • Examples: Number of heads in coin tosses, sum of two dice, height of a student.

🎙️ Until now, we've talked about events. A random variable takes those qualitative outcomes and turns them into numbers, which makes them easier to work with mathematically. It's a fundamental concept for probability distributions.

Discrete vs. Continuous Random Variables

  • Discrete RV: Takes on a finite or countably infinite number of values (e.g., number of heads, number of defects).
  • Continuous RV: Takes on any value within a given interval (e.g., height, weight, time).
  • Class 12 CBSE focuses primarily on Discrete Random Variables.

🎙️ Understanding the difference is crucial. Discrete variables are typically counts, while continuous variables are measurements. For our syllabus, we'll mainly deal with discrete random variables and their distributions.

Probability Distribution of a Random Variable

  • A table or formula that lists all possible values a discrete random variable can take.
  • And their corresponding probabilities (P(X=x)).
  • Properties: 0 ≤ P(X=x) ≤ 1 for all x, and ΣP(X=x) = 1.

🎙️ This distribution tells us exactly how the probability is 'spread out' among the possible values of the random variable. It's like a complete picture of the chances for each outcome.

Mean (Expectation) & Variance of a Random Variable

  • Mean (E[X]): The average value of the random variable over many trials. E[X] = ΣxᵢP(X=xᵢ).
  • Variance (Var[X]): Measures the spread or dispersion of the distribution around the mean. Var[X] = E[X²] - (E[X])².
  • Standard Deviation (SD[X]) = √Var[X].

🎙️ The mean gives us the central tendency, what we expect on average. Variance tells us how much the values typically deviate from that average. These are key descriptive statistics for a probability distribution.

Bernoulli Trials & Binomial Distribution

  • Bernoulli Trial: A random experiment with only two possible outcomes, 'success' (p) or 'failure' (q=1-p).
  • Binomial Distribution: Applies to the number of successes in a fixed number (n) of independent Bernoulli trials.
  • Assumptions: Fixed number of trials, independent trials, only two outcomes, constant probability of success.

🎙️ Bernoulli trials are the building blocks of the binomial distribution. Think of any situation where you repeat a simple 'yes/no' experiment multiple times, like flipping a coin repeatedly or checking items on an assembly line for defects.

The Binomial Distribution Formula

  • P(X=k) = C(n, k) * pᵏ * qⁿ⁻ᵏ
  • n: number of trials, k: number of successes (0 ≤ k ≤ n).
  • p: probability of success in a single trial.
  • q: probability of failure in a single trial (q = 1 - p).
  • C(n, k): Binomial coefficient, 'n choose k'.

🎙️ This formula allows us to calculate the exact probability of getting 'k' successes in 'n' trials. It combines combinations (for the number of ways to get k successes) with the probabilities of success and failure.

1Identify n, k, p, q
2Calculate C(n, k)
3Substitute into formula
4Compute P(X=k)

Worked Example 5: Binomial Distribution (Dice Roll)

  • A die is thrown 5 times.
  • Getting an even number is considered a 'success'.
  • Find the probability of getting exactly 3 successes.

🎙️ Let's break this down. What are 'n', 'k', 'p', and 'q' in this scenario? An even number on a die roll is 2, 4, or 6. Identify these parameters first, then apply the binomial formula.

🎲🎲🎲🎲🎲
Five Dice Rolls
28 / 30

Solution: Example 5 (Dice Roll)

  • n = 5 (number of trials).
  • k = 3 (number of successes).
  • p = P(even number) = 3/6 = 1/2.
  • q = 1 - p = 1/2.
  • P(X=3) = C(5, 3) * (1/2)³ * (1/2)² = 10 * (1/8) * (1/4) = 10/32 = 5/16.

🎙️ The probability of getting exactly three even numbers in five rolls is 5/16. This demonstrates how the binomial distribution can model repeated independent trials with two outcomes.

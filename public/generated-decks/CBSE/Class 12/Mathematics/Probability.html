Recap: Basics of Probability

  • Random Experiment: Outcome cannot be predicted.
  • Sample Space (S): Set of all possible outcomes.
  • Event (E): A subset of the sample space.
  • P(E) = n(E) / n(S): Favorable outcomes / Total outcomes.

🎙️ Let's quickly refresh our Class 11 concepts. Remember, probability deals with experiments where outcomes aren't certain, but we know all possibilities. The probability of an event is simply the ratio of desired outcomes to the total.

Defining Conditional Probability P(A|B)

  • P(A|B) is the probability of event A occurring, given that event B has already occurred.
  • The sample space effectively reduces to event B.
  • Formula: P(A|B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B), where P(B) > 0.

🎙️ This formula is fundamental. The numerator is the probability of both A and B happening, and the denominator is the probability of the condition B happening. It's like finding the proportion of B that also contains A.

1Identify Events A & B
2Find P(A ∩ B)
3Find P(B)
4Calculate P(A ∩ B) / P(B)

Multiplication Theorem of Probability

  • Derived directly from conditional probability.
  • P(A ∩ B) = P(A) * P(B|A)
  • Also, P(A ∩ B) = P(B) * P(A|B)
  • Used when events occur in sequence or both must happen.

🎙️ This theorem is crucial when you want to find the probability of two events both occurring. If you know the probability of the first event and the conditional probability of the second given the first, you can multiply them.

Worked Example 2: Drawing Balls

  • A bag contains 5 red and 3 black balls.
  • Two balls are drawn one after the other without replacement.
  • What is the probability that both balls drawn are red?

🎙️ Let R1 be the event of drawing a red ball first, and R2 be the event of drawing a red ball second. We need P(R1 ∩ R2). P(R1) is 5/8. After R1 occurs, there are 4 red balls left and 7 total balls, so P(R2|R1) is 4/7.

Defining Independent Events

  • Two events A and B are independent if P(A|B) = P(A) or P(B|A) = P(B).
  • This simplifies the multiplication theorem to: P(A ∩ B) = P(A) * P(B).
  • This condition is used to *check* for independence.

🎙️ The key takeaway for independent events is that the conditional probability of one event, given the other, is just the marginal probability of that event. This leads to the simpler product rule for their intersection.

Dependent (P(A|B) ≠ P(A))
Independent (P(A|B) = P(A))

Defining Total Probability Theorem

  • If E1, E2, ..., En are a partition of the sample space (mutually exclusive and exhaustive events).
  • And A is any event.
  • Then P(A) = P(E1)P(A|E1) + P(E2)P(A|E2) + ... + P(En)P(A|En).

🎙️ A partition means the events cover the entire sample space without overlapping. This formula essentially says that the total probability of A is the sum of probabilities of A occurring through each possible pathway or condition.

1Partition Sample Space (Eᵢ)
2Find P(Eᵢ) for each partition
3Find P(A|Eᵢ) for each partition
4Sum P(Eᵢ) * P(A|Eᵢ)

Bayes' Theorem: Reversing the Cause

  • You have symptoms (Effect). What's the probability of having a certain disease (Cause)?
  • Bayes' Theorem helps us find P(Cause|Effect).
  • It 'reverses' the conditional probability.

🎙️ Bayes' Theorem is incredibly powerful for diagnostic reasoning. Often, we know P(Effect|Cause), but we want to know P(Cause|Effect). This theorem provides the mathematical bridge to flip that conditional probability.

Defining Bayes' Theorem

  • If E1, E2, ..., En are a partition of the sample space.
  • And A is any event with P(A) > 0.
  • Then P(Ei|A) = [P(Ei) * P(A|Ei)] / Σ [P(Ej) * P(A|Ej)] for j=1 to n.

🎙️ The numerator is the probability of the specific 'cause' (Ei) leading to the 'effect' (A). The denominator is the total probability of the 'effect' (A) occurring through any of the possible causes, which is simply the Total Probability Theorem.

1Identify Partitions Eᵢ and Event A
2Calculate P(Eᵢ) * P(A|Eᵢ) for desired Eᵢ
3Calculate Total Probability P(A) = Σ P(Ej)P(A|Ej)
4Divide: [P(Eᵢ)P(A|Eᵢ)] / P(A)

Mean (Expectation) of a Random Variable

  • The 'expected value' or 'average' outcome of a random variable.
  • Denoted as E(X) or μ (mu).
  • Formula: E(X) = Σ [x_i * P(X=x_i)].

🎙️ The mean of a random variable tells us what value we would expect, on average, if we were to repeat the random experiment many times. It's a weighted average, where each value is weighted by its probability.

Bernoulli Trials & Binomial Distribution

  • Bernoulli Trial: An experiment with only two outcomes (success/failure).
  • Binomial Distribution: Describes the number of successes in a fixed number of independent Bernoulli trials.
  • Assumptions: Fixed 'n' trials, independent trials, constant 'p' (prob. of success).

🎙️ Think of a free throw in basketball: either it goes in (success) or it doesn't (failure). If you take 10 free throws, the binomial distribution tells you the probability of making exactly 7 shots, for example.

✅❌
Success or Failure

Binomial Distribution Formula

  • P(X=k) = nCk * p^k * q^(n-k)
  • Where: n = number of trials
  • k = number of successes (0, 1, ..., n)
  • p = probability of success in a single trial
  • q = 1 - p = probability of failure in a single trial

🎙️ This formula is your key to solving binomial distribution problems. 'nCk' accounts for the number of ways to get 'k' successes in 'n' trials, and 'p^k * q^(n-k)' is the probability of one specific sequence of 'k' successes and 'n-k' failures.

1Identify n, k, p, q
2Calculate nCk
3Calculate p^k
4Calculate q^(n-k)
5Multiply all terms

Common Mistakes in Probability

  • Confusing independent and mutually exclusive events.
  • Incorrectly applying conditional probability vs. intersection.
  • Errors in identifying n, k, p for Binomial distribution.
  • Calculation errors, especially with fractions and large numbers.

🎙️ Students often mix up 'mutually exclusive' (cannot happen together, P(A ∩ B) = 0) with 'independent' (occurrence of one doesn't affect other, P(A ∩ B) = P(A)P(B)). Always read the problem carefully to correctly identify the type of events and distribution.

Concept 1: Rate of Change of Quantities

  • Think of a car's speedometer: it shows how fast distance changes with time.
  • Similarly, derivatives measure how one quantity changes with respect to another.
  • It's the instantaneous rate of change.

🎙️ Just like a speedometer tells you your speed at any given moment, derivatives help us find the 'speed' or 'rate' at which one variable changes with respect to another. It's a fundamental concept that connects calculus to the physical world.

🚗
Speedometer: Rate of Change

Formal Definition: Rate of Change

  • If y = f(x), then dy/dx represents the rate of change of y with respect to x.
  • If y changes with respect to time (t), we use dy/dt.
  • Units are crucial: e.g., cm³/s, m/s, ₹/unit.

🎙️ Mathematically, dy/dx is the derivative of y with respect to x. It's a powerful tool to quantify change. Always pay attention to the units; they tell you exactly what rate is being measured.

Worked Example 1: Area of a Circle

  • Problem: Find the rate at which the area of a circle changes with respect to its radius (r) when r = 5 cm.
  • Step 1: Write down the formula for the area (A) of a circle.
  • Step 2: Differentiate A with respect to r.
  • Step 3: Substitute the given value of r.

🎙️ Let's apply this. The area of a circle is A = πr². We need to find dA/dr. Differentiating gives 2πr. Now, simply plug in the value of r to get the specific rate of change.

1A = πr²
2dA/dr = 2πr
3Substitute r=5
7 / 33

Solution: Area of a Circle

  • Given A = πr².
  • Differentiating w.r.t. r: dA/dr = d/dr (πr²) = 2πr.
  • When r = 5 cm, dA/dr = 2π(5) = 10π cm²/cm.
  • Conclusion: The area is changing at 10π cm² per cm increase in radius.

🎙️ So, for every unit increase in the radius, the area changes by 10π square centimeters at that instant. This shows the exact rate of change at a specific point, not an average rate.

Concept 2: Increasing and Decreasing Functions

  • Imagine climbing a hill (increasing) or walking downhill (decreasing).
  • A function is increasing if its graph goes up as you move from left to right.
  • A function is decreasing if its graph goes down.

🎙️ This concept is intuitive. When you climb a hill, your height increases. When you descend, your height decreases. Derivatives help us mathematically determine these 'slopes' of a function's graph.

Using Derivatives for Inc/Dec Functions

  • If f'(x) > 0 for all x in an interval, f(x) is strictly increasing in that interval.
  • If f'(x) < 0 for all x in an interval, f(x) is strictly decreasing in that interval.
  • If f'(x) = 0, the function is constant or at a turning point.

🎙️ The sign of the first derivative, f'(x), tells us everything! A positive derivative means an upward slope, hence increasing. A negative derivative means a downward slope, hence decreasing. This is a core idea.

f'(x) > 0
f'(x) < 0

Worked Example 2: Inc/Dec Function

  • Problem: Find the intervals in which f(x) = x² - 4x + 6 is strictly increasing or decreasing.
  • Step 1: Find the first derivative, f'(x).
  • Step 2: Set f'(x) = 0 to find critical points (where slope is zero).
  • Step 3: Test intervals using values to check the sign of f'(x).

🎙️ Let's apply the steps. First, differentiate the given function. Then, find the point where the derivative is zero. This point divides the number line into intervals, which we then test to see the function's behavior.

1f'(x) = 2x-4
22x-4 = 0 => x=2
3Test intervals
12 / 33

Solution: Inc/Dec Function

  • Given f(x) = x² - 4x + 6. Then f'(x) = 2x - 4.
  • Set f'(x) = 0 => 2x - 4 = 0 => x = 2.
  • Interval 1: (-∞, 2). Choose x=0, f'(0) = -4 < 0. So, f(x) is strictly decreasing.
  • Interval 2: (2, ∞). Choose x=3, f'(3) = 2 > 0. So, f(x) is strictly increasing.

🎙️ We found that x=2 is the critical point. For x values less than 2, the derivative is negative, meaning the function is decreasing. For x values greater than 2, the derivative is positive, meaning the function is increasing.

Concept 3: Tangents and Normals

  • Imagine a car turning a corner: the tangent is the direction it's heading at that exact point.
  • A normal is a line perpendicular to the tangent at the point of contact.
  • Derivatives give us the slope of the tangent at any point on a curve.

🎙️ Tangents and normals are crucial geometric applications. Think of a smooth curve. A tangent 'just touches' the curve at one point, showing its instantaneous direction. The normal is like the perpendicular path from that point.

📏
Curve with a line
15 / 33

Equation of a Tangent

  • If y = f(x), the slope of the tangent at a point (x₀, y₀) is dy/dx evaluated at (x₀, y₀).
  • Let this slope be 'm'.
  • The equation of the tangent line is: (y - y₀) = m(x - x₀).

🎙️ Remember the point-slope form of a straight line from Class 11? Here, the 'slope' is given by the derivative evaluated at the specific point where the tangent touches the curve. It's a direct application.

➡️
Direction of Tangent
16 / 33

Equation of a Normal

  • The normal is perpendicular to the tangent at the point of contact.
  • If 'm' is the slope of the tangent, the slope of the normal is -1/m (provided m ≠ 0).
  • The equation of the normal line is: (y - y₀) = (-1/m)(x - x₀).

🎙️ Since the normal is perpendicular, its slope is the negative reciprocal of the tangent's slope. Be careful with cases where the tangent is horizontal (m=0) or vertical (m undefined); the normal will be vertical or horizontal, respectively.

Worked Example 3: Finding Tangent

  • Problem: Find the equation of the tangent to the curve y = x³ - 3x + 2 at the point (2, 4).
  • Step 1: Find dy/dx.
  • Step 2: Calculate the slope 'm' at the given point (x₀, y₀).
  • Step 3: Use the point-slope form to write the equation.

🎙️ Let's walk through this. First, differentiate the function to get the general slope expression. Then, substitute the x-coordinate of the given point to find the specific slope of the tangent. Finally, use the formula.

1dy/dx = 3x²-3
2m = 3(2)²-3 = 9
3y-4 = 9(x-2)
18 / 33

Solution: Finding Tangent

  • Given y = x³ - 3x + 2. dy/dx = 3x² - 3.
  • At (2, 4), slope m = 3(2)² - 3 = 3(4) - 3 = 12 - 3 = 9.
  • Equation of tangent: (y - 4) = 9(x - 2).
  • y - 4 = 9x - 18 => 9x - y - 14 = 0.

🎙️ The final equation of the tangent line is 9x - y - 14 = 0. Always simplify your equation to the standard form ax + by + c = 0 for clarity and consistency in exams.

Concept 4: Approximations using Differentials

  • Ever needed to estimate a value like √25.3 without a calculator?
  • Differentials allow us to approximate small changes in a function's value.
  • It's like finding a small 'jump' from a known point.

🎙️ Approximations are incredibly useful in real-world scenarios where exact values might be hard to calculate or unnecessary. Differentials give us a quick and accurate way to estimate values close to known points.

🤏
Small Change
21 / 33

Formal Idea: Approximations

  • If y = f(x), and Δx is a small change in x, then the corresponding change in y, Δy, is approximately dy.
  • Δy ≈ dy = (dy/dx) * Δx.
  • So, f(x + Δx) ≈ f(x) + f'(x)Δx.

🎙️ The key formula here is f(x + Δx) ≈ f(x) + f'(x)Δx. We use a known value f(x) and add the estimated change (f'(x)Δx) to approximate the value at a slightly different point (x + Δx).

Worked Example 4: Approximating Square Root

  • Problem: Use differentials to approximate √25.3.
  • Step 1: Identify f(x), x, and Δx.
  • Step 2: Find f'(x).
  • Step 3: Apply the approximation formula: f(x + Δx) ≈ f(x) + f'(x)Δx.

🎙️ For √25.3, we choose x=25 because its square root is easily known. Δx is the small difference, 0.3. Then we find the derivative of √x and substitute these values into the approximation formula.

1f(x)=√x, x=25, Δx=0.3
2f'(x)=1/(2√x)
3Calculate f(25)+f'(25)Δx
23 / 33

Solution: Approximating Square Root

  • Let f(x) = √x. Then f'(x) = 1/(2√x).
  • Let x = 25 and Δx = 0.3.
  • f(x + Δx) ≈ f(x) + f'(x)Δx.
  • √25.3 ≈ √25 + (1/(2√25)) * 0.3 = 5 + (1/10) * 0.3 = 5 + 0.03 = 5.03.

🎙️ So, √25.3 is approximately 5.03. This method provides a very good estimate for small changes. Make sure to clearly identify your f(x), x, and Δx in exam problems.

Concept 5: Maxima and Minima

  • Finding the highest (maximum) or lowest (minimum) points of a function.
  • Think of the peak of a mountain or the bottom of a valley.
  • Crucial for optimisation problems (e.g., maximum profit, minimum cost).

🎙️ Maxima and Minima are perhaps the most powerful applications of derivatives. They help us find optimal solutions in various fields, from engineering to economics. Where is the function at its best, or its worst?

📈📉
Peaks and Valleys
26 / 33

First Derivative Test for Maxima/Minima

  • Step 1: Find f'(x) and set it to zero to find critical points (x=c).
  • Step 2: Check the sign of f'(x) in intervals around 'c'.
  • If f'(x) changes from +ve to -ve at 'c', then 'c' is a point of local maxima.
  • If f'(x) changes from -ve to +ve at 'c', then 'c' is a point of local minima.

🎙️ The first derivative test relies on the change in the slope of the function. If the slope goes from positive to negative, you've hit a peak. If it goes from negative to positive, you've hit a valley.

1Find f'(x)=0
2Test sign change
3+ to - for max
4- to + for min
27 / 33

Second Derivative Test for Maxima/Minima

  • Step 1: Find f'(x) and set it to zero to find critical points (x=c).
  • Step 2: Find the second derivative, f''(x).
  • If f''(c) < 0, then x=c is a point of local maxima.
  • If f''(c) > 0, then x=c is a point of local minima.
  • If f''(c) = 0, the test fails; use the first derivative test.

🎙️ The second derivative test is often quicker if it works. It tells us about the concavity of the function. A negative second derivative means concave down (a peak), and a positive means concave up (a valley).

1Find f'(x)=0 for c
2Find f''(x)
3f''(c)<0 for max
4f''(c)>0 for min

Worked Example 5: Maxima/Minima

  • Problem: Find the local maxima and minima for f(x) = x³ - 6x² + 9x + 15.
  • Step 1: Find f'(x) and critical points.
  • Step 2: Find f''(x).
  • Step 3: Apply the second derivative test at critical points.

🎙️ Let's find the local extrema for this cubic function. We first find the critical points by setting the first derivative to zero. Then, we use the second derivative test to classify these points as local maxima or minima.

1f'(x)=3x²-12x+9
2f'(x)=0 => x=1,3
3f''(x)=6x-12
4Test f''(1), f''(3)
29 / 33

Solution: Maxima/Minima

  • f'(x) = 3x² - 12x + 9. Setting f'(x) = 0 gives 3(x-1)(x-3) = 0, so x = 1, 3.
  • f''(x) = 6x - 12.
  • At x = 1: f''(1) = 6(1) - 12 = -6 < 0. So, x=1 is a point of local maxima. Local maximum value = f(1) = 19.
  • At x = 3: f''(3) = 6(3) - 12 = 6 > 0. So, x=3 is a point of local minima. Local minimum value = f(3) = 15.

🎙️ We found two critical points. At x=1, the second derivative is negative, indicating a local maximum. At x=3, it's positive, indicating a local minimum. Remember to always state both the point and the value.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Confusing slope of tangent with slope of normal (negative reciprocal!).
  • Incorrectly identifying x and Δx in approximation problems.
  • Not testing intervals correctly in the first derivative test.
  • Forgetting to find the actual maximum/minimum VALUE, not just the point.
  • Errors in differentiation (e.g., product rule, chain rule).

🎙️ Students often lose marks due to these common errors. Always double-check your calculations, especially the signs. Understand the difference between finding the point of extremum and the extremum value itself. Practice makes perfect!

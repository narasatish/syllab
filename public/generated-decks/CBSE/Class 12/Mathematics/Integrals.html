What is Integration? The Reverse Process

  • It's the inverse operation of differentiation.
  • Also known as anti-differentiation.
  • Finds a function whose derivative is given.

🎙️ Think of integration as going backward. If differentiation gives you the rate of change, integration helps you find the original quantity from its rate of change. It's like unwrapping a gift to find what's inside.

Antiderivative: The Core Idea

  • A function F(x) is an antiderivative of f(x) if F'(x) = f(x).
  • Example: If f(x) = 2x, then F(x) = x² is an antiderivative.
  • There can be many antiderivatives for a function.

🎙️ The antiderivative is the function we are looking for. For instance, if you differentiate x², you get 2x. So, x² is an antiderivative of 2x. But x²+C is also an antiderivative, which brings us to the constant of integration.

Basic Formulas: Power Rule & Constants

  • ∫ xⁿ dx = (xⁿ⁺¹)/(n+1) + C (for n ≠ -1)
  • ∫ k dx = kx + C (where k is a constant)
  • Remember 'C' for the constant of integration!

🎙️ Let's start with the most fundamental rules. The power rule is crucial and applies to most polynomial terms. For a constant, its integral is simply the constant multiplied by x, plus C. Never forget that 'C' in indefinite integrals!

Basic Formulas: Trigonometric Functions

  • ∫ sin(x) dx = -cos(x) + C
  • ∫ cos(x) dx = sin(x) + C
  • ∫ sec²(x) dx = tan(x) + C

🎙️ Just as you learned derivatives of trigonometric functions, their integrals are equally important. Pay close attention to the signs, as they are often a source of error. Mastering these basic formulas will form the bedrock of your integral calculations.

Properties: Linearity & Sum/Difference

  • ∫ [f(x) + g(x)] dx = ∫ f(x) dx + ∫ g(x) dx
  • ∫ [f(x) - g(x)] dx = ∫ f(x) dx - ∫ g(x) dx
  • ∫ k f(x) dx = k ∫ f(x) dx (where k is a constant)

🎙️ These properties are very similar to those of differentiation. They allow us to break down complex integrals into simpler ones, making them much easier to solve. You can integrate term by term and factor out constants.

Worked Example 1: Applying Basic Rules

  • Problem: Evaluate ∫ (x³ + 2sin(x) - 5) dx
  • Solution: ∫ x³ dx + ∫ 2sin(x) dx - ∫ 5 dx
  • = (x⁴/4) + 2(-cos(x)) - 5x + C
  • = x⁴/4 - 2cos(x) - 5x + C

🎙️ This example demonstrates how to use the linearity property and apply the power rule and trigonometric integral formulas. Remember to integrate each term separately and then combine them, always adding the constant 'C' at the end.

Common Mistake: The Missing 'C'!

  • Always add the constant of integration 'C' for indefinite integrals.
  • Without 'C', your answer is incomplete and incorrect.
  • It represents the entire family of antiderivatives.

🎙️ This is a very common error, especially under exam pressure. The constant 'C' is crucial because the derivative of any constant is zero. So, when integrating, we must account for any potential constant that might have been present in the original function. Always make it a habit!

Method 1: Integration by Substitution

  • Transforms a complex integral into a simpler one.
  • Identify a part of the integrand whose derivative is also present.
  • Let u = g(x), then du = g'(x) dx.

🎙️ Substitution is like a change of variables. We pick a part of the function, let's say 'u', such that its derivative also appears in the integrand. This simplifies the integral into a more manageable form, often reducing it to one of the basic formulas we just learned.

➡️
Simplifying Integrals

Worked Example 2: Using Substitution

  • Problem: Evaluate ∫ 2x (x² + 1)⁵ dx
  • Let u = x² + 1, then du/dx = 2x, so du = 2x dx.
  • Integral becomes ∫ u⁵ du.
  • = u⁶/6 + C.
  • Substitute back: (x² + 1)⁶/6 + C.

🎙️ Here, we identify x²+1 as a good candidate for 'u' because its derivative, 2x, is also present. After substitution, the integral becomes a simple power rule problem. Don't forget the final step: substituting 'x' back into the expression.

Method 2: Integration by Parts (Product Rule for Integrals)

  • Used for integrals of products of two functions.
  • Formula: ∫ u dv = uv - ∫ v du
  • Choose 'u' and 'dv' using the ILATE rule (Inverse, Log, Algebraic, Trig, Exp).

🎙️ Integration by Parts is the integral equivalent of the product rule for differentiation. It helps integrate products of functions that can't be solved by simple substitution. The key is to correctly identify 'u' and 'dv' using the ILATE priority rule to make the ∫ v du term simpler than the original integral.

Worked Example 3: Applying Integration by Parts

  • Problem: Evaluate ∫ x cos(x) dx
  • Let u = x (Algebraic), dv = cos(x) dx (Trigonometric).
  • Then du = dx, v = sin(x).
  • Using ∫ u dv = uv - ∫ v du:
  • = x sin(x) - ∫ sin(x) dx
  • = x sin(x) - (-cos(x)) + C = x sin(x) + cos(x) + C.

🎙️ In this example, we have a product of an algebraic and a trigonometric function. Following ILATE, 'x' is 'u' and 'cos(x) dx' is 'dv'. We then apply the formula, perform the remaining integral, and add the constant 'C'. Always double-check your calculations.

Definite Integrals: Area Under the Curve

  • An integral with upper and lower limits of integration.
  • Represents the net signed area between the curve f(x) and the x-axis.
  • Result is a specific numerical value, NOT a function with 'C'.

🎙️ Unlike indefinite integrals, definite integrals have specific limits, 'a' and 'b'. The value obtained is a number, representing the accumulated quantity or the area under the curve between those limits. This is where integrals truly shine in real-world applications.

Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (Part 1)

  • If F'(x) = f(x), then ∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx = F(b) - F(a).
  • Connects differentiation and integration.
  • Provides a method to evaluate definite integrals.

🎙️ This theorem is a cornerstone of calculus, providing the practical method for evaluating definite integrals. It states that you first find the antiderivative of the function, then evaluate it at the upper limit and subtract its value at the lower limit. This eliminates the need for 'C'.

🌉

Worked Example 4: Evaluating Definite Integrals

  • Problem: Evaluate ∫₁² x² dx
  • Antiderivative of x² is x³/3.
  • Apply FTC: [x³/3]₁² = (2³/3) - (1³/3)
  • = (8/3) - (1/3) = 7/3.

🎙️ Here's a straightforward application of the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus. First, find the indefinite integral. Then, substitute the upper limit, then the lower limit, and subtract the results. Notice there's no 'C' in the final answer.

Properties of Definite Integrals (Part 1)

  • ∫ₐᵃ f(x) dx = 0
  • ∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx = - ∫ᵇₐ f(x) dx
  • ∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx = ∫ₐᶜ f(x) dx + ∫ᶜᵇ f(x) dx (where a < c < b)

🎙️ These properties are very useful for simplifying definite integrals or breaking them down. The first one makes sense: if the upper and lower limits are the same, there's no interval, so the area is zero. The second allows you to swap limits by changing the sign.

Properties of Definite Integrals (Part 2)

  • ∫₀ᵃ f(x) dx = ∫₀ᵃ f(a-x) dx
  • ∫₋ᵃᵃ f(x) dx = 2∫₀ᵃ f(x) dx (if f is even)
  • ∫₋ᵃᵃ f(x) dx = 0 (if f is odd)

🎙️ These properties, especially the last two concerning even and odd functions, are incredibly powerful for solving specific types of definite integrals quickly in exams. Understanding symmetry can save a lot of calculation time. Make sure to review function types.

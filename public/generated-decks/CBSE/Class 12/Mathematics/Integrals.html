Integrals: The Antiderivative

  • Inverse process of differentiation.
  • Finding a function whose derivative is given.
  • Also known as 'primitives'.

🎙️ At its core, integration is the reverse operation of differentiation. If you differentiate a function to get another function, integration helps you go back from the derived function to the original one. This is why it's called the antiderivative.

The Indefinite Integral

  • Represents the family of all antiderivatives.
  • Notation: ∫f(x) dx.
  • Result includes an arbitrary constant 'C'.

🎙️ When we talk about indefinite integrals, we're looking for a general solution, a family of functions, whose derivative is the given function. The integral symbol '∫' signifies this operation, and 'dx' indicates the variable of integration.

The Constant of Integration 'C'

  • Derivative of any constant is zero.
  • Therefore, many functions can have the same derivative.
  • 'C' accounts for this ambiguity.

🎙️ This 'C' is crucial! When you differentiate x² + 5, you get 2x. If you differentiate x² + 100, you also get 2x. So, when integrating 2x, how do we know if it came from x² + 5 or x² + 100? The 'C' covers all these possibilities, representing any arbitrary constant.

The 'Plus C'

Fundamental Formulas (Part 1)

  • ∫x^n dx = (x^(n+1))/(n+1) + C, (n ≠ -1)
  • ∫(1/x) dx = log|x| + C
  • ∫e^x dx = e^x + C

🎙️ These are your foundational formulas, much like the basic differentiation rules. Memorize them thoroughly, as they are the building blocks for more complex integrations. Remember the special case for x^-1.

Properties of Indefinite Integrals

  • ∫k f(x) dx = k ∫f(x) dx (Constant multiple rule)
  • ∫[f(x) ± g(x)] dx = ∫f(x) dx ± ∫g(x) dx (Sum/Difference rule)
  • Integrals are linear operators.

🎙️ These properties simplify integration of expressions involving sums, differences, and constant multiples. They allow us to break down complex integrals into simpler, manageable parts. This linearity is a powerful tool.

Worked Example: Basic Integration

  • Integrate: ∫(3x² + 2x - 5) dx
  • Step 1: Apply sum/difference and constant multiple rules.
  • Step 2: Use power rule for each term.
  • Solution: x³ + x² - 5x + C

🎙️ Let's walk through a simple example. We apply the linearity property to integrate each term separately. Remember to add 'C' at the end, as this is an indefinite integral. Always double-check by differentiating your answer.

Integration by Substitution

  • Simplifies complex integrals.
  • Introduce a new variable 'u'.
  • Transform the integral in terms of 'u' and 'du'.

🎙️ Substitution is a powerful technique to convert a complex integral into a simpler, more recognizable form. The key is to identify a part of the integrand whose derivative is also present (or a constant multiple of it). This allows us to change variables effectively.

↔️
Changing Variables
13 / 25

Worked Example: Substitution Method

  • Integrate: ∫2x√(1+x²) dx
  • Let u = 1+x². Then du = 2x dx.
  • Integral becomes ∫√u du = (2/3)u^(3/2) + C.
  • Substitute back: (2/3)(1+x²)^(3/2) + C

🎙️ Here, by letting u = 1+x², the derivative 2x dx appears directly in the integrand. This makes the substitution straightforward, transforming the integral into a simple power rule problem. Don't forget to substitute back to express the answer in terms of the original variable.

Integration by Parts

  • Used for products of functions.
  • Formula: ∫u dv = uv - ∫v du.
  • Choose 'u' and 'dv' using the ILATE rule.

🎙️ Integration by Parts is for integrals involving products of two different types of functions, where substitution doesn't work directly. The ILATE rule (Inverse, Logarithmic, Algebraic, Trigonometric, Exponential) helps us decide which function to choose as 'u' for easier integration.

✂️
Breaking Down Products
16 / 25

Worked Example: Integration by Parts

  • Integrate: ∫x e^x dx
  • Choose u = x, dv = e^x dx.
  • Then du = dx, v = e^x.
  • Apply formula: x e^x - ∫e^x dx = x e^x - e^x + C.

🎙️ In this example, 'x' is algebraic and 'e^x' is exponential. Following ILATE, 'x' comes before 'e^x', so we choose u=x. This simplifies the ∫v du part, making the integral solvable. Practice makes perfect with choosing 'u' and 'dv'.

The Definite Integral

  • Integral with upper and lower limits.
  • Represents a specific numerical value (e.g., area).
  • Notation: ∫_a^b f(x) dx.

🎙️ Unlike indefinite integrals, definite integrals have specific boundaries, 'a' and 'b', called the lower and upper limits. The result is a single numerical value, often representing a quantity like area, volume, or total change. This is where integrals become very concrete.

Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (FTC)

  • Links differentiation and integration.
  • Part 1: d/dx [∫_a^x f(t) dt] = f(x).
  • Part 2: ∫_a^b f(x) dx = F(b) - F(a), where F'(x) = f(x).

🎙️ The FTC is arguably the most important theorem in calculus. It elegantly connects the two seemingly separate concepts of differentiation and integration. Part 2 is particularly useful for evaluating definite integrals, simplifying what could otherwise be a complex summation process.

⚛️
Core Principle

Properties of Definite Integrals (Part 1)

  • ∫_a^a f(x) dx = 0
  • ∫_a^b f(x) dx = -∫_b^a f(x) dx
  • ∫_a^b f(x) dx = ∫_a^c f(x) dx + ∫_c^b f(x) dx (where a < c < b)

🎙️ These properties are incredibly useful for simplifying definite integrals and solving problems efficiently. For instance, the interval addition property allows us to break down an integral over a larger interval into parts, which is helpful for piecewise functions.

Worked Example: Definite Integral

  • Evaluate: ∫_1^2 (x² + 1) dx
  • Step 1: Find the indefinite integral F(x) = (x³/3 + x).
  • Step 2: Apply FTC Part 2: F(2) - F(1).
  • Solution: (8/3 + 2) - (1/3 + 1) = (14/3) - (4/3) = 10/3.

🎙️ Here, we first find the antiderivative of the function. Then, we evaluate it at the upper limit and subtract its value at the lower limit. Notice that the constant 'C' cancels out in definite integrals, which is why it's not explicitly written in the final answer.

Continuity at a Point: The Formal Definition

  • A function f(x) is continuous at x=a if:
  • 1. lim (x→a⁻) f(x) exists (Left Hand Limit)
  • 2. lim (x→a⁺) f(x) exists (Right Hand Limit)
  • 3. LHL = RHL = f(a) (Function Value)

🎙️ For a function to be continuous at a specific point 'a', three conditions must be met. The limit of the function as x approaches 'a' from the left must equal the limit as x approaches 'a' from the right, and both must be equal to the actual value of the function at 'a'. This ensures there are no gaps or jumps at that precise point.

1Left Hand Limit (LHL)
2Right Hand Limit (RHL)
3Function Value f(a)

Worked Example 1: Is f(x) = x² continuous at x=2?

  • Step 1: Find f(2). f(2) = 2² = 4.
  • Step 2: Find LHL: lim (x→2⁻) x² = 2² = 4.
  • Step 3: Find RHL: lim (x→2⁺) x² = 2² = 4.
  • Conclusion: LHL = RHL = f(2) = 4. So, f(x) is continuous at x=2.

🎙️ Let's apply our definition to a simple polynomial function. For f(x) = x², we evaluate the function at x=2, and then calculate the left-hand and right-hand limits as x approaches 2. Since all three values are equal, we confirm that the function is indeed continuous at x=2. Polynomials are generally continuous everywhere.

When a Function is NOT Continuous: Types of Discontinuity

  • Removable Discontinuity: A 'hole' in the graph.
  • Jump Discontinuity: A sudden 'jump' in the graph.
  • Infinite Discontinuity: The function value approaches infinity.

🎙️ Just as it's important to know what continuity is, it's equally important to recognize what makes a function discontinuous. These 'breaks' in the graph come in different forms: a single missing point, a sudden shift in value, or the function shooting off to infinity. Each type violates one or more conditions of continuity.

Worked Example 2: Continuity of Piecewise Function

  • Consider f(x) = { x+2 if x ≤ 1; x² if x > 1 } at x=1.
  • Step 1: f(1) = 1+2 = 3.
  • Step 2: LHL = lim (x→1⁻) (x+2) = 1+2 = 3.
  • Step 3: RHL = lim (x→1⁺) (x²) = 1² = 1.
  • Conclusion: LHL ≠ RHL. So, f(x) is discontinuous at x=1.

🎙️ Piecewise functions are excellent for testing continuity because their definition changes at specific points. Here, we check the point where the definition switches, x=1. We find the function value and both limits. Since the left-hand limit does not match the right-hand limit, the function has a jump discontinuity at x=1.

Continuity over an Interval: A Smooth Road Trip 🛣️

  • A function is continuous over an interval if it's continuous at every point within that interval.
  • For closed interval [a,b], it must be continuous from the right at 'a' and from the left at 'b'.
  • Think of a smooth road trip from start to finish, no bumps!

🎙️ Extending our concept, a function is continuous over an entire interval if it's continuous at every single point in that range. For closed intervals, we also need to consider one-sided continuity at the endpoints. This ensures a truly unbroken path across the entire specified domain.

Properties of Continuous Functions: Combining Smoothness

  • If f and g are continuous, then f ± g, f · g are continuous.
  • f/g is continuous (provided g(x) ≠ 0).
  • Composite function (f o g)(x) is continuous if f and g are continuous.

🎙️ These properties are incredibly useful as they save us from checking continuity conditions for every complex function. If you know that individual functions are continuous, you can often conclude the continuity of their sums, differences, products, quotients, and even compositions, under certain conditions. This simplifies many problems.

Differentiability: Understanding the Slope ⛰️

  • Differentiability relates to the existence of a unique tangent line at every point.
  • It's about how 'smooth' a curve is, without sharp corners or cusps.
  • Think of a smooth mountain slope versus a jagged peak.

🎙️ Now, let's move to differentiability. While continuity means no breaks, differentiability implies an even higher level of smoothness. It means that at every point on the curve, you can draw a unique tangent line, signifying a well-defined instantaneous rate of change. Sharp corners, like the peak of a mountain, don't allow for a unique tangent.

Geometric Meaning: Slope of the Tangent

  • The derivative of a function f(x) at a point x=a, denoted f'(a), represents the slope of the tangent line to the curve y=f(x) at (a, f(a)).
  • It tells us the instantaneous rate of change of the function at that point.
  • Imagine the speedometer of a car at a specific moment.

🎙️ Geometrically, the derivative is the slope of the tangent line. This is a powerful concept because it quantifies how steeply a function is rising or falling at any given point. It's the 'instantaneous rate of change', much like the speed of a car at a particular instant in time.

Differentiability at a Point: The Formal Definition

  • A function f(x) is differentiable at x=a if:
  • lim (h→0⁻) [f(a+h) - f(a)]/h exists (Left Hand Derivative, LHD)
  • lim (h→0⁺) [f(a+h) - f(a)]/h exists (Right Hand Derivative, RHD)
  • LHD = RHD

🎙️ Similar to continuity, differentiability at a point 'a' requires the existence and equality of two limits: the Left Hand Derivative and the Right Hand Derivative. These limits represent the slope of the tangent approaching from the left and right, respectively. If they are equal, the tangent is unique, and the function is differentiable.

1Left Hand Derivative (LHD)
2Right Hand Derivative (RHD)

Differentiability Implies Continuity: A One-Way Street ➡️

  • If a function is differentiable at a point, it MUST be continuous at that point.
  • Think: If a path is smooth, it must also be connected.
  • The converse is NOT true: A continuous function is not necessarily differentiable.

🎙️ This is a critical relationship to understand. Differentiability is a stronger condition than continuity. If a function is smooth enough to have a well-defined tangent at every point, it absolutely cannot have any breaks or jumps. However, just because a function is continuous doesn't mean it's smooth; it could have sharp corners, like the absolute value function.

Worked Example 3: Is f(x) = x² differentiable at x=0?

  • f'(x) = 2x.
  • LHD = lim (h→0⁻) [ (0+h)² - 0² ]/h = lim (h→0⁻) h²/h = lim (h→0⁻) h = 0.
  • RHD = lim (h→0⁺) [ (0+h)² - 0² ]/h = lim (h→0⁺) h²/h = lim (h→0⁺) h = 0.
  • Conclusion: LHD = RHD = 0. So, f(x) is differentiable at x=0, and f'(0)=0.

🎙️ Let's test the differentiability of f(x) = x² at x=0 using the formal definition. We calculate both the Left Hand Derivative and the Right Hand Derivative. Since both limits exist and are equal to zero, the function is differentiable at x=0. This confirms our understanding of smooth curves.

When a Function is NOT Differentiable: Sharp Corners & Jumps

  • Sharp corners (like |x| at x=0): No unique tangent.
  • Cusps: Similar to sharp corners, tangent approaches vertically.
  • Discontinuities: If not continuous, cannot be differentiable.

🎙️ Functions fail to be differentiable at points where they are not continuous, or where they have sharp corners or cusps. At a sharp corner, you can draw multiple tangent lines, so there isn't a unique slope. This is a key visual indicator of non-differentiability, even if the function is continuous.

Worked Example 4: Differentiability of f(x) = |x| at x=0

  • f(x) = { -x if x < 0; x if x ≥ 0 }.
  • LHD = lim (h→0⁻) [|0+h| - |0|]/h = lim (h→0⁻) |-h|/h = lim (h→0⁻) (-h)/h = -1.
  • RHD = lim (h→0⁺) [|0+h| - |0|]/h = lim (h→0⁺) |h|/h = lim (h→0⁺) h/h = 1.
  • Conclusion: LHD ≠ RHD. So, f(x) = |x| is not differentiable at x=0.

🎙️ This is a classic example. The absolute value function is continuous at x=0, but it has a sharp corner. When we calculate the LHD and RHD, we find they are -1 and 1, respectively. Since these are not equal, the function is not differentiable at x=0, even though it's continuous there.

Chain Rule: Differentiating Layered Functions ⛓️

  • Used for differentiating composite functions, like f(g(x)).
  • Derivative of outer function (keeping inner function same) multiplied by derivative of inner function.
  • d/dx [f(g(x))] = f'(g(x)) * g'(x).

🎙️ The Chain Rule is one of the most powerful and frequently used rules in differentiation. It helps us find the derivative of a function that is 'nested' inside another function. Think of it as peeling an onion, differentiating layer by layer, from the outside in, and multiplying the results.

⛓️
Derivative of outer * derivative of inner

Worked Example 5: Differentiate sin(x²)

  • Let y = sin(u) where u = x².
  • Step 1: Differentiate outer function (sin u) w.r.t u: dy/du = cos(u).
  • Step 2: Differentiate inner function (u = x²) w.r.t x: du/dx = 2x.
  • Step 3: Apply Chain Rule: dy/dx = (dy/du) * (du/dx) = cos(x²) * 2x = 2x cos(x²).

🎙️ Here's a step-by-step application of the Chain Rule. We identify the outer function (sine) and the inner function (x²). We differentiate each part separately and then multiply them together to get the final derivative. Always remember to substitute the inner function back in the end.

Implicit Differentiation: When Y is Hiding! 🕵️‍♀️

  • Used when y cannot be easily expressed as a function of x (y=f(x)).
  • Differentiate both sides of the equation with respect to x.
  • Remember to use the chain rule for terms involving y (e.g., d/dx (y²) = 2y dy/dx).

🎙️ Sometimes, functions are defined implicitly, meaning y is not explicitly isolated. In such cases, we use implicit differentiation. The key is to differentiate every term with respect to x, treating y as a function of x, and applying the chain rule whenever you differentiate a term containing y. Then, solve for dy/dx.

🕵️‍♀️

Worked Example 6: Find dy/dx for x² + y² = 25

  • Step 1: Differentiate both sides w.r.t x: d/dx(x²) + d/dx(y²) = d/dx(25).
  • Step 2: Apply rules: 2x + 2y (dy/dx) = 0.
  • Step 3: Isolate dy/dx: 2y (dy/dx) = -2x.
  • Step 4: Solve for dy/dx: dy/dx = -2x / 2y = -x/y.

🎙️ This example demonstrates how to find the derivative of a circle's equation using implicit differentiation. Notice how the derivative of y² becomes 2y * dy/dx due to the chain rule. After differentiating, the remaining algebraic steps involve isolating dy/dx to get the final answer.

Common Mistakes to Avoid! ❌

  • Forgetting to check LHL ≠ RHL for continuity in piecewise functions.
  • Assuming continuity implies differentiability (remember |x|).
  • Not applying Chain Rule correctly, especially in implicit differentiation.
  • Algebraic errors when isolating dy/dx.

🎙️ It's common for students to make certain errors in this chapter. Always verify both LHL and RHL for continuity, and remember that differentiability is a stronger condition. The Chain Rule is often overlooked or misapplied, so practice it diligently. Also, be careful with algebraic manipulations at the end of implicit differentiation problems.

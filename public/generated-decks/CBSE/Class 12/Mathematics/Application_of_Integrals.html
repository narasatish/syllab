Area Bounded by a Curve and the X-axis

  • This is the fundamental application of definite integrals.
  • We divide the area into infinitely many thin vertical strips.
  • The sum of these strips gives the total area.

🎙️ The most basic application is finding the area enclosed by a curve, the x-axis, and two vertical lines. Think of it as approximating the area with many tiny rectangles. As the width of these rectangles approaches zero, their sum becomes the definite integral, giving us the exact area.

The Definite Integral as Area: Formal Definition

  • If y = f(x) is a continuous function, the area A bounded by the curve, the x-axis, and the ordinates x=a and x=b is given by:
  • A = ∫_a^b f(x) dx
  • Remember: Area is always a positive quantity.

🎙️ Here's the formal definition. When f(x) is above the x-axis, the integral directly gives the area. If it's below, the integral value will be negative, so we take its absolute value. This formula is the cornerstone of our entire chapter.

  • Step 2: Set up the definite integral.

🎙️ Let's walk through an example. The function is y = x², and our limits are x=1 to x=3. The integral will be from 1 to 3 of x² dx. Evaluating this, we get [x³/3] from 1 to 3, which is (27/3) - (1/3) = 26/3 square units. Always remember to write 'square units' with your final answer.

Integrating with Respect to Y: The Formula

  • If x = g(y) is a continuous function, the area A bounded by the curve, the y-axis, and the abscissas y=c and y=d is given by:
  • A = ∫_c^d g(y) dy
  • Ensure g(y) is positive in the interval [c,d] for direct calculation.

🎙️ This formula is symmetric to the x-axis integration. The key is to express x as a function of y and use the y-limits of integration. Remember, if g(y) is negative, take the absolute value of the integral.

Area Between Two Curves: Top Minus Bottom

  • What if the area isn't bounded by an axis, but by two different curves?
  • We find the area by subtracting the 'lower' function's integral from the 'upper' function's integral.
  • This effectively gives us the area of the region between them.

🎙️ This is a very common scenario in exams. Imagine two rivers flowing side by side, and you want to find the area of the land strip between them. We calculate the area under the 'upper' curve and subtract the area under the 'lower' curve within the specified limits. The result is the area of the region enclosed by both curves.

Formula for Area Between Curves

  • If y = f(x) and y = g(x) are two continuous functions such that f(x) ≥ g(x) in [a,b], the area A is:
  • A = ∫_a^b [f(x) - g(x)] dx
  • The limits 'a' and 'b' are often the intersection points of the curves.

🎙️ The crucial part here is correctly identifying which function is 'above' the other in the given interval. If the curves intersect multiple times, you might need to split the integral into multiple parts, where the 'upper' and 'lower' functions might switch.

Worked Example 3: Area Between a Line and a Parabola

  • Find the area enclosed by y = x and y = x².
  • Step 1: Find the points of intersection.
  • Step 2: Determine which curve is 'above' the other.
  • Step 3: Set up and evaluate the integral.

🎙️ First, find where x = x², which gives x=0 and x=1. Between 0 and 1, the line y=x is above the parabola y=x². So, the integral is from 0 to 1 of (x - x²) dx. This evaluates to [x²/2 - x³/3] from 0 to 1, giving (1/2 - 1/3) = 1/6 square units.

Handling Multiple Intersection Points: Divide and Conquer

  • Sometimes curves intersect more than twice, creating multiple enclosed regions.
  • In such cases, the 'upper' and 'lower' functions might swap.
  • We need to split the total area into sub-regions and sum their individual areas.

🎙️ This is where problems get a little trickier. If two curves cross each other multiple times, you can't just use one integral from the leftmost to the rightmost intersection. You must break the problem down into smaller, manageable parts, integrating each segment separately.

Strategy: Divide and Conquer for Complex Areas

  • Step 1: Sketch the region to visualize the curves and their intersections.
  • Step 2: Find all points of intersection by setting the functions equal.
  • Step 3: Divide the total area into sub-regions based on intersection points.
  • Step 4: For each sub-region, identify the upper and lower curves and set up the integral.
  • Step 5: Sum the absolute values of the areas of all sub-regions.

🎙️ This systematic approach is key to avoiding errors in complex problems. A good sketch is your best friend here. It helps you correctly identify the limits of integration for each sub-region and determine which function is on top.

1Sketch Region
2Find Intersections
3Divide Sub-regions
4Integrate Each Part
5Sum Areas

Worked Example 4: Area Bounded by y=x and y=x³

  • Find the area of the region bounded by y = x and y = x³.
  • Step 1: Find intersection points: x³ = x => x(x²-1) = 0 => x = -1, 0, 1.
  • Step 2: Observe regions: From x=-1 to 0, x³ is above x. From x=0 to 1, x is above x³.
  • Step 3: Set up two integrals and sum their areas.

🎙️ Here, we have three intersection points: (-1,-1), (0,0), and (1,1). This means we need two integrals. For [-1,0], it's ∫ (x³-x) dx. For [0,1], it's ∫ (x-x³) dx. Notice the order of subtraction changes. The total area will be the sum of the absolute values of these two integrals.

Example: Using Integration to Find Area of a Circle

  • Consider a circle x² + y² = r².
  • We can express y = √(r² - x²).
  • We calculate the area of one quadrant and multiply by 4.

🎙️ For a circle centered at the origin, we can solve for y to get y = ±√(r² - x²). We'll take the positive root for the upper semicircle. Integrating from 0 to r gives the area of one quadrant. Multiplying this by 4 yields the familiar formula πr².

Worked Example 5: Area of an Ellipse

  • Find the area of the ellipse x²/a² + y²/b² = 1.
  • Express y in terms of x: y = (b/a)√(a² - x²).
  • Calculate the area of one quadrant and multiply by 4.

🎙️ Similar to the circle, we use symmetry. The integral for one quadrant would be ∫_0^a (b/a)√(a² - x²) dx. This integral often requires a trigonometric substitution (x = a sin θ). After evaluation and multiplying by 4, we arrive at the formula πab for the area of an ellipse.

Common Mistakes to Avoid in Application of Integrals

  • Incorrectly identifying upper and lower curves, especially with multiple intersections.
  • Forgetting to take the absolute value of area if the curve is below the axis.
  • Errors in finding intersection points or limits of integration.
  • Calculation mistakes during integration or evaluation of definite integrals.
  • Not sketching the region, leading to conceptual errors.

🎙️ These are the most frequent errors students make. Always draw a clear diagram; it's your visual guide. Double-check your intersection points and ensure you're subtracting the correct functions. Remember, area is always positive, so if your integral gives a negative result, take its absolute value.

Chapter Recap: Key Takeaways

  • Area under y=f(x) from x=a to x=b: ∫_a^b f(x) dx.
  • Area under x=g(y) from y=c to y=d: ∫_c^d g(y) dy.
  • Area between two curves f(x) and g(x): ∫_a^b [f(x) - g(x)] dx (where f(x) ≥ g(x)).
  • For multiple intersections, divide the area into sub-regions and sum them up.

🎙️ Let's quickly summarize what we've learned today. The core idea is that definite integrals allow us to find areas of regions bounded by curves. Whether it's a single curve, two curves, or complex multi-region areas, the principle remains the same: integrate the difference between the 'upper' and 'lower' boundaries.

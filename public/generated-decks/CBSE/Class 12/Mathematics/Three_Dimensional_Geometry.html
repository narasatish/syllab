Recalling 2D vs. 3D Space

  • 2D: Defined by X and Y axes (flat plane).
  • 3D: Defined by X, Y, and Z axes (space).
  • Adds depth and volume to our understanding.

🎙️ Before we dive deep, let's quickly recall the difference between 2D and 3D. In 2D, we deal with shapes on a flat surface like a paper, using two coordinates. In 3D, we add a third dimension, depth, which allows us to describe objects in our real world.

The 3D Coordinate System: Axes & Octants

  • Three mutually perpendicular axes: X, Y, Z.
  • Origin (0,0,0) is the intersection point.
  • Space divided into 8 octants.

🎙️ Imagine three lines crossing at a single point, each perpendicular to the other two. These are our X, Y, and Z axes. They divide the entire space into eight regions, much like quadrants in 2D, but here they are called octants.

Locating a Point: (x, y, z) Coordinates

  • Every point P in space has unique coordinates (x, y, z).
  • x, y, z represent perpendicular distances from YZ, XZ, XY planes respectively.
  • Points on axes or planes have specific zero coordinates.

🎙️ Just as you locate a point on a map using latitude and longitude, in 3D, we use three coordinates (x, y, z). These values tell us exactly where a point is relative to the origin and the three coordinate planes.

Calculating Distance Between Two Points

  • Extension of the 2D distance formula.
  • Distance between P(x1, y1, z1) and Q(x2, y2, z2).
  • Formula: √[(x2-x1)² + (y2-y1)² + (z2-z1)²].

🎙️ The distance formula in 3D is a direct generalization from 2D. We simply add the square of the difference in the z-coordinates. This formula is vital for calculating lengths of segments, perimeters, and more in 3D space.

Section Formula: Dividing a Line Segment (Internal)

  • Finds coordinates of a point dividing a segment in a given ratio.
  • Point R divides P(x1,y1,z1) and Q(x2,y2,z2) internally in ratio m:n.
  • Formula: R = [(mx2+nx1)/(m+n), (my2+ny1)/(m+n), (mz2+nz1)/(m+n)].

🎙️ The section formula helps us find the coordinates of a point that lies *between* two given points and divides the line segment joining them in a specific ratio. This is a direct extension of the 2D section formula.

Section Formula: External Division & Midpoint

  • External Division: R = [(mx2-nx1)/(m-n), (my2-ny1)/(m-n), (mz2-nz1)/(m-n)].
  • Midpoint Formula: Special case when m:n = 1:1.
  • Midpoint M = [(x1+x2)/2, (y1+y2)/2, (z1+z2)/2].

🎙️ External division means the point lies *outside* the segment on the line extended. The formula is similar, just with a minus sign. The midpoint is a very common special case where the ratio is 1:1, making the calculation simpler.

Direction Cosines: Orienting a Line in Space

  • Cosines of angles a, b, g made by a line with positive X, Y, Z axes.
  • Represented as l = cos a, m = cos b, n = cos g.
  • Important relation: l² + m² + n² = 1.

🎙️ Direction cosines tell us the orientation of a line in 3D space. They are essentially the projections of a unit vector along the line onto the coordinate axes. The sum of their squares is always one, a crucial property.

Direction Ratios: Proportional to Direction Cosines

  • Any three numbers a, b, c proportional to direction cosines (l, m, n).
  • Not unique; many sets of DRs for a single line.
  • If (a,b,c) are DRs, then (ka,kb,kc) are also DRs for any k≠0.

🎙️ Direction ratios are simpler to find than direction cosines. They are just numbers proportional to the direction cosines. Think of them as a vector pointing in the direction of the line, but not necessarily a unit vector.

Connecting Direction Cosines and Direction Ratios

  • If (a, b, c) are DRs, then DCs (l, m, n) can be found.
  • l = a/√(a²+b²+c²), m = b/√(a²+b²+c²), n = c/√(a²+b²+c²).
  • The denominator is the magnitude of the vector (a,b,c).

🎙️ This slide shows how to convert direction ratios into unique direction cosines. We essentially normalize the direction ratios by dividing each by the magnitude of the vector formed by the DRs. This ensures l² + m² + n² = 1.

Equation of a Line in Space: Vector Form

  • Line passing through point A (position vector 'a') and parallel to vector 'b'.
  • Vector equation: r = a + λb.
  • r is the position vector of any point on the line.
  • λ is a scalar parameter.

🎙️ The vector form of a line's equation is intuitive. It says that any point on the line can be reached by starting at a known point 'a' on the line and moving some multiple (λ) of the direction vector 'b'.

Equation of a Line in Space: Cartesian Form

  • Line passing through (x1, y1, z1) and having DRs (a, b, c).
  • Cartesian equation: (x-x1)/a = (y-y1)/b = (z-z1)/c.
  • This form is derived directly from the vector form.
  • Each part equals the parameter λ.

🎙️ The Cartesian form is often more practical for calculations. Here, (x1, y1, z1) is a point on the line, and (a, b, c) are its direction ratios. Remember that 'a', 'b', 'c' cannot be zero simultaneously. If one is zero, the numerator must also be zero for that term.

Angle Between Two Lines in Space

  • Given two lines with direction vectors b1 and b2 (or DRs (a1,b1,c1) and (a2,b2,c2)).
  • Vector form: cos θ = |b1 · b2| / (|b1| |b2|).
  • Cartesian form: cos θ = |a1a2 + b1b2 + c1c2| / √(a1²+b1²+c1²)√(a2²+b2²+c2²).
  • Angle is always acute (0 to π/2).

🎙️ The angle between two lines is found using their direction vectors or direction ratios. We use the dot product formula, taking the absolute value to ensure we get the acute angle between them. Remember, parallel lines have θ=0, perpendicular lines have θ=π/2.

Shortest Distance Between Two Skew Lines: Concept

  • Skew lines are non-parallel, non-intersecting lines in 3D space.
  • They do not lie in the same plane.
  • The shortest distance is the length of the common perpendicular between them.

🎙️ Skew lines are a unique concept in 3D. Unlike parallel lines that never meet, or intersecting lines that meet at a point, skew lines never meet and are not parallel. Imagine two roads on different levels that cross over each other without connecting.

Shortest Distance Between Skew Lines: Formula

  • Lines: r1 = a1 + λb1 and r2 = a2 + μb2.
  • Formula: d = |(b1 x b2) · (a2 - a1)| / |b1 x b2|.
  • Denominator is the magnitude of the cross product of direction vectors.
  • Numerator involves scalar triple product.

🎙️ This formula is crucial for finding the shortest distance between two skew lines. It looks complex but involves standard vector operations: cross product, dot product, and magnitude. Master this formula for exam success.

Common Mistakes & Exam Tips for 3D Geometry

  • Sign errors in section formula or distance formula.
  • Confusing DRs with DCs (l²+m²+n²=1 only for DCs).
  • Incorrect vector operations (dot/cross product) for shortest distance.
  • Not identifying 'a' and 'b' correctly in line equations.

🎙️ Many students lose marks due to careless errors. Always double-check your signs, especially with negative coordinates. Ensure you understand the distinction between direction ratios and direction cosines, and practice your vector algebra.

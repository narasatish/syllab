Scalars vs. Vectors: The Definitions

  • Scalar: A quantity with magnitude only.
  • Vector: A quantity with both magnitude and direction.
  • Magnitude: The numerical value or size of the quantity.

🎙️ Think of magnitude as 'how much' and direction as 'which way'. This distinction is the very foundation of vector algebra and will be vital throughout your studies.

Vector Representation: Formal Definition

  • A directed line segment from point A to point B is denoted as vector AB.
  • Its length, denoted as |AB|, is the magnitude.
  • Its direction is from A to B.

🎙️ We often denote a vector with a single lowercase letter with an arrow above it, like 'a⃗', or in bold, like 'a'. The magnitude is always a non-negative real number.

Key Vector Types: Zero & Unit Vectors

  • Zero Vector (Null Vector): Magnitude 0, no specific direction. Denoted by 0⃗.
  • Unit Vector: Magnitude 1. Used to specify direction. Denoted by â (read 'a hat').
  • Calculated as â = a⃗ / |a⃗|.

🎙️ The zero vector is like standing still, no movement. A unit vector is incredibly useful because it isolates direction, allowing us to combine it with any magnitude.

More Vector Types: Co-initial, Collinear, Equal

  • Co-initial Vectors: Vectors having the same initial point.
  • Collinear Vectors: Vectors parallel to the same line, irrespective of magnitude or direction.
  • Equal Vectors: Same magnitude AND same direction.

🎙️ Be careful with collinear vectors – they can point in opposite directions. Equal vectors, however, are identical in every aspect, they represent the same displacement or force.

Position Vector: Formal Definition

  • The position vector of a point P(x, y, z) with respect to the origin O(0, 0, 0) is OP⃗.
  • Represented as r⃗ = x î + y ĵ + z k̂.

🎙️ This is a fundamental concept for working with vectors in 2D and 3D space. It allows us to describe any point using a vector starting from the origin.

Triangle Law of Vector Addition

  • If two vectors are represented by two sides of a triangle taken in order,
  • Then their sum (resultant) is represented by the third side taken in the opposite order.
  • a⃗ + b⃗ = c⃗

🎙️ This is a visual and intuitive way to add vectors. Place the tail of the second vector at the head of the first. The resultant vector connects the tail of the first to the head of the second.

🔺
Head-to-Tail Rule
17 / 30

Parallelogram Law of Vector Addition

  • If two vectors are represented by the adjacent sides of a parallelogram starting from a common point,
  • Then their sum (resultant) is represented by the diagonal passing through that common point.
  • Equivalent to the Triangle Law.

🎙️ This law is particularly useful when two forces or velocities act on an object from the same point. Both the Triangle and Parallelogram laws yield the same resultant vector.

Scalar Multiplication: Definition & Properties

  • Multiplying vector a⃗ by scalar k: k a⃗.
  • Magnitude: |k a⃗| = |k| |a⃗|.
  • Direction: Same as a⃗ if k>0, opposite if k<0. Zero vector if k=0.

🎙️ This operation is straightforward. It essentially stretches or shrinks the vector. A negative scalar acts like a mirror, flipping the vector's direction.

Component Form: 2D and 3D Vectors

  • 2D: a⃗ = x î + y ĵ. Magnitude |a⃗| = √(x² + y²).
  • 3D: a⃗ = x î + y ĵ + z k̂. Magnitude |a⃗| = √(x² + y² + z²).
  • î, ĵ, k̂ are unit vectors along the positive X, Y, Z axes respectively.

🎙️ The unit vectors î, ĵ, k̂ are your fundamental directions. Any vector can be uniquely expressed as a linear combination of these orthogonal unit vectors.

Worked Example: Magnitude & Direction Cosines

  • Given vector a⃗ = 2î - ĵ + 2k̂.
  • Step 1: Find magnitude |a⃗| = √(2² + (-1)² + 2²) = √9 = 3.
  • Step 2: Direction Cosines (l, m, n) are x/|a⃗|, y/|a⃗|, z/|a⃗|.
  • Result: l=2/3, m=-1/3, n=2/3.

🎙️ Direction cosines tell you the cosine of the angle the vector makes with the x, y, and z axes. They are essentially the components of the unit vector in that direction.

Scalar (Dot) Product: How Much Work?

  • When you push a box, only the force component in the direction of motion does 'work'.
  • The dot product tells us 'how much' of one vector acts in the direction of another.

🎙️ The dot product is a way to multiply two vectors to get a scalar quantity. It's often used to find the angle between two vectors or to calculate work done.

Scalar (Dot) Product: Definition & Properties

  • Definition: a⃗ ⋅ b⃗ = |a⃗| |b⃗| cos θ, where θ is the angle between them.
  • Component form: a⃗ ⋅ b⃗ = a₁b₁ + a₂b₂ + a₃b₃.
  • Properties: Commutative (a⃗ ⋅ b⃗ = b⃗ ⋅ a⃗), Distributive.

🎙️ Remember, the result of a dot product is always a scalar. If two vectors are perpendicular (θ=90°), their dot product is zero, as cos 90° = 0.

Vector (Cross) Product: Definition & Properties

  • Definition: a⃗ × b⃗ = (|a⃗| |b⃗| sin θ) n̂, where n̂ is a unit vector perpendicular to both a⃗ and b⃗.
  • Component form: Use determinant method (î(a₂b₃-a₃b₂) - ĵ(a₁b₃-a₃b₁) + k̂(a₁b₂-a₂b₁)).
  • Properties: Not commutative (a⃗ × b⃗ = - b⃗ × a⃗), Distributive.

🎙️ The direction of n̂ is given by the right-hand rule. If two vectors are parallel (θ=0° or 180°), their cross product is the zero vector, as sin 0° = sin 180° = 0.

Common Mistakes in Vector Algebra

  • Confusing Scalar & Vector Products: Dot product gives scalar, cross product gives vector.
  • Incorrectly applying direction: Forgetting 'head-to-tail' in addition, or right-hand rule for cross product.
  • Magnitude errors: Forgetting the square root or squaring negative numbers incorrectly.
  • Treating vectors like scalars: Adding magnitudes without considering direction.

🎙️ Many students lose marks by making these fundamental errors. Always double-check your definitions and the nature of the result you expect (scalar or vector).

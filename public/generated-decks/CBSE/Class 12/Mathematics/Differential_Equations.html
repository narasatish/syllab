Understanding Differential Equations

  • An equation involving derivatives of a dependent variable w.r.t. an independent variable
  • Relates a function to its rates of change
  • Examples: dy/dx = x+y, d²y/dx² + y = 0

🎙️ Think of differential equations as mathematical expressions that describe how things change. They involve derivatives, which represent rates of change. These equations are fundamental in modeling real-world phenomena.

Order of a Differential Equation

  • The order is the highest order of the derivative present in the differential equation
  • It indicates how many times the dependent variable has been differentiated
  • Example: dy/dx + y = x (Order 1)

🎙️ To find the order, simply look for the derivative with the highest power of 'd' in the numerator. For instance, d²y/dx² has an order of 2, while dy/dx has an order of 1. It's about the 'level' of differentiation.

🔢
Highest Derivative

Degree of a Differential Equation

  • The degree is the highest power of the highest order derivative after making the equation free from radicals and fractions of derivatives
  • Only defined if the equation is a polynomial in its derivatives
  • Example: (d²y/dx²)³ + (dy/dx)² + y = 0 (Degree 3)

🎙️ The degree is the power to which the highest order derivative is raised. However, be careful! The equation must first be a polynomial in its derivatives – no fractional or negative powers, and no derivatives inside functions like sin(dy/dx). Always simplify first.

General vs. Particular Solutions

  • General Solution: Contains arbitrary constants, represents a family of curves
  • Particular Solution: Obtained by assigning specific values to arbitrary constants using initial/boundary conditions, represents a unique curve

🎙️ A general solution gives you a whole family of curves, like y = mx + c represents all lines. A particular solution is one specific curve from that family, found when you have extra information, like a point the line passes through.

Forming Differential Equations

  • Identify the arbitrary constants in the given equation
  • Differentiate the equation successively as many times as the number of arbitrary constants
  • Eliminate the arbitrary constants from these equations

🎙️ The goal here is to construct a differential equation from a given family of curves. The number of differentiations you perform should match the number of arbitrary constants you need to eliminate. This process effectively 'removes' the specific constants, leaving a general DE.

1Identify Constants
2Differentiate (N times)
3Eliminate Constants

  • Not eliminating all arbitrary constants completely
  • Algebraic errors during substitution

Solving by Variable Separable

  • A DE of the form dy/dx = f(x)g(y) or f(x)dx + g(y)dy = 0
  • Rearrange to separate x terms with dx and y terms with dy
  • Integrate both sides

🎙️ This is the simplest method. The key is to isolate all 'x' terms and 'dx' on one side and all 'y' terms and 'dy' on the other. Once separated, integrate both sides independently, and don't forget the constant of integration, 'C'.

1Separate Variables
2Integrate Both Sides
3Add Constant (C)

Homogeneous Differential Equations

  • A first-order, first-degree DE is homogeneous if dy/dx = f(y/x)
  • Or if M(x,y)dx + N(x,y)dy = 0 where M and N are homogeneous functions of the same degree
  • A function f(x,y) is homogeneous of degree 'n' if f(λx, λy) = λⁿf(x,y)

🎙️ Homogeneous means 'of the same kind'. In DEs, it refers to functions where all terms have the same total degree. If you substitute λx for x and λy for y, you should be able to factor out λ raised to some power.

Solving Homogeneous DEs

  • Substitute y = vx, so dy/dx = v + x(dv/dx)
  • The equation transforms into a variable separable form in v and x
  • Solve for v, then substitute back v = y/x to get the solution in y and x

🎙️ The clever substitution y = vx is the trick here. This transforms the homogeneous equation into a simpler variable separable form involving v and x, which we already know how to solve. Remember to replace v with y/x at the very end.

1Substitute y=vx
2Separate Variables (v,x)
3Integrate & Substitute back

Linear Differential Equations

  • A first-order DE is linear if it is of the form dy/dx + Py = Q, where P and Q are functions of x or constants
  • Also, dx/dy + P'x = Q', where P' and Q' are functions of y or constants

🎙️ Linear differential equations have a very specific structure. The dependent variable 'y' and its derivative 'dy/dx' appear only in the first degree, and they are not multiplied together or inside any functions. Recognizing this form is key.

📏
Standard Form

Solving Linear DEs: Integrating Factor

  • Find the Integrating Factor (IF) = e^(∫Pdx)
  • Multiply the entire linear DE by the IF
  • The LHS becomes d/dx (y * IF); Integrate both sides: y * IF = ∫(Q * IF)dx + C

🎙️ The integrating factor is a magic multiplier that makes the left-hand side a perfect derivative. Once you've calculated the IF, the solution formula is straightforward. Pay close attention to the integration of Q * IF on the RHS.

1Identify P(x) and Q(x)
2Calculate IF = e^∫Pdx
3Apply Formula: y*IF = ∫(Q*IF)dx + C

DE in Population Growth

  • Model: dP/dt = kP, where P is population, t is time, k is growth rate constant
  • Solution: P(t) = P₀e^(kt)
  • This exponential growth/decay model is widely used

🎙️ This simple first-order DE describes scenarios where the rate of change is directly proportional to the current amount. It leads to exponential growth or decay, depending on the sign of 'k'. This is a very common application you might encounter.

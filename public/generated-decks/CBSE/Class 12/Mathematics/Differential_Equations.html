What is a Differential Equation?

  • An equation involving derivatives.
  • Relates a function to its derivatives.
  • Describes rates of change.

🎙️ Simply put, a differential equation is an equation that includes at least one derivative of an unknown function. It's a powerful tool because it allows us to express relationships between quantities and their rates of change, which are everywhere in nature and technology.

Order of a Differential Equation

  • Highest order derivative present.
  • Determined by the 'highest' differentiation.
  • A positive integer.

🎙️ The order of a differential equation is the order of the highest derivative appearing in the equation. For example, if the highest derivative is d²y/dx², the order is 2. It's always a positive integer, indicating the level of change we are observing.

Degree of a Differential Equation

  • Power of the highest order derivative.
  • Equation must be a polynomial in derivatives.
  • Defined only if all derivatives have integer powers.

🎙️ The degree of a differential equation is the power of the highest order derivative, but only after the equation has been freed from radicals and fractions involving the derivatives. If the equation cannot be expressed as a polynomial in its derivatives, the degree is undefined. This is a common point of confusion.

Worked Example 1: Order & Degree

  • Equation: (d³y/dx³) + 2(d²y/dx²)² - dy/dx + y = 0
  • Highest order derivative: d³y/dx³
  • Order: 3
  • Degree: 1 (power of d³y/dx³)

🎙️ Let's look at an example. In this equation, the highest order derivative is d³y/dx³, making the order 3. Its power is 1, so the degree is 1. Remember, always simplify the equation to a polynomial in derivatives first.

General & Particular Solutions

  • General: Contains arbitrary constants.
  • Represents a family of curves.
  • Particular: Obtained by specific conditions.

🎙️ A general solution to a differential equation includes arbitrary constants, representing a family of curves. A particular solution is derived from the general solution by applying specific initial or boundary conditions, fixing the values of those constants and giving a unique curve.

Formation of a Differential Equation

  • From a given general solution.
  • Eliminate arbitrary constants.
  • Number of constants = Order of DE.

🎙️ We can also work backward! Given a general solution with arbitrary constants, we can form the differential equation by differentiating the solution repeatedly until all arbitrary constants are eliminated. The order of the resulting differential equation will be equal to the number of arbitrary constants.

🛠️
Building Equations

Worked Example 2: Forming a DE

  • Given: y = Ax + B (A, B are constants)
  • Step 1: y' = A
  • Step 2: y'' = 0
  • Result: y'' = 0 is the DE.

🎙️ Consider y = Ax + B. Differentiating once gives y' = A. Differentiating again gives y'' = 0. Here, we eliminated A and B. Since there were two arbitrary constants, the resulting DE is of order 2. This is a straightforward example.

2Differentiate again
3Eliminate constants

Methods of Solving DEs: Variable Separable

  • Rearrange terms to separate variables.
  • Integrate both sides independently.
  • Suitable for dy/dx = f(x)g(y) form.

🎙️ The variable separable method is the simplest technique. We rearrange the equation so that all terms involving 'x' and 'dx' are on one side, and all terms involving 'y' and 'dy' are on the other. Then, we integrate both sides separately to find the solution.

↔️
Separate & Integrate

Worked Example 3: Variable Separable

  • Solve: dy/dx = (1+y²)/(1+x²)
  • Separate: dy/(1+y²) = dx/(1+x²)
  • Integrate: ∫dy/(1+y²) = ∫dx/(1+x²)
  • Solution: tan⁻¹y = tan⁻¹x + C

🎙️ Here's a classic example. We move terms involving y to the left with dy, and terms involving x to the right with dx. Then, we integrate both sides. Don't forget the constant of integration, C, on one side after integrating.

Homogeneous Differential Equations

  • Function f(x,y) is homogeneous of degree n.
  • If f(λx, λy) = λⁿ f(x,y).
  • Can be written as dy/dx = f(y/x).

🎙️ A differential equation is homogeneous if all terms have the same degree. More formally, if dy/dx = f(x,y), then f(x,y) is a homogeneous function of degree zero. This allows us to make a substitution to simplify the equation.

Steps to Solve Homogeneous DEs

  • Check for homogeneity.
  • Substitute y = vx, so dy/dx = v + x(dv/dx).
  • Separate variables (v and x).
  • Integrate and substitute back y/x for v.

🎙️ The key to solving homogeneous DEs is the substitution y = vx. This transforms the equation into a variable separable form involving v and x, which we already know how to solve. After finding v, remember to replace it with y/x to get the solution in terms of y and x.

1Check Homogeneity
2Substitute y=vx
3Separate & Integrate
4Substitute Back

Worked Example 4: Homogeneous DE

  • Solve: dy/dx = (x+y)/x
  • Substitute y=vx: v + x(dv/dx) = (x+vx)/x = 1+v
  • x(dv/dx) = 1 => dv = dx/x
  • Integrate: v = ln|x| + C
  • Solution: y/x = ln|x| + C => y = x(ln|x| + C)

🎙️ Let's apply the steps. After substituting y=vx, the equation simplifies, allowing us to separate v and x. Integrate, and then back-substitute y/x for v. Always be careful with the algebra during substitution.

Linear DEs: dy/dx + Py = Q

  • Form: dy/dx + P(x)y = Q(x).
  • P(x) and Q(x) are functions of x (or constants).
  • y and its derivatives appear linearly (power 1).

🎙️ Linear differential equations are a very important type. They have a specific form: dy/dx plus a function of x multiplied by y, equals another function of x. The key is that y and dy/dx appear with power one, and there are no products of y with its derivatives.

📏
Straightforward Form
18 / 25

Steps to Solve Linear DEs

  • Identify P(x) and Q(x).
  • Calculate Integrating Factor (IF) = e^∫P(x)dx.
  • Multiply equation by IF: y * IF = ∫(Q(x) * IF)dx + C.
  • Solve for y.

🎙️ The method for linear DEs involves an 'integrating factor'. Once you find P(x) and Q(x), calculate the integrating factor. Then, use the formula y * IF = ∫(Q * IF)dx + C. This transforms the left side into the derivative of (y * IF), making integration straightforward.

1Find P & Q
2Compute IF
3Apply Formula
4Solve for y
19 / 25

Worked Example 5: Linear DE

  • Solve: dy/dx + (y/x) = x²
  • P(x) = 1/x, Q(x) = x²
  • IF = e^∫(1/x)dx = e^ln|x| = x
  • y * x = ∫(x² * x)dx + C = ∫x³dx + C
  • Solution: xy = x⁴/4 + C => y = x³/4 + C/x

🎙️ Here, P(x) is 1/x and Q(x) is x². Calculate the integrating factor, which turns out to be x. Multiply the entire equation by x, then integrate the right-hand side. Finally, isolate y to get the general solution. Be careful with integration constants.

Linear DEs: dx/dy + Px = Q

  • Form: dx/dy + P(y)x = Q(y).
  • P(y) and Q(y) are functions of y (or constants).
  • Integrating Factor (IF) = e^∫P(y)dy.
  • Solution: x * IF = ∫(Q(y) * IF)dy + C.

🎙️ Sometimes, the roles of x and y are swapped. The equation might be linear in x, meaning dx/dy + P(y)x = Q(y). The method is analogous: P and Q are functions of y, the integrating factor is with respect to y, and the final solution gives x in terms of y.

Worked Example 6: Linear DE (dx/dy)

  • Solve: (1+y²)dx + (x - e^tan⁻¹y)dy = 0
  • Rearrange: dx/dy + x/(1+y²) = e^tan⁻¹y / (1+y²)
  • P(y) = 1/(1+y²), Q(y) = e^tan⁻¹y / (1+y²)
  • IF = e^∫(1/(1+y²))dy = e^tan⁻¹y
  • x * e^tan⁻¹y = ∫(e^tan⁻¹y * e^tan⁻¹y / (1+y²))dy + C
  • Solution: x * e^tan⁻¹y = (e^(2tan⁻¹y))/2 + C

🎙️ This example requires careful rearrangement to fit the dx/dy + P(y)x = Q(y) form. Once in standard form, identify P(y) and Q(y), calculate the integrating factor, and proceed with the integration. This type is a bit trickier, so practice is essential.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Incorrectly identifying Order/Degree.
  • Forgetting the constant of integration 'C'.
  • Algebraic errors during substitution (Homogeneous).
  • Wrongly calculating the Integrating Factor (Linear).

🎙️ Many students lose marks due to simple errors. Double-check your order and degree, and *never* forget 'C' in indefinite integrals. Be meticulous with algebraic manipulations, especially during substitutions, and ensure your integrating factor is correctly calculated. Practice is key to avoiding these pitfalls.

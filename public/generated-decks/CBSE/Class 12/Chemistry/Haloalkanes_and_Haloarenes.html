Introduction: Halogen Derivatives

  • Organic compounds with halogen atoms replacing hydrogen.
  • Haloalkanes: Halogen attached to an sp3 hybridized carbon.
  • Haloarenes: Halogen attached to an sp2 hybridized carbon of an aromatic ring.

🎙️ These compounds are essentially hydrocarbons where one or more hydrogen atoms have been replaced by halogen atoms. Understanding this fundamental difference in carbon hybridization is key to predicting their properties and reactions.

Classification of Haloalkanes

  • Based on number of halogen atoms: mono-, di-, tri-, polyhalogen compounds.
  • Based on hybridization of carbon: primary (1°), secondary (2°), tertiary (3°).
  • Alkyl, Allylic, Benzylic, Vinylic, Aryl halides.

🎙️ Classification helps us predict their reactivity. Primary, secondary, and tertiary refer to the number of carbon atoms directly attached to the carbon bearing the halogen, influencing steric hindrance and carbocation stability.

IUPAC Naming Conventions

  • Identify the longest carbon chain containing the halogen.
  • Number the chain to give the halogen atom the lowest possible number.
  • Halogens are named as prefixes: fluoro, chloro, bromo, iodo.

🎙️ Correct nomenclature is vital for clear communication in chemistry. Always prioritize the halogen's position when numbering the parent chain, and remember to list substituents alphabetically.

The C-X Bond: Polar & Reactive

  • Halogens are more electronegative than carbon, creating a polar bond (Cδ+ - Xδ-).
  • This polarity makes the carbon atom electrophilic, susceptible to nucleophilic attack.
  • Bond strength and length vary: C-F > C-Cl > C-Br > C-I (strength decreases, length increases).

🎙️ The polarity of the C-X bond is fundamental to the reactivity of haloalkanes. Understanding bond strength and length helps explain trends in their chemical behavior, particularly as leaving groups.

Preparing Haloalkanes: From Alcohols

  • Reaction with Hydrogen Halides (HX): R-OH + HX → R-X + H2O (e.g., HCl, HBr, HI).
  • Reaction with Phosphorus Halides (PCl3, PCl5): R-OH + PCl5 → R-Cl + POCl3 + HCl.
  • Reaction with Thionyl Chloride (SOCl2): R-OH + SOCl2 → R-Cl + SO2 + HCl (Darzen's process).

🎙️ This is a very common and versatile method. Thionyl chloride (SOCl2) is particularly useful as the gaseous by-products (SO2, HCl) escape, leaving a pure alkyl halide product.

Preparing Haloalkanes: From Hydrocarbons

  • Free Radical Halogenation of Alkanes: R-H + X2 (UV light/heat) → R-X + HX (non-selective).
  • Electrophilic Addition to Alkenes: Alkene + HX → Alkyl halide (Markovnikov's rule).
  • Addition of Halogens to Alkenes: Alkene + X2 → Vicinal dihalide.

🎙️ Free radical halogenation is generally non-selective, leading to mixtures of products. Markovnikov's rule is crucial for predicting the major product in addition reactions involving unsymmetrical alkenes.

Halogen Exchange: Finkelstein & Swartz

  • Finkelstein Reaction: R-Cl/Br + NaI → R-I + NaCl/NaBr (in dry acetone).
  • Swartz Reaction: R-Br/Cl + AgF/Hg2F2/CoF2/SbF3 → R-F + metal halide.
  • These are specific methods for preparing iodoalkanes and fluoroalkanes respectively.

🎙️ These are specific methods to prepare iodoalkanes and fluoroalkanes, which are harder to obtain directly. The Finkelstein reaction is driven by the precipitation of NaCl or NaBr in acetone.

Preparing Haloarenes

  • Electrophilic Substitution: Benzene + X2 (Fe/FeX3 catalyst) → Halobenzene + HX.
  • Sandmeyer Reaction: Diazotization of aniline followed by CuX/HX (e.g., CuCl/HCl for chlorobenzene).
  • Gattermann Reaction (variant of Sandmeyer, uses copper powder).

🎙️ Direct halogenation of benzene requires a Lewis acid catalyst. The Sandmeyer reaction is a versatile method for introducing a halogen directly onto an aromatic ring through a diazonium salt intermediate.

Physical Properties: Boiling Point, Density, Solubility

  • Boiling Point: R-I > R-Br > R-Cl > R-F (due to increasing Van der Waals forces with molecular size).
  • Density: R-I > R-Br > R-Cl (increases with atomic mass of halogen).
  • Solubility: Slightly soluble in water, but soluble in organic solvents due to their non-polar nature.

🎙️ The increase in boiling point and density down the group is due to increasing molecular mass and strength of Van der Waals forces. Their polarity is generally not enough to overcome the hydrogen bonding in water.

Chemical Reactions: Haloalkanes - Nucleophilic Substitution

  • The C-X bond is polar, making the carbon atom electrophilic.
  • Halogen is a good leaving group, replaced by a nucleophile.
  • Two main mechanisms: SN1 (unimolecular) and SN2 (bimolecular).

🎙️ This is the most important reaction type for haloalkanes. The electron-deficient carbon atom attached to the halogen is an ideal target for electron-rich species, i.e., nucleophiles.

SN2 Mechanism: Concerted Reaction

  • Bimolecular (rate depends on [RX] and [Nu-]), one-step reaction.
  • Nucleophile attacks from the backside, simultaneously with leaving group departure.
  • Results in inversion of configuration (Walden inversion) if chiral center is present.
  • Favored by primary haloalkanes and strong nucleophiles; polar aprotic solvents.

🎙️ SN2 reactions are favored by primary haloalkanes due to less steric hindrance. The single transition state involves both reactants, leading to an inversion of stereochemistry.

SN1 Mechanism: Two-Step Reaction

  • Unimolecular (rate depends only on [RX]), two-step reaction.
  • Step 1: Slow ionization to form a carbocation intermediate (rate-determining step).
  • Step 2: Fast attack of nucleophile on the planar carbocation.
  • Results in racemization (mixture of enantiomers) if chiral center is present.

🎙️ SN1 reactions are favored by tertiary haloalkanes due to the stability of tertiary carbocations. Polar protic solvents also favor SN1 by stabilizing the carbocation intermediate.

Factors Affecting SN1/SN2

  • Structure of Haloalkane: 3° > 2° > 1° for SN1; 1° > 2° > 3° for SN2.
  • Nature of Nucleophile: Strong nucleophiles favor SN2; weak nucleophiles favor SN1.
  • Nature of Leaving Group: Good leaving groups (weak bases) favor both SN1 and SN2.
  • Solvent: Polar protic solvents favor SN1; polar aprotic solvents favor SN2.

🎙️ Understanding these factors is crucial for predicting the dominant mechanism in a given reaction. Remember the interplay between steric hindrance, carbocation stability, and solvent effects.

Worked Example: Predicting Substitution

  • Question: Predict the major product and mechanism when (CH3)3C-Br reacts with H2O.
  • Answer: Major product is (CH3)3C-OH (tert-butanol).
  • Mechanism: SN1. Tertiary halide forms a stable carbocation, and H2O is a weak nucleophile.

🎙️ This example clearly demonstrates an SN1 reaction. The bulky tertiary halide prevents SN2 attack, and the weak nucleophile (water) allows for carbocation formation as the rate-determining step.

Elimination Reactions: Dehydrohalogenation

  • Haloalkanes react with alcoholic KOH, leading to removal of HX.
  • Hydrogen removed from β-carbon, halogen from α-carbon, forming an alkene.
  • Follows Saytzeff's Rule: The more substituted alkene is the major product.

🎙️ This reaction competes with nucleophilic substitution. Saytzeff's rule is important for predicting the regioselectivity when multiple beta-hydrogens are available for abstraction.

Reactions with Metals: Grignard & Wurtz

  • Grignard Reagents (R-MgX): R-X + Mg → R-MgX (in dry ether). Highly reactive nucleophiles.
  • Wurtz Reaction: 2R-X + 2Na → R-R + 2NaX (in dry ether). Used for coupling alkyl halides.

🎙️ Grignard reagents are extremely versatile synthetic tools, acting as strong nucleophiles and bases in organic synthesis. The Wurtz reaction is useful for synthesizing symmetrical alkanes with an even number of carbon atoms.

Chemical Reactions: Haloarenes - Lower Reactivity

  • Resonance effect: C-X bond acquires partial double bond character, making it stronger.
  • sp2 hybridized carbon: C-X bond is shorter and stronger than in haloalkanes.
  • Phenyl cation instability: Nucleophilic substitution via SN1 is highly unfavorable.

🎙️ Haloarenes are significantly less reactive towards nucleophilic substitution compared to haloalkanes. These three factors collectively explain their reduced reactivity.

Haloarenes: Electrophilic Substitution

  • Halogens are deactivating groups but ortho-para directing.
  • Nitration (conc. HNO3/H2SO4): Forms o- and p-nitrohalobenzenes.
  • Halogenation (X2/FeX3): Forms dihalobenzenes (o- and p-).
  • Friedel-Crafts Alkylation/Acylation (R-Cl/RCOCl + AlCl3).

🎙️ Despite being deactivating, halogens direct incoming electrophiles to ortho and para positions due to resonance stabilization of the intermediate carbocations. This is an important exception to remember.

Haloarenes: Nucleophilic Substitution (Difficult)

  • Requires harsh conditions (high temperature and pressure), e.g., Dow's process to make phenol from chlorobenzene.
  • Presence of strong electron-withdrawing groups (e.g., -NO2) at ortho/para positions activates the ring.
  • Some reactions proceed via a benzyne intermediate under extreme conditions.

🎙️ Direct nucleophilic substitution on haloarenes is challenging due to the factors discussed earlier. Electron-withdrawing groups at ortho/para positions activate the ring towards nucleophilic attack by stabilizing the intermediate carbanion.

Polyhalogen Compounds: Key Examples

  • Dichloromethane (CH2Cl2): Solvent, paint remover, propellant.
  • Chloroform (CHCl3): Solvent, historically an anesthetic, stored in dark bottles.
  • Iodoform (CHI3): Antiseptic (due to liberation of free iodine).
  • Carbon tetrachloride (CCl4): Solvent, fire extinguisher (now restricted).

🎙️ These compounds have multiple halogen atoms and distinct applications. Chloroform must be stored in dark bottles to prevent its oxidation to phosgene, a poisonous gas.

Environmental Concerns: DDT & Freons

  • DDT (Dichlorodiphenyltrichloroethane): Potent insecticide, non-biodegradable, bioaccumulates, banned in many countries.
  • Freons (Chlorofluorocarbons - CFCs): Used as refrigerants, propellants; deplete the stratospheric ozone layer.
  • Understanding the environmental impact is crucial for sustainable chemistry.

🎙️ While highly effective in their initial applications, the environmental persistence and toxicity of compounds like DDT, and the ozone-depleting potential of Freons, led to their regulation and eventual ban worldwide.

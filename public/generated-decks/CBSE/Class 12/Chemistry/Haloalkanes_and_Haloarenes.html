What are Haloalkanes?

  • Alkanes with one or more hydrogen atoms replaced by halogen atoms (F, Cl, Br, I).
  • General formula: R-X, where R is an alkyl group and X is a halogen.
  • Also commonly known as alkyl halides.

🎙️ Haloalkanes are essentially derivatives of alkanes where a hydrogen is substituted by a halogen. Think of methane becoming chloromethane. This substitution changes their properties significantly.

What are Haloarenes?

  • Aromatic compounds where one or more hydrogen atoms on the benzene ring are replaced by halogen atoms.
  • General formula: Ar-X, where Ar is an aryl group and X is a halogen.
  • Also known as aryl halides.

🎙️ Similarly, haloarenes are derivatives of aromatic hydrocarbons like benzene. The halogen is directly attached to the benzene ring, which imparts unique reactivity compared to haloalkanes.

Classification: Based on Number of Halogen Atoms

  • Monohaloalkanes: Contain one halogen atom.
  • Dihaloalkanes: Contain two halogen atoms.
  • Polyhaloalkanes: Contain three or more halogen atoms.

🎙️ We can classify haloalkanes based on how many halogen atoms are present in their structure. Monohaloalkanes are the most common, but di- and polyhalo compounds also exist with diverse applications.

Classification: Compounds with sp³ C-X Bond

  • Alkyl Halides (R-X): Halogen attached to an sp³ carbon of an alkyl group.
  • Allylic Halides: Halogen attached to an sp³ carbon next to a C=C double bond.
  • Benzylic Halides: Halogen attached to an sp³ carbon next to an aromatic ring.

🎙️ This classification is crucial for understanding reactivity. In all these, the carbon atom directly bonded to the halogen is sp³ hybridised, but its neighbours significantly influence its chemical behaviour.

Alkyl Halides: Primary, Secondary, Tertiary

  • Primary (1°): Halogen attached to a carbon bonded to one other carbon atom.
  • Secondary (2°): Halogen attached to a carbon bonded to two other carbon atoms.
  • Tertiary (3°): Halogen attached to a carbon bonded to three other carbon atoms.

🎙️ The degree of alkyl halide (primary, secondary, tertiary) is extremely important, especially when we discuss reaction mechanisms like SN1 and SN2. Make sure you can identify them correctly from their structures.

IUPAC Nomenclature of Haloalkanes

  • Select the longest continuous carbon chain containing the halogen atom.
  • Number the chain from the end closer to the halogen or other substituents.
  • Halogens are treated as prefixes (fluoro, chloro, bromo, iodo) in alphabetical order.

🎙️ Correct IUPAC naming is essential for clear communication in chemistry. Remember the priority rules, especially when multiple substituents are present. Let's look at an example to clarify these rules.

Worked Example: Naming Haloalkanes

  • Name: CH₃-CH(Cl)-CH₂-CH₃
  • Step 1: Longest carbon chain is four carbons, so 'butane'.
  • Step 2: Halogen (Cl) is at carbon 2, numbering from the right.
  • Step 3: The IUPAC name is 2-Chlorobutane.

🎙️ Here's a simple example. Identify the parent alkane, locate the halogen, and number the chain correctly to give the lowest possible numbers to substituents. Practice with more complex structures.

IUPAC Nomenclature of Haloarenes

  • For monohalogenated benzene, use 'halo' prefix (e.g., Chlorobenzene).
  • For dihalogenated benzene, use ortho- (1,2-), meta- (1,3-), para- (1,4-) prefixes or numerical locants.
  • For more than two substituents, use numerical locants (1,2,3- etc.) and alphabetical order.

🎙️ For haloarenes, the positions on the benzene ring are key. Remember the ortho, meta, para system for disubstituted rings, or use numbers for more complex structures. Both systems are frequently tested.

Worked Example: Naming Haloarenes

  • Name: C₆H₄(Cl)₂ (with chloro groups opposite each other)
  • Step 1: Parent aromatic ring is benzene.
  • Step 2: Two chloro groups are present.
  • Step 3: Their positions are 1 and 4. The name is 1,4-Dichlorobenzene or p-Dichlorobenzene.

🎙️ This example demonstrates naming a disubstituted benzene. Both numerical and common (p-) nomenclature are acceptable and frequently used. Always ensure the lowest possible numbers are assigned to substituents.

Nature of the C-X Bond

  • Halogens are more electronegative than carbon.
  • The C-X bond is polar, with carbon having a partial positive charge (δ+) and halogen a partial negative charge (δ-).
  • This polarity makes the carbon susceptible to nucleophilic attack.

🎙️ The polarity of the C-X bond is fundamental to understanding the reactivity of haloalkanes and haloarenes. The partial positive charge on carbon makes it an electrophilic center, crucial for many reactions.

Physical Properties: Boiling Points

  • Higher than parent alkanes due to stronger van der Waals forces (larger size/mass of halogens).
  • Increases with increasing halogen size (I > Br > Cl > F) for a given alkyl group.
  • Increases with increasing alkyl chain length (more surface area for interaction).

🎙️ Boiling points are influenced by intermolecular forces. The increased mass and polarizability of halogens lead to stronger London dispersion forces and dipole-dipole interactions, raising boiling points compared to alkanes.

Physical Properties: Density

  • Generally higher than hydrocarbons due to the heavier halogen atoms.
  • Increases with increasing halogen size (I > Br > Cl) for a given alkyl group.
  • Increases with increasing number of halogen atoms in the molecule.

🎙️ Density follows a similar trend to boiling points. Heavier halogen atoms contribute significantly to the overall molecular mass, leading to higher densities. Many polyhalogenated compounds are denser than water.

Physical Properties: Solubility

  • Poorly soluble in water, despite their polarity.
  • Cannot form strong hydrogen bonds with water molecules.
  • Soluble in non-polar organic solvents due to similar intermolecular forces.

🎙️ Despite being polar, haloalkanes and haloarenes are not very soluble in water. This is because they cannot effectively break the strong hydrogen bonds between water molecules to form new, equally strong interactions.

Methods of Preparation: From Alcohols

  • Reaction with Hydrogen Halides (HX): R-OH + HX → R-X + H₂O.
  • Reaction with Phosphorus Halides (PX₃, PX₅): R-OH + PCl₅ → R-Cl + POCl₃ + HCl.
  • Reaction with Thionyl Chloride (SOCl₂): R-OH + SOCl₂ → R-Cl + SO₂↑ + HCl↑ (Darzen's reaction).

🎙️ Alcohols are versatile starting materials for preparing haloalkanes. Different reagents offer different advantages; for example, thionyl chloride is preferred for alkyl chlorides as gaseous byproducts are easily removed.

Methods of Preparation: From Hydrocarbons (Free Radical Halogenation)

  • Alkanes react with halogens (Cl₂, Br₂) in the presence of UV light or heat.
  • Proceeds via a free radical mechanism.
  • Gives a mixture of isomeric monohaloalkanes and polyhalogenated compounds, making it less suitable for good yields.

🎙️ Free radical halogenation is a classic reaction but often lacks selectivity. It's important to understand the mechanism, but for synthetic purposes, other methods are preferred due to the formation of product mixtures.

Methods of Preparation: From Alkenes (Addition of HX)

  • Alkenes react with hydrogen halides (HCl, HBr, HI) via electrophilic addition.
  • Follows Markovnikov's rule: Hydrogen adds to the carbon with more hydrogen atoms, halogen adds to the carbon with fewer hydrogen atoms.
  • Anti-Markovnikov addition occurs with HBr in the presence of peroxides.

🎙️ Addition of HX to alkenes is a reliable method, especially for preparing secondary and tertiary haloalkanes. Remember Markovnikov's rule for predicting the major product in the absence of peroxides.

Methods of Preparation: Halogen Exchange Reactions

  • Finkelstein Reaction: R-X + NaI → R-I + NaX (in dry acetone). Used for preparing alkyl iodides.
  • Swartz Reaction: R-X + AgF / Hg₂F₂ / CoF₂ / SbF₃ → R-F + metal halide. Used for preparing alkyl fluorides.

🎙️ These are specific named reactions used to prepare alkyl iodides and fluorides, which are harder to synthesize directly. Finkelstein is for iodides using NaI, while Swartz is for fluorides using metallic fluorides.

Reactions of Haloalkanes: Nucleophilic Substitution (SN1 & SN2)

  • Halogen atom is replaced by a nucleophile.
  • SN2: Bimolecular, one step, inversion of configuration, order of reactivity 1° > 2° > 3°.
  • SN1: Unimolecular, two steps (carbocation intermediate), racemisation, order of reactivity 3° > 2° > 1°.

🎙️ Nucleophilic substitution is the most characteristic reaction of haloalkanes. Understanding the differences between SN1 and SN2 mechanisms, including steric hindrance and carbocation stability, is critical for exams.

Reactions of Haloalkanes: Elimination Reactions (E1 & E2)

  • Dehydrohalogenation: Removal of HX from adjacent carbons, forming an alkene.
  • E2: Bimolecular, one step, strong base required.
  • E1: Unimolecular, two steps (carbocation intermediate), weak base can be used. Follows Saytzeff's rule (more substituted alkene is major product).

🎙️ Elimination reactions compete with substitution. The choice between SN1/SN2 and E1/E2 depends on the substrate, nucleophile/base strength, and solvent. Saytzeff's rule is key for predicting the major alkene product.

Applications & Key Takeaways

  • Haloalkanes & Haloarenes are vital as solvents, refrigerants, anesthetics, and in pesticides.
  • Remember classification, IUPAC naming, and the nature of the C-X bond.
  • Key reactions include nucleophilic substitution (SN1/SN2) and elimination (E1/E2).

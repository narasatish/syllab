What are Alcohols? The Hydroxyl Group

  • Organic compounds containing -OH group attached to an alkyl or substituted alkyl group
  • General formula: R-OH
  • -OH group is called the hydroxyl group
  • Derived from hydrocarbons by replacing a hydrogen atom with a hydroxyl group

🎙️ Alcohols are fundamentally characterized by the presence of a hydroxyl group. This functional group dictates many of their chemical and physical properties.

Classification of Alcohols by Hydroxyl Count

  • Monohydric: One -OH group (e.g., Ethanol)
  • Dihydric: Two -OH groups (e.g., Ethane-1,2-diol)
  • Trihydric: Three -OH groups (e.g., Propane-1,2,3-triol)
  • Polyhydric: More than three -OH groups

🎙️ The number of hydroxyl groups present in an alcohol molecule is a primary basis for its classification. This impacts their solubility and reactivity.

Nomenclature of Alcohols: IUPAC Rules

  • Identify the longest carbon chain containing the -OH group
  • Number the chain to give the -OH group the lowest possible number
  • Replace '-e' of alkane with '-ol'
  • Indicate the position of the -OH group by a number

🎙️ Accurate naming is crucial for clear communication in chemistry. The IUPAC system provides a systematic way to name alcohols, avoiding ambiguity.

Preparation of Alcohols: From Alkenes (Hydration)

  • Acid-catalysed hydration: Alkenes react with water in presence of acid (H₂SO₄)
  • Hydroboration-oxidation: Alkenes react with diborane (B₂H₆) followed by oxidation with H₂O₂ in alkaline medium
  • Follows Markovnikov's rule for acid-catalysed hydration
  • Hydroboration-oxidation is anti-Markovnikov addition

🎙️ Alkenes are versatile starting materials for alcohol synthesis. Both direct hydration and hydroboration-oxidation are key methods, each with specific regioselectivity.

Preparation of Alcohols: From Carbonyl Compounds (Reduction)

  • Aldehydes and Ketones: Reduced to primary and secondary alcohols respectively
  • Using reducing agents like LiAlH₄ (Lithium Aluminium Hydride) or NaBH₄ (Sodium Borohydride)
  • Carboxylic Acids and Esters: Reduced to primary alcohols using strong reducing agents like LiAlH₄

🎙️ Carbonyl compounds, including aldehydes, ketones, carboxylic acids, and esters, can be efficiently converted into alcohols through reduction reactions. The choice of reducing agent is critical.

Physical Properties of Alcohols: Boiling Point

  • Higher boiling points than corresponding hydrocarbons, haloalkanes, or ethers
  • Due to intermolecular hydrogen bonding between -OH groups
  • Branching decreases boiling point
  • Size of alkyl group increases boiling point

🎙️ The presence of the hydroxyl group and its ability to form hydrogen bonds significantly impacts the physical properties of alcohols, particularly their boiling points.

Acidity of Alcohols

  • Alcohols are weakly acidic, weaker than water
  • Acidity order: Primary > Secondary > Tertiary
  • Electron-donating groups (alkyl groups) decrease acidity by stabilizing the alkoxide ion less
  • React with active metals (Na, K) to form alkoxides and H₂ gas

🎙️ While often considered neutral, alcohols exhibit weak acidic character. Understanding the factors influencing their acidity is important for predicting reactivity.

Reactions of Alcohols: C-O Bond Cleavage (Dehydration)

  • Alcohols undergo dehydration (elimination of water) to form alkenes
  • Catalyst: Concentrated H₂SO₄ or H₃PO₄
  • Temperature dependent: Primary alcohols require higher temperatures than secondary or tertiary
  • Follows Zaitsev's rule for major product

🎙️ Dehydration is a crucial elimination reaction where alcohols lose a water molecule to form alkenes. The reaction conditions and the type of alcohol influence the product.

Reactions of Alcohols: O-H Bond Cleavage (Esterification)

  • Alcohols react with carboxylic acids or acid derivatives to form esters
  • Reaction is reversible and acid-catalyzed (Fischer Esterification)
  • Water is removed to shift equilibrium to the right
  • Example: Ethanol + Acetic Acid → Ethyl Acetate + Water

🎙️ Esterification is a common reaction of alcohols where the O-H bond breaks, leading to the formation of sweet-smelling esters, often used in perfumes and flavorings.

Worked Example: Alcohol Dehydration Product

  • Question: Predict the major product when 2-methylbutan-2-ol is heated with concentrated H₂SO₄.
  • Solution: 2-methylbutan-2-ol is a tertiary alcohol. Dehydration will occur, following Zaitsev's rule. The major product will be 2-methylbut-2-ene.
  • Common Mistake: Forgetting Zaitsev's rule and forming the less substituted alkene.

🎙️ This example demonstrates how to apply the principles of dehydration and Zaitsev's rule to predict the major product. Always consider the stability of the alkene formed.

What are Phenols? Hydroxyl on Benzene

  • Organic compounds where the -OH group is directly attached to a benzene ring
  • Aromatic compounds
  • Simplest phenol is C₆H₅OH
  • Different from alcohols due to direct attachment to aromatic ring

🎙️ Phenols are distinct from alcohols because their hydroxyl group is directly bonded to an aromatic ring. This structural difference profoundly affects their properties.

Nomenclature of Phenols: Common & IUPAC

Preparation of Phenols: From Haloarenes (Dow's Process)

  • Chlorobenzene is fused with NaOH at 623 K and 320 atm
  • Forms sodium phenoxide, followed by acidification to yield phenol
  • Involves nucleophilic substitution at high temperature and pressure
  • An industrial method for phenol synthesis

🎙️ Dow's process is a significant industrial route for synthesizing phenol from haloarenes, highlighting the conditions needed for such reactions on an aromatic ring.

Preparation of Phenols: From Cumene (Modern Method)

  • Cumene (isopropylbenzene) is oxidized in air to cumene hydroperoxide
  • Cumene hydroperoxide is then treated with dilute acid
  • Yields phenol and acetone as a valuable byproduct
  • Most important industrial method today

Acidity of Phenols: Stronger than Alcohols

  • Phenols are more acidic than alcohols and even water
  • Due to resonance stabilization of the phenoxide ion
  • Electron-withdrawing groups (e.g., -NO₂) increase acidity
  • Electron-donating groups (e.g., -CH₃) decrease acidity

🎙️ The direct attachment of the hydroxyl group to the benzene ring in phenols leads to a significantly higher acidity compared to alcohols, a key distinction.

Reactions of Phenols: Electrophilic Substitution (Nitration)

  • -OH group is ortho- and para- directing and activating
  • Phenols react with dilute HNO₃ at low temperature to form o-nitrophenol and p-nitrophenol
  • With concentrated HNO₃, it forms 2,4,6-trinitrophenol (picric acid)

🎙️ The hydroxyl group makes the benzene ring in phenols highly activated towards electrophilic substitution, directing incoming groups to ortho and para positions.

Reactions of Phenols: Kolbe's Reaction

  • Phenol reacts with sodium hydroxide to form sodium phenoxide
  • Sodium phenoxide then reacts with carbon dioxide under pressure (4-7 atm) and temperature (398 K)
  • Followed by acidification, it yields salicylic acid (o-hydroxybenzoic acid)
  • Important for Aspirin synthesis

🎙️ Kolbe's reaction is a classic example of carboxylation of phenoxide, leading to salicylic acid, a crucial intermediate for many pharmaceuticals like Aspirin.

What are Ethers? The Oxygen Bridge

  • Organic compounds in which an oxygen atom is bonded to two alkyl or aryl groups
  • General formula: R-O-R', where R and R' can be same or different
  • Considered derivatives of water where both hydrogen atoms are replaced by alkyl/aryl groups

🎙️ Ethers are characterized by an oxygen atom acting as a bridge between two hydrocarbon groups. This unique structure gives them distinct properties and uses.

Nomenclature of Ethers: Common & IUPAC

  • Common name: Name alkyl/aryl groups alphabetically, followed by 'ether' (e.g., Diethyl ether)
  • IUPAC name: Smaller alkyl group + oxygen = 'alkoxy' group, then name the larger alkyl group as the parent alkane (e.g., Ethoxyethane)
  • Example: CH₃OCH₂CH₃ is Methoxyethane

🎙️ Ethers have both common and IUPAC naming systems. For the exam, it's important to be comfortable with both, especially converting between them.

Preparation of Ethers: Williamson Synthesis

  • Alkyl halide reacts with sodium alkoxide (or phenoxide)
  • Best results with primary alkyl halides
  • Secondary and tertiary alkyl halides tend to undergo elimination (E2) instead of substitution (SN2)
  • Asymmetric ethers can be prepared

🎙️ Williamson synthesis is a versatile and important method for preparing both symmetrical and asymmetrical ethers. The choice of alkyl halide is crucial to avoid unwanted side reactions.

Properties of Ethers: Physical & Chemical

  • Physical: Lower boiling points than alcohols (no H-bonding)
  • Soluble in water to a small extent
  • Chemical: Generally unreactive
  • Cleavage of C-O bond by hot concentrated HI or HBr; Forms alkyl halides and alcohols (or phenols)

🎙️ Ethers are relatively inert compared to alcohols and phenols. Their lack of hydrogen bonding affects their physical properties, while their C-O bond can be cleaved under strong acidic conditions.

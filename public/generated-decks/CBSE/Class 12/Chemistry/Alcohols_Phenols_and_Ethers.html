Alcohols: The Hydroxyl Group

  • Organic compounds with -OH group attached to an alkyl or substituted alkyl group.
  • General formula: R-OH.
  • Classified based on the number of hydroxyl groups and the carbon attached.

🎙️ Alcohols are essentially hydrocarbons where one or more hydrogen atoms are replaced by a hydroxyl group. This -OH group is what defines their characteristic properties.

Classification of Alcohols

  • Monohydric: One -OH group (e.g., Ethanol).
  • Dihydric: Two -OH groups (e.g., Ethane-1,2-diol).
  • Trihydric: Three -OH groups (e.g., Propane-1,2,3-triol).

🎙️ We classify alcohols based on the number of hydroxyl groups present in their structure. This simple distinction significantly impacts their physical and chemical behavior.

Primary, Secondary, Tertiary Alcohols

  • Primary (1°): -OH attached to a 1° carbon.
  • Secondary (2°): -OH attached to a 2° carbon.
  • Tertiary (3°): -OH attached to a 3° carbon.

🎙️ This classification is crucial as it dictates the reactivity of the alcohol, particularly in oxidation and dehydration reactions. Understanding the degree of carbon atom is key.

IUPAC Naming of Alcohols

  • Longest carbon chain containing -OH.
  • Number chain to give -OH lowest number.
  • Replace '-e' of alkane with '-ol'.

🎙️ The IUPAC system provides a systematic way to name alcohols, ensuring clarity and consistency worldwide. Remember to prioritize the hydroxyl group's position.

Phenols: Aromatic Hydroxyl Compounds

  • Hydroxyl group directly attached to an aromatic ring.
  • Simplest phenol is C₆H₅OH.
  • Different from aromatic alcohols (e.g., benzyl alcohol).

🎙️ Phenols are distinct from alcohols because the -OH group is directly bonded to a benzene ring. This direct attachment gives phenols unique properties, especially their acidity.

Naming Phenols

  • Parent compound is 'phenol'.
  • Substituents named with numbers (ortho, meta, para).
  • Examples: 2-Methylphenol (o-Cresol), 3-Nitrophenol (m-Nitrophenol).

🎙️ For substituted phenols, we use 'phenol' as the base name. Remember the ortho, meta, para prefixes for disubstituted benzene rings, which are also common names.

Ethers: The 'Bridge' Molecules

  • Organic compounds with an oxygen atom bonded to two alkyl or aryl groups.
  • General formula: R-O-R' or Ar-O-Ar'.
  • Act as solvents and intermediates.

🎙️ Ethers are characterized by an oxygen atom linking two hydrocarbon groups. They are commonly used as solvents due to their relatively unreactive nature.

🌉
Oxygen Bridge

Ether Types & Naming

  • Simple (Symmetrical) Ethers: R = R' (e.g., Diethyl ether).
  • Mixed (Unsymmetrical) Ethers: R ≠ R' (e.g., Ethyl methyl ether).
  • IUPAC: Alkoxyalkane (smaller alkyl group + 'oxy').

🎙️ Ethers can be symmetrical or unsymmetrical. Their IUPAC naming involves identifying the smaller alkyl group as an 'alkoxy' substituent and the larger as the parent alkane.

Isomerism in Functional Groups

  • Functional Isomerism: Alcohols and Ethers (CnH2n+2O).
  • Positional Isomerism: Alcohols (e.g., Propan-1-ol vs Propan-2-ol).
  • Metamerism: Ethers (e.g., Diethyl ether vs Methyl propyl ether).

🎙️ These functional groups exhibit various types of isomerism. Alcohols and ethers, for example, can be functional group isomers, sharing the same molecular formula but different functional groups.

Alcohol Synthesis: From Alkenes

  • Acid-catalyzed hydration: Markovnikov's rule, carbocation intermediate.
  • Hydroboration-oxidation: Anti-Markovnikov addition, syn addition.
  • Both methods yield different products based on alkene structure.

🎙️ Alkenes are versatile starting materials for alcohol synthesis. Acid-catalyzed hydration gives Markovnikov products, while hydroboration-oxidation gives anti-Markovnikov products.

Alcohol Synthesis: From Carbonyls

  • Reduction of Aldehydes & Ketones: Using LiAlH₄ or NaBH₄.
  • Aldehydes give primary alcohols.
  • Ketones give secondary alcohols.

🎙️ Aldehydes and ketones can be reduced to alcohols using common reducing agents. The type of alcohol formed depends on whether you start with an aldehyde or a ketone.

Alcohol Synthesis: Grignard Reagents

  • Reaction with aldehydes/ketones/esters followed by hydrolysis.
  • Formaldehyde gives primary alcohols.
  • Other aldehydes give secondary alcohols, ketones give tertiary alcohols.

🎙️ Grignard reagents are powerful tools for forming new carbon-carbon bonds and are particularly useful for synthesizing primary, secondary, and tertiary alcohols with specific carbon skeletons.

Ether Synthesis: Williamson Synthesis

  • Reaction of an alkyl halide with sodium alkoxide or phenoxide.
  • SN2 mechanism, best with primary alkyl halides.
  • Avoid tertiary alkyl halides due to elimination.

🎙️ The Williamson synthesis is a highly versatile method for preparing both symmetrical and unsymmetrical ethers. It's crucial to use a primary alkyl halide to minimize side reactions.

Boiling Points: Alcohols vs Ethers

  • Alcohols have higher boiling points than ethers of comparable molar mass.
  • Reason: Intermolecular hydrogen bonding in alcohols.
  • Ethers only have weaker dipole-dipole interactions.

🎙️ The presence of the highly electronegative oxygen and hydrogen in the -OH group allows alcohols to form strong intermolecular hydrogen bonds, significantly raising their boiling points compared to ethers.

Solubility in Water

  • Lower alcohols and ethers are soluble in water.
  • Reason: Ability to form hydrogen bonds with water molecules.
  • Solubility decreases with increasing size of the hydrocarbon part.

🎙️ The ability to form hydrogen bonds with water molecules is key to their solubility. However, as the non-polar alkyl chain gets longer, it dominates, reducing overall solubility.

Acidity: Alcohols vs Phenols

  • Phenols are more acidic than alcohols due to resonance stabilization of phenoxide ion.
  • Alcohols are weaker acids than water.
  • Electron-withdrawing groups increase acidity, electron-donating groups decrease it.

🎙️ This is a very important concept. The stability of the conjugate base, the phenoxide ion, due to resonance makes phenols significantly more acidic than alcohols. Remember the effect of substituents!

Alcohols: The Hydroxy Cousins of Water

  • Alcohols are organic compounds containing a hydroxyl (-OH) group.
  • The -OH group is attached to an alkyl group (R).
  • Think of them as water (H-O-H) where one H is replaced by an R group (R-O-H).

🎙️ Just as water is essential for life, alcohols are fundamental building blocks in organic chemistry. Their structure is very similar to water, which influences many of their properties.

Classification of Alcohols: How Many OHs?

  • Monohydric Alcohols: Contain one -OH group (e.g., Ethanol).
  • Dihydric Alcohols: Contain two -OH groups (e.g., Ethane-1,2-diol, Glycol).
  • Trihydric Alcohols: Contain three -OH groups (e.g., Propane-1,2,3-triol, Glycerol).

🎙️ This classification is straightforward and based purely on the count of hydroxyl groups. The number of -OH groups significantly impacts their physical properties like boiling point and solubility.

Monohydric Alcohols: Primary, Secondary, Tertiary

  • Primary (1°): -OH attached to a primary carbon (bonded to one other C).
  • Secondary (2°): -OH attached to a secondary carbon (bonded to two other C atoms).
  • Tertiary (3°): -OH attached to a tertiary carbon (bonded to three other C atoms).

🎙️ This sub-classification for monohydric alcohols is crucial as it dictates their reactivity, especially towards oxidation and dehydration reactions. Understanding the carbon atom's connectivity is key.

Nomenclature of Alcohols: The IUPAC Way

  • Identify the longest continuous carbon chain containing the -OH group.
  • Number the chain such that the carbon bearing the -OH group gets the lowest possible number.
  • Replace the '-e' of the corresponding alkane name with '-ol'.
  • Indicate the position of the -OH group by its number before the '-ol' suffix.

🎙️ IUPAC naming provides a systematic way to name alcohols, ensuring clarity and consistency worldwide. Practice is essential to master these rules and avoid common errors.

1Longest C-chain with -OH
2Number chain (OH gets lowest)
3Replace '-e' of alkane with '-ol'
4Specify position of -OH

Worked Example: Naming Alcohols

  • CH₃CH₂CH₂OH: Propan-1-ol (common name: n-propyl alcohol)
  • CH₃CH(OH)CH₃: Propan-2-ol (common name: isopropyl alcohol)
  • (CH₃)₃COH: 2-Methylpropan-2-ol (common name: t-butyl alcohol)

🎙️ Let's apply the IUPAC rules to these examples. Notice how the position of the hydroxyl group is indicated, and for branched chains, substituents are named and numbered correctly.

Preparation of Alcohols: From Alkenes

  • Acid-catalysed Hydration: Alkenes react with water in the presence of an acid catalyst (H₂SO₄) following Markovnikov's rule.
  • Hydroboration-oxidation: Alkenes react with diborane (B₂H₆) followed by oxidation with H₂O₂ in alkaline medium, following anti-Markovnikov's rule.

🎙️ These two methods are crucial for synthesizing alcohols from alkenes, allowing us to control the position of the hydroxyl group based on Markovnikov's or anti-Markovnikov's addition.

1Acid-catalysed hydration
2Hydroboration-oxidation

Preparation of Alcohols: From Carbonyl Compounds

  • Reduction of Aldehydes: Yields primary alcohols (e.g., using NaBH₄ or LiAlH₄).
  • Reduction of Ketones: Yields secondary alcohols (e.g., using NaBH₄ or LiAlH₄).
  • Reduction of Carboxylic Acids & Esters: Yields primary alcohols (stronger reducing agent like LiAlH₄ needed).

🎙️ Carbonyl compounds are versatile precursors for alcohols. The choice of reducing agent and the specific carbonyl compound determines the type of alcohol formed.

Preparation of Alcohols: Grignard Reagents

  • Formaldehyde + RMgX → Primary alcohol.
  • Other Aldehydes + RMgX → Secondary alcohol.
  • Ketones + RMgX → Tertiary alcohol.

🎙️ Grignard reagents (RMgX) are truly magical in organic synthesis, allowing for the formation of new carbon-carbon bonds and the synthesis of all three types of alcohols by reacting with different carbonyl compounds.

Phenols: Aromatic Hydroxyls

  • Phenols are compounds where the hydroxyl (-OH) group is directly attached to an aromatic ring (benzene ring).
  • They are distinct from alcohols due to the direct attachment to the sp² hybridised carbon of the benzene ring.
  • The simplest phenol is C₆H₅OH, known simply as phenol.

🎙️ The direct attachment of the -OH group to the aromatic ring gives phenols unique electronic properties, especially regarding their acidity and reactivity towards electrophilic substitution.

Nomenclature of Phenols: Common & IUPAC

  • The simplest member is named 'Phenol'.
  • Substituted phenols are named as derivatives of phenol, numbering the carbon atoms of the ring starting from the carbon bearing the -OH group.
  • Common names like 'cresols' (methylphenols) and 'catechol' (benzene-1,2-diol) are frequently used.

🎙️ While IUPAC naming is systematic, many phenols are still commonly referred to by their trivial names. Understanding both is important for exam purposes.

Preparation of Phenols: From Haloarenes (Dow's Process)

  • Chlorobenzene is heated with aqueous NaOH at 623 K and 300 atm pressure.
  • This forms sodium phenoxide, which is then acidified with dilute HCl.
  • Yields phenol. This is a harsh industrial process.

🎙️ Dow's process highlights the difficulty in substituting a halogen directly attached to an aromatic ring, requiring extreme conditions due to the partial double bond character of the C-Cl bond.

2Sodium phenoxide formation

Preparation of Phenols: From Diazonium Salts

  • Aniline is treated with nitrous acid (NaNO₂ + HCl) at 0-5°C to form benzene diazonium chloride.
  • The diazonium salt is then hydrolysed by warming with water (H₂O, warm).
  • This yields phenol, nitrogen gas, and HCl. A good laboratory method.

🎙️ This method is particularly useful in the laboratory because diazonium salts are highly reactive and can be converted into various substituted aromatic compounds, including phenols.

Preparation of Phenols: From Cumene

  • Cumene (isopropylbenzene) is oxidised in the presence of air to cumene hydroperoxide.
  • Cumene hydroperoxide is then treated with dilute acid.
  • This gives phenol and acetone. It is the major industrial method for phenol production.

🎙️ The cumene process is highly significant industrially due to its efficiency and the co-production of acetone, a valuable solvent. It's an important reaction to remember for exams.

Acidity Battle: Phenols vs. Alcohols

  • Phenols are significantly more acidic than alcohols and even water.
  • The phenoxide ion (conjugate base of phenol) is resonance stabilised.
  • The negative charge is delocalised over the benzene ring, making the phenoxide ion more stable.
  • Alcohols lack this resonance stabilisation for their alkoxide ions.

🎙️ Understanding the resonance stabilisation of the phenoxide ion is crucial for explaining why phenols are acidic. This is a frequently asked conceptual question in exams.

Water (Weakly Acidic)
Alcohol (Very Weakly Acidic)

Electrophilic Substitution in Phenols

  • The -OH group is an ortho- and para-directing group.
  • It strongly activates the benzene ring towards electrophilic aromatic substitution.
  • Examples: Nitration, Halogenation (e.g., bromination with bromine water), Sulphonation, Kolbe's Reaction, Reimer-Tiemann Reaction.

🎙️ The activating nature of the hydroxyl group means phenols react readily with electrophiles. Remember the specific conditions for each reaction, as they can vary.

Ethers: The Alkoxy World

  • Ethers are organic compounds with an oxygen atom bonded to two alkyl or aryl groups (R-O-R').
  • They can be seen as derivatives of water where both hydrogen atoms are replaced by R or Ar groups.
  • Ethers are generally quite unreactive and are often used as solvents.

🎙️ Ethers are characterised by the presence of an oxygen atom linking two organic groups. Their relative inertness makes them excellent solvents for many organic reactions.

Classification & Nomenclature of Ethers

  • Simple (Symmetrical) Ethers: Both alkyl/aryl groups attached to oxygen are identical (e.g., Diethyl ether).
  • Mixed (Unsymmetrical) Ethers: The alkyl/aryl groups attached to oxygen are different (e.g., Ethyl methyl ether).
  • IUPAC Naming: Named as 'alkoxyalkanes', where the smaller alkyl group forms the 'alkoxy' part.

🎙️ For IUPAC naming, identify the larger alkyl group as the parent alkane and the smaller alkyl group along with the oxygen as the alkoxy substituent. Common names are also widely used.

Preparation of Ethers: Williamson Synthesis

  • Reaction of an alkoxide (R-O⁻Na⁺) with a primary alkyl halide (R'-X).
  • This is an SN2 reaction, so the alkyl halide must be primary to avoid elimination.
  • Versatile method for preparing both symmetrical and unsymmetrical ethers.

🎙️ Williamson synthesis is a crucial named reaction. Remember the SN2 mechanism and the requirement for a primary alkyl halide to ensure ether formation over alkene formation.

1Alkoxide ion (R-O⁻Na⁺)
2Primary alkyl halide (R'-X)
3SN2 reaction to form R-O-R'

Preparation of Ethers: Dehydration of Alcohols

  • Primary alcohols undergo intermolecular dehydration in the presence of concentrated H₂SO₄.
  • Temperature control is critical: 413 K (140°C) for ether formation.
  • At higher temperatures (443 K), alkenes are formed via intramolecular dehydration.

🎙️ This method is good for preparing symmetrical ethers. The precise temperature control is vital to direct the reaction towards ether formation rather than alkene formation.

Chemical Properties of Ethers: Cleavage by HI/HBr

  • Ethers are generally unreactive, but they undergo cleavage of the C-O bond.
  • Reaction occurs with hot, concentrated hydrohalic acids (HI or HBr).
  • Products are alkyl halides and alcohols (or phenols).
  • The order of reactivity of hydrogen halides is HI > HBr > HCl.

🎙️ This is one of the few important reactions ethers undergo. The mechanism of cleavage depends on the nature of the alkyl groups, often involving SN1 or SN2 pathways.

Common Mistakes to Avoid!

  • Confusing acidity order: Phenol > Water > Alcohol.
  • Using tertiary alkyl halides in Williamson synthesis (leads to elimination, not ether).
  • Incorrectly applying Markovnikov's vs. Anti-Markovnikov's rules for alkene hydration.
  • Forgetting resonance stabilization in phenols when explaining acidity.

🎙️ These are frequently observed errors in exams. A thorough understanding of reaction mechanisms and underlying principles will help you avoid these pitfalls and score better.

What is Electrochemistry?

  • Branch of chemistry studying the interconversion of electrical and chemical energy.
  • Deals with reactions involving electron transfer (redox reactions).
  • Focuses on processes occurring in electrochemical cells.

🎙️ Electrochemistry is fundamentally about energy transformation. It investigates how chemical reactions can generate electricity and, conversely, how electrical energy can drive non-spontaneous chemical reactions. The core of these processes lies in redox reactions, where electrons are transferred between species.

Electrochemical Cells: The Basics

  • Devices that convert chemical energy into electrical energy or vice-versa.
  • Consist of two electrodes dipped in an electrolyte.
  • Two main types: Galvanic (Voltaic) and Electrolytic cells.

🎙️ An electrochemical cell is the fundamental unit where these energy conversions take place. It typically involves two electrodes, usually metals, immersed in an ionic solution called an electrolyte. We categorize these cells based on the direction of energy conversion: Galvanic cells generate electricity, while Electrolytic cells consume it.

Construction of a Galvanic Cell

  • Two half-cells: each with an electrode in an electrolyte.
  • Electrodes connected externally by a wire and a voltmeter.
  • Electrolyte solutions connected by a salt bridge.

🎙️ A galvanic cell is built from two distinct half-cells. Each half-cell contains an electrode immersed in its respective electrolyte solution. These electrodes are then connected externally to allow electron flow, and the two electrolyte solutions are connected internally by a salt bridge to maintain electrical neutrality.

Working of a Daniell Cell

  • Anode (oxidation): Zinc loses electrons (Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻).
  • Cathode (reduction): Copper ions gain electrons (Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu).
  • Electrons flow from anode to cathode through the external circuit.

🎙️ In a Daniell cell, the zinc electrode acts as the anode, where oxidation occurs, releasing electrons. These electrons travel through the external wire to the copper electrode, which is the cathode. Here, copper ions from the solution accept these electrons and get reduced, depositing as copper metal. This electron flow constitutes the electric current.

The Salt Bridge: Its Crucial Role

  • Connects the two half-cell solutions.
  • Contains an inert electrolyte (e.g., KCl, KNO₃ in agar-agar gel).
  • Maintains electrical neutrality in the half-cells by allowing ion migration.

🎙️ The salt bridge is indispensable for the continuous operation of a galvanic cell. As reactions proceed, charge builds up in each half-cell – excess positive ions at the anode and depleted positive ions at the cathode. The salt bridge allows inert ions to migrate, neutralizing these charges and ensuring the electron flow continues by completing the internal circuit.

Cell Representation (IUPAC Convention)

  • Anode (oxidation) on the left, Cathode (reduction) on the right.
  • Single vertical line (|) for phase boundary (electrode-electrolyte).
  • Double vertical line (||) for salt bridge.
  • Example: Zn(s) | Zn²⁺(aq, C₁) || Cu²⁺(aq, C₂) | Cu(s).

🎙️ To uniformly describe electrochemical cells, we use a standard IUPAC notation. The anode, where oxidation occurs, is always written on the left, followed by its electrolyte. The cathode, where reduction occurs, is on the right. A single line denotes a phase boundary, and a double line represents the salt bridge, indicating the separation of the two half-cells while maintaining electrical contact.

Worked Example 1: Cell Representation

  • Problem: Represent the cell formed by Mg electrode in 0.1 M MgSO₄ and Ag electrode in 0.01 M AgNO₃. Given: Mg is more reactive than Ag.
  • Solution: Mg will be oxidized (anode), Ag⁺ will be reduced (cathode).
  • Cell: Mg(s) | Mg²⁺(aq, 0.1 M) || Ag⁺(aq, 0.01 M) | Ag(s).

🎙️ Let's apply the IUPAC convention. Since Magnesium is more reactive, it will lose electrons and act as the anode. Silver ions will gain electrons and form the cathode. Remember to include the concentrations of the electrolytes. This representation clearly outlines the components and their states within the electrochemical cell.

Electrode Potential

  • Potential difference developed between an electrode and its electrolyte solution.
  • Arises due to charge separation at the interface.
  • Standard Electrode Potential (E°): Measured under standard conditions (298 K, 1 atm, 1 M concentration).

🎙️ When a metal electrode is immersed in its ion solution, a potential difference is established at the interface. This is known as the electrode potential. It's a measure of the tendency of an electrode to gain or lose electrons. To allow for comparison, we define the standard electrode potential, measured under specific, reproducible conditions.

Standard Hydrogen Electrode (SHE)

  • Reference electrode used to measure standard electrode potentials of other electrodes.
  • Consists of a platinum electrode in 1 M H⁺ solution, with H₂ gas at 1 atm.
  • Its standard electrode potential is arbitrarily assigned as zero (E° = 0.00 V).

🎙️ It's impossible to measure the absolute electrode potential of a single half-cell. Therefore, we need a reference. The Standard Hydrogen Electrode, or SHE, serves this purpose. By convention, its potential is set to zero, allowing us to measure and compare the potentials of all other electrodes relative to it.

Measuring Electrode Potential

  • Connect the half-cell of interest to a SHE.
  • Measure the potential difference using a voltmeter.
  • The measured potential difference is the standard electrode potential of the unknown half-cell.

🎙️ To measure the standard electrode potential of any half-cell, we construct a galvanic cell by pairing it with a SHE. The potential difference recorded by the voltmeter is then directly the standard electrode potential of the unknown electrode, since the SHE's potential is zero. This simple method allows us to build the electrochemical series.

Electrochemical Series

  • Arrangement of elements in increasing order of their standard reduction potentials.
  • Helps predict the relative oxidizing and reducing strengths of substances.
  • Elements higher in the series are stronger oxidizing agents; lower are stronger reducing agents.

🎙️ The electrochemical series is a powerful tool. It's essentially a list of standard reduction potentials, ordered from most negative to most positive. This series allows us to predict the spontaneity of redox reactions, determining which species will be oxidized and which will be reduced when brought together. A higher position indicates a greater tendency to get reduced.

Nernst Equation: Non-Standard Conditions

  • Relates electrode potential to concentration of ions and temperature.
  • E = E° - (RT/nF) ln Q OR E = E° - (0.0592/n) log Q (at 298 K).
  • Q = reaction quotient; n = number of electrons transferred; F = Faraday constant.

🎙️ While standard electrode potentials are useful, most reactions don't occur under standard conditions. The Nernst equation allows us to calculate the electrode potential, or cell potential, under non-standard conditions, taking into account the actual concentrations of reactants and products, and temperature. It's a cornerstone for understanding real-world electrochemical systems.

Worked Example 2: Nernst Equation

  • Problem: Calculate the electrode potential of a Cu electrode dipped in 0.1 M CuSO₄ solution at 298 K. Given E°(Cu²⁺/Cu) = +0.34 V.
  • Solution: Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu. n=2. Q = 1/[Cu²⁺] = 1/0.1 = 10.
  • E = E° - (0.0592/n) log Q = 0.34 - (0.0592/2) log(10) = 0.34 - 0.0296 = 0.3104 V.

🎙️ Here, we apply the Nernst equation to find the electrode potential under a non-standard concentration. Notice how the potential changes from the standard value due to the altered concentration of copper ions. This demonstrates the practical application of the Nernst equation in predicting cell behavior under various conditions.

Concentration Cells

  • Galvanic cells where both half-cells have the same electrodes but different electrolyte concentrations.
  • Potential arises solely due to the difference in concentration.
  • Electrons flow from the lower concentration half-cell to the higher concentration one.

🎙️ Concentration cells are a special type of galvanic cell where the driving force for electron flow isn't different chemical species, but rather a difference in the concentration of the same species. The system tries to equalize these concentrations, leading to a spontaneous flow of electrons and generation of a small potential difference. This is a direct application of the Nernst equation.

Electrolytic Cells

  • Convert electrical energy into chemical energy.
  • Non-spontaneous redox reactions are driven by external electrical energy.
  • Anode is positive, Cathode is negative (opposite of galvanic cells).

🎙️ In contrast to galvanic cells, electrolytic cells require an external power source to operate. They force non-spontaneous chemical reactions to occur by supplying electrical energy. Think of electroplating or the production of elements like sodium and chlorine from molten salt. The polarity of electrodes is also reversed compared to galvanic cells.

Electrolysis: Driving Non-Spontaneous Reactions

  • Process of chemical decomposition by passing electric current through an electrolyte.
  • Ions migrate to oppositely charged electrodes.
  • Example: Electrolysis of molten NaCl produces Na metal and Cl₂ gas.

🎙️ Electrolysis is the core process occurring in an electrolytic cell. It involves breaking down a compound using electricity. For instance, in molten sodium chloride, Na⁺ ions move to the cathode and get reduced to sodium metal, while Cl⁻ ions move to the anode and get oxidized to chlorine gas. This is how many reactive elements are industrially produced.

Faraday's First Law of Electrolysis

  • The mass of a substance deposited or liberated at any electrode is directly proportional to the quantity of electricity passed through the electrolyte.
  • W ∝ Q OR W = ZQ = ZIt.
  • Z is the electrochemical equivalent (mass deposited by 1 Coulomb of charge).

🎙️ Faraday's laws quantify the relationship between electricity and the amount of substance produced or consumed during electrolysis. The first law states that the more charge you pass through an electrolytic cell, the more material will be deposited or liberated at the electrodes. This is a direct proportionality, with Z being the constant of proportionality.

Faraday's Second Law of Electrolysis

  • When the same quantity of electricity is passed through different electrolytes connected in series, the masses of the substances deposited/liberated are proportional to their chemical equivalent weights.
  • W₁/W₂ = E₁/E₂.
  • Chemical equivalent weight = Molar mass / n-factor (valency).

🎙️ Faraday's second law extends the concept to multiple electrolytic cells connected in series, meaning the same quantity of charge passes through each. It states that the masses deposited will be in the ratio of their chemical equivalent weights. This law is crucial for understanding how different substances react to the same amount of electrical input.

Worked Example 3: Faraday's Law

  • Problem: A solution of AgNO₃ is electrolyzed for 10 minutes with a current of 1.5 amperes. What mass of silver is deposited? (Molar mass of Ag = 108 g/mol, F = 96485 C/mol).
  • Solution: Q = It = 1.5 A × (10 × 60) s = 900 C.
  • Ag⁺ + e⁻ → Ag. 1 mol Ag (108g) requires 1 mol e⁻ (96485 C).
  • Mass of Ag = (108 g / 96485 C) × 900 C = 1.006 g.

🎙️ This example demonstrates how to apply Faraday's first law. We first calculate the total charge passed, then use the stoichiometry of the reduction reaction and Faraday's constant to determine the mass of silver deposited. Remember that 1 mole of electrons carries a charge of 1 Faraday. Pay attention to units and conversions, especially time to seconds.

Corrosion: An Electrochemical Phenomenon

  • Slow eating away of a metal due to its reaction with environment (air, moisture).
  • Electrochemical process: Anodic (oxidation of metal) and Cathodic (reduction of O₂/H⁺) reactions.
  • Example: Rusting of iron (Fe → Fe²⁺ → Fe₂O₃.xH₂O).

🎙️ Corrosion is a natural, destructive electrochemical process. It's essentially an uncontrolled oxidation of metals, often involving water and oxygen. Rusting of iron is the most common example, where iron acts as the anode, and oxygen in the presence of water acts as the cathode. Understanding its electrochemical nature is key to preventing it.

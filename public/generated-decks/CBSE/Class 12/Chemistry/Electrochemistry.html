What is Electrochemistry?

  • Branch of chemistry dealing with the interconversion of chemical and electrical energy.
  • Studies reactions involving electron transfer (redox reactions).
  • Chemical energy transformed into electrical energy (e.g., batteries).
  • Electrical energy used to drive non-spontaneous chemical reactions (e.g., electrolysis).

🎙️ Electrochemistry is fundamentally about energy transformation. It's the science behind why your phone battery works and how we can produce elements like aluminum from their ores. We'll be focusing heavily on redox reactions, so a quick recall of Class 11 concepts will be beneficial.

Electrochemical Cells: The Basics

  • Devices that convert chemical energy to electrical energy or vice-versa.
  • Two main types: Galvanic (Voltaic) Cells and Electrolytic Cells.
  • Involve redox reactions occurring at two separate electrodes.
  • Key components: Anode (oxidation) and Cathode (reduction).

🎙️ At the heart of electrochemistry are electrochemical cells. These are essentially two half-cells connected, where electron transfer drives the process. Understanding the distinction between galvanic and electrolytic cells is crucial for your exams.

Galvanic (Voltaic) Cells: Generating Electricity

  • Convert chemical energy of spontaneous redox reactions into electrical energy.
  • Electrons flow from anode (negative terminal) to cathode (positive terminal) externally.
  • Example: Daniell Cell (Zinc-Copper cell).
  • Used in batteries and fuel cells.

🎙️ Galvanic cells are essentially mini power plants. They harness the energy released from a naturally occurring redox reaction to produce an electric current. Think of a common battery – that's a galvanic cell in action.

Components of a Galvanic Cell

  • Anode: Electrode where oxidation occurs (negative terminal).
  • Cathode: Electrode where reduction occurs (positive terminal).
  • Electrolytes: Solutions containing ions for charge transfer.
  • Salt Bridge: Connects the two half-cells, maintaining electrical neutrality.

🎙️ Each component plays a critical role. Oxidation at the anode releases electrons, which travel through the external circuit to the cathode where reduction takes place. The salt bridge is often overlooked but is vital for continuous operation.

The Role of the Salt Bridge

  • Connects the two half-cells, allowing ion migration without mixing solutions.
  • Maintains electrical neutrality in both half-cells.
  • Prevents charge build-up, ensuring continuous electron flow.
  • Typically contains an inert electrolyte like KCl or KNO₃ in agar gel.

🎙️ Without a salt bridge, the build-up of charges in the half-cells would quickly stop the reaction. The negative ions from the salt bridge migrate to the anode compartment, and positive ions to the cathode compartment, neutralizing the excess charges.

Representation of a Galvanic Cell

  • IUPAC convention for concise description of an electrochemical cell.
  • Anode (oxidation half-cell) on the left, Cathode (reduction half-cell) on the right.
  • Single vertical line (|) denotes phase boundary (e.g., metal/solution).
  • Double vertical line (||) represents the salt bridge.

🎙️ This standardized notation is crucial for communicating cell information efficiently. For example, for a Daniell cell, it would be Zn(s) | Zn²⁺(aq) || Cu²⁺(aq) | Cu(s). Remember to always put the anode on the left.

Example: The Daniell Cell

  • Consists of a Zinc electrode in ZnSO₄ solution and a Copper electrode in CuSO₄ solution.
  • Anode: Zn(s) → Zn²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ (Oxidation)
  • Cathode: Cu²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ → Cu(s) (Reduction)
  • Overall reaction: Zn(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + Cu(s).

🎙️ The Daniell cell is the most common example of a galvanic cell. Understanding its working principle, including the electron flow and ion movement, is fundamental. Zinc is more reactive than copper, so it gets oxidized.

Electrode Potential & Standard Electrode Potential

  • Potential difference developed between the electrode and its electrolyte solution.
  • Arises due to charge separation at the interface.
  • Cannot be measured directly, only potential difference between two electrodes.
  • Standard Electrode Potential (E°): Measured at 298 K, 1 atm, 1 M concentration.

🎙️ Every half-cell has an electrode potential, which reflects its tendency to gain or lose electrons. Since absolute potential cannot be measured, we use a reference electrode to determine relative potentials, which is what Standard Electrode Potential signifies.

Standard Hydrogen Electrode (SHE)

  • Reference electrode with a defined potential of exactly 0.00 V at standard conditions.
  • Consists of a platinum electrode in 1 M H⁺ solution, with H₂ gas at 1 atm pressure.
  • Used to measure standard electrode potentials of other electrodes.
  • Can act as both anode or cathode depending on the connected half-cell.

🎙️ The SHE is our universal benchmark. By connecting any unknown half-cell to the SHE, we can measure its standard electrode potential. Remember its components and the conditions under which its potential is defined as zero.

Measuring Cell Potential (EMF)

  • Electromotive Force (EMF) or Cell Potential (E_cell) is the potential difference between two electrodes.
  • E_cell = E_cathode - E_anode (both as standard reduction potentials).
  • A positive E_cell indicates a spontaneous reaction (Galvanic cell).
  • Always use standard reduction potentials from the electrochemical series.

🎙️ This formula is critical for calculating the voltage produced by a galvanic cell. Always ensure you are using standard reduction potentials for both electrodes. The more positive the E_cell, the greater the driving force of the reaction.

Worked Example 1: Calculating Cell EMF

  • Calculate E°_cell for the reaction: Mg(s) + 2Ag⁺(aq) → Mg²⁺(aq) + 2Ag(s)
  • Given: E°(Mg²⁺/Mg) = -2.37 V, E°(Ag⁺/Ag) = +0.80 V
  • Identify anode (oxidation) and cathode (reduction).
  • Anode: Mg → Mg²⁺ (oxidation), Cathode: Ag⁺ → Ag (reduction).

🎙️ Here, Magnesium is oxidized, so it's the anode. Silver ions are reduced, making silver the cathode. Applying the formula E°_cell = E°_cathode - E°_anode, we get 0.80 V - (-2.37 V) = 3.17 V. A positive EMF confirms spontaneity.

Nernst Equation: Non-Standard Conditions

  • Relates electrode potential or cell potential to concentrations of species.
  • E_cell = E°_cell - (RT/nF) ln Q, or E_cell = E°_cell - (0.0592/n) log Q at 298K.
  • Q is the reaction quotient, n is the number of electrons transferred.
  • R = gas constant, T = temperature in Kelvin, F = Faraday's constant.

🎙️ The Nernst equation is vital because most real-world electrochemical processes don't occur under standard conditions. It allows us to calculate cell potentials when concentrations or partial pressures deviate from 1 M or 1 atm. Pay attention to the 'n' value and 'Q'.

Applying the Nernst Equation

  • For a half-cell: E = E° - (0.0592/n) log ([products]/[reactants]).
  • For a full cell: E_cell = E°_cell - (0.0592/n) log ([products]/[reactants])_overall.
  • Pure solids and liquids are not included in the expression for Q.
  • Crucial for understanding concentration cells and pH effects on potentials.

🎙️ Remember that 'n' represents the total number of electrons exchanged in the balanced overall reaction. The reaction quotient 'Q' is calculated just like for chemical equilibrium, considering only aqueous species and gases. This equation is frequently tested.

Worked Example 2: Nernst Equation

  • Calculate the electrode potential of a copper electrode in 0.1 M CuSO₄ solution at 298 K.
  • Given: E°(Cu²⁺/Cu) = +0.34 V.
  • Half-reaction: Cu²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ → Cu(s). Here n = 2.
  • E = E° - (0.0592/n) log (1/[Cu²⁺]).

🎙️ Using the Nernst equation: E = 0.34 - (0.0592/2) log (1/0.1). This simplifies to E = 0.34 - 0.0296 log(10) = 0.34 - 0.0296 = 0.3104 V. Notice how the potential changes with concentration.

Relation between ΔG, E_cell, and K

  • Gibbs Free Energy (ΔG) and cell potential (E_cell) are related: ΔG = -nFE_cell.
  • For standard conditions: ΔG° = -nFE°_cell.
  • At equilibrium (E_cell = 0): ΔG° = -RT ln K, where K is the equilibrium constant.
  • Therefore, E°_cell = (RT/nF) ln K, or E°_cell = (0.0592/n) log K at 298K.

🎙️ These equations beautifully link electrochemistry with thermodynamics. A negative ΔG corresponds to a positive E_cell, indicating spontaneity. A larger equilibrium constant K implies a greater driving force for the reaction, reflected in a higher E°_cell.

Electrolytic Cells: Driving Non-Spontaneous Reactions

  • Convert electrical energy into chemical energy to drive non-spontaneous reactions.
  • Requires an external power source (battery).
  • Anode is positive, cathode is negative (opposite to galvanic cells).
  • Examples: Electrolysis of water, molten NaCl, electroplating.

🎙️ Unlike galvanic cells, electrolytic cells need a push to make reactions happen. We supply electrical energy to force a non-spontaneous redox reaction. This is crucial for industrial processes like the production of metals and chemicals.

Galvanic vs. Electrolytic Cells: A Comparison

  • Energy Conversion: Chemical to Electrical (Galvanic), Electrical to Chemical (Electrolytic).
  • Spontaneity: Spontaneous (Galvanic), Non-spontaneous (Electrolytic).
  • Anode Polarity: Negative (Galvanic), Positive (Electrolytic).
  • Cathode Polarity: Positive (Galvanic), Negative (Electrolytic).

Faraday's Laws of Electrolysis

  • Quantitative aspects of electrolysis, relating current to amount of substance.
  • Faraday's First Law: Mass deposited is proportional to charge passed.
  • Faraday's Second Law: Masses of substances deposited by same charge are proportional to their equivalent weights.
  • Faraday constant (F): Charge of one mole of electrons (96485 C/mol).

🎙️ These laws, proposed by Michael Faraday, are fundamental to understanding how much product we can obtain from an electrolytic process. They are frequently tested with numerical problems, so practice is essential.

Faraday's First Law of Electrolysis

  • The mass (m) of a substance deposited or liberated at any electrode is directly proportional to the quantity of electricity (Q) passed through the electrolyte.
  • m ∝ Q, where Q = I × t (Current × Time).
  • m = ZIt, where Z is the electrochemical equivalent.
  • Z = Equivalent Weight / F = Molar Mass / (n × F).

🎙️ This law provides a direct way to calculate the amount of substance produced. Remember that 'n' here refers to the number of electrons involved in the half-reaction for that specific substance. Make sure units are consistent (Amperes, seconds, Coulombs).

Worked Example 3: Faraday's Law

  • A solution of AgNO₃ is electrolyzed for 10 minutes with a current of 1.5 Amperes.
  • Calculate the mass of silver deposited at the cathode.
  • Given: Molar mass of Ag = 108 g/mol, 1 F = 96485 C/mol.
  • Ag⁺ + e⁻ → Ag(s). Here n = 1.

🎙️ First, calculate Q = I x t = 1.5 A x (10 x 60 s) = 900 C. Then, moles of electrons = Q/F = 900/96485. Since 1 mole of Ag requires 1 mole of electrons, mass of Ag = (900/96485) x 108 g = 1.008 g. This type of problem is very common.

Corrosion: An Electrochemical Phenomenon

  • Deterioration of a metal due to its reaction with its environment.
  • Primarily an electrochemical process (e.g., rusting of iron).
  • Anode: Metal oxidation (e.g., Fe → Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻).
  • Cathode: Reduction of O₂ in presence of H⁺ (e.g., O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ → 2H₂O).

🎙️ Corrosion is a significant problem, causing enormous economic losses. Rusting of iron is a classic example, requiring both oxygen and moisture. Understanding its electrochemical nature helps in developing effective prevention strategies.

Prevention of Corrosion

  • Barrier Protection: Coating metal surface with paint, grease, or other metals (galvanizing).
  • Sacrificial Protection: Connecting a more active metal (e.g., Mg, Zn) to the iron.
  • Electrical Protection: Applying an external current to make the metal cathodic.
  • Using anti-rust solutions (inhibitors).

🎙️ Preventing corrosion is crucial for extending the life of metallic structures. Galvanizing, where iron is coated with zinc, is a common example of sacrificial protection. Zinc is more easily oxidized than iron, protecting the iron.

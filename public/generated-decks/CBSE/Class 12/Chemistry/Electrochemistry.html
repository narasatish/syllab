Electrochemical Cells: The Core Idea

  • Contain two electrodes (conductors) immersed in an electrolyte (ion conductor).
  • At the electrodes, oxidation and reduction reactions occur.

🎙️ Electrochemical cells are the fundamental units where these energy conversions take place. They are essentially setups where chemical reactions involving electron transfer can be harnessed or driven. Understanding their structure is key to understanding electrochemistry.

Two Main Types: Galvanic vs. Electrolytic Cells

  • Galvanic (Voltaic) Cell: Chemical energy → Electrical energy (Spontaneous).
  • Electrolytic Cell: Electrical energy → Chemical energy (Non-spontaneous).
  • Both involve redox reactions but differ in direction and energy flow.

🎙️ These are the two pillars of electrochemistry. Galvanic cells are what we commonly know as batteries, producing electricity from spontaneous reactions. Electrolytic cells, on the other hand, use electricity to force non-spontaneous reactions to occur, like in electroplating.

Galvanic (Voltaic) Cells: Generating Electricity

  • Converts chemical energy from a spontaneous redox reaction into electrical energy.
  • Example: Daniell cell (Zn-Cu cell) is a classic example.
  • Anode (oxidation) is negative, Cathode (reduction) is positive.

🎙️ Galvanic cells are the powerhouses that generate electricity. They work on the principle of spontaneous redox reactions, where electrons flow from the species being oxidized to the species being reduced, creating an electric current. Remember, in galvanic cells, the anode is negative and the cathode is positive.

Components of a Galvanic Cell

  • Two Half-Cells: Each containing an electrode and an electrolyte.
  • Electrodes: Conductors where redox reactions occur (Anode & Cathode).
  • Electrolyte: Ionic solution allowing ion movement.

🎙️ A typical galvanic cell consists of two separate compartments, called half-cells, each with an electrode immersed in an electrolyte. These components work together to facilitate the electron transfer and maintain electrical neutrality, which is essential for continuous current flow.

The Salt Bridge: Its Crucial Role

  • U-shaped tube containing an inert electrolyte (e.g., KCl, KNO₃ in agar-agar gel).
  • Connects the two half-cells, completing the internal circuit.
  • Maintains electrical neutrality by allowing ion migration, preventing charge build-up.

🎙️ The salt bridge is often overlooked but is absolutely vital. Without it, charge would build up in the half-cells, quickly stopping the electron flow. It allows ions to move between the half-cells, balancing the charges and ensuring the cell can operate continuously.

Working of a Daniell Cell (Zn-Cu): Anode & Oxidation

  • At the Zinc electrode (Anode): Zn(s) → Zn²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻.
  • Zinc metal gets oxidized, losing electrons and dissolving into the solution.
  • Electrons flow from the anode through the external circuit.

🎙️ In a Daniell cell, the zinc electrode acts as the anode, where oxidation occurs. Zinc atoms lose two electrons each, becoming zinc ions that dissolve into the solution. These released electrons then travel through the external wire towards the copper electrode.

Working of a Daniell Cell (Zn-Cu): Cathode & Reduction

  • At the Copper electrode (Cathode): Cu²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ → Cu(s).
  • Copper ions from the solution gain electrons and deposit as copper metal.
  • This is where reduction takes place.

🎙️ Simultaneously, at the copper electrode, which is the cathode, copper ions from the solution accept the electrons arriving from the external circuit. They get reduced and deposit as solid copper metal onto the electrode. This continuous flow of electrons constitutes the electric current.

Cell Notation/Representation

  • A shorthand method to describe an electrochemical cell.
  • Anode | Anode Electrolyte || Cathode Electrolyte | Cathode.
  • Single vertical line (|) denotes a phase boundary (electrode-electrolyte).
  • Double vertical line (||) denotes the salt bridge.

🎙️ Cell notation is a standardized way to represent an electrochemical cell, making it easy to understand its components and reactions. Remember the order: Anode on the left, Cathode on the right, with the salt bridge in the middle. Pay attention to the phase boundaries.

Electrode Potential: The Driving Force

  • Potential difference developed between the electrode and its electrolyte.
  • Arises due to charge separation at the interface.
  • A measure of the tendency of an electrode to lose or gain electrons.

🎙️ Every electrode immersed in its solution develops a certain potential difference. This 'electrode potential' quantifies how strongly an electrode wants to either give up electrons (oxidation potential) or accept electrons (reduction potential). It's the inherent driving force for electron transfer.

Standard Electrode Potential (E°): Reference Point

  • Electrode potential measured under standard conditions:
  • 1 M concentration for all ions, 1 atm pressure for all gases, 298 K (25°C).
  • It's impossible to measure absolute electrode potential; only relative values are determined.

🎙️ To compare the potentials of different electrodes, we need a universal reference point. This is where standard electrode potential comes in. By defining a set of standard conditions, we can measure and compare the relative tendencies of various electrodes to undergo reduction or oxidation.

Standard Hydrogen Electrode (SHE): The Universal Reference

  • A reference electrode with a defined standard electrode potential of 0.00 V.
  • Consists of a platinum electrode in 1 M H⁺ solution, with H₂ gas at 1 atm bubbling through.
  • Can act as both anode or cathode depending on the other half-cell.

🎙️ The SHE is the cornerstone for measuring all other standard electrode potentials. By arbitrarily assigning its potential as zero, we can then construct a cell with SHE and any other half-cell, and the measured cell potential will directly give us the standard electrode potential of that other half-cell.

Measuring Cell Potential (E°cell): EMF

  • The potential difference between the two half-cells of a galvanic cell.
  • Also known as Electromotive Force (EMF) or Cell Voltage.
  • E°cell = E°cathode - E°anode (always reduction potentials).
  • A positive E°cell indicates a spontaneous reaction.

🎙️ The cell potential, or EMF, is the maximum potential difference a cell can generate. It's calculated by subtracting the standard reduction potential of the anode from that of the cathode. A positive cell potential is essential for a galvanic cell to function spontaneously.

Electrochemical Series: Predicting Reactivity

  • List of standard electrode potentials arranged in increasing order of reduction potential.
  • Higher E° value means greater tendency for reduction (stronger oxidizing agent).
  • Lower E° value means greater tendency for oxidation (stronger reducing agent).

🎙️ The electrochemical series is a powerful tool. It allows us to predict the spontaneity of redox reactions and the relative strengths of oxidizing and reducing agents. Remember, species higher in the series will oxidize species lower in the series.

Nernst Equation: Non-Standard Conditions

  • Relates electrode potential to concentration of ions (and partial pressure of gases).
  • Used to calculate cell potential under non-standard conditions.
  • Crucial for understanding real-world electrochemical systems where conditions vary.

🎙️ While standard electrode potentials are useful, most reactions in the real world don't occur under standard conditions. The Nernst equation allows us to adjust the potential for varying concentrations and pressures, making our calculations much more applicable to practical scenarios.

Derivation & Formula of Nernst Equation

  • For a general redox reaction: aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD
  • Ecell = E°cell - (RT/nF) ln Q
  • Where Q is the reaction quotient, n is moles of electrons, R is gas constant, F is Faraday constant, T is temperature.

🎙️ This is the core formula for the Nernst equation. Remember the terms: Ecell is the non-standard potential, E°cell is the standard potential, n is the number of electrons transferred, and Q is the reaction quotient, similar to Keq but for non-equilibrium conditions.

Electrolytic Cells: Driving Non-Spontaneous Reactions

  • Converts electrical energy into chemical energy to drive non-spontaneous redox reactions.
  • Requires an external power source (battery).
  • Anode (oxidation) is positive, Cathode (reduction) is negative.

🎙️ Unlike galvanic cells, electrolytic cells need an external push. They use electricity to force reactions that would not occur naturally. Notice the change in polarity: for electrolytic cells, the anode is positive and the cathode is negative, which is opposite to galvanic cells.

Electrolysis: Process & Examples (Molten NaCl)

  • The process of chemical decomposition produced by passing an electric current through a liquid or solution.
  • Example: Electrolysis of molten NaCl.
  • At Cathode (-): Na⁺(l) + e⁻ → Na(l) (Reduction)
  • At Anode (+): 2Cl⁻(l) → Cl₂(g) + 2e⁻ (Oxidation)

🎙️ Electrolysis is a key industrial process. In molten NaCl, sodium ions are reduced to sodium metal at the cathode, and chloride ions are oxidized to chlorine gas at the anode. This is how reactive metals like sodium are extracted.

Faraday's First Law of Electrolysis: Quantity of Product

  • The mass of any substance deposited or liberated at an electrode is directly proportional to the quantity of electricity passed.
  • W ∝ Q (where Q = I × t)
  • W = Z × I × t (Z is electrochemical equivalent).

🎙️ Faraday's laws quantify the relationship between electricity and the amount of substance produced during electrolysis. The first law simply states that if you pass more charge, you'll get more product. Remember the formula W = ZIt, where Z is a constant specific to each substance.

Faraday's Second Law of Electrolysis: Ratio of Products

  • When the same quantity of electricity is passed through different electrolytes, the masses of substances deposited are proportional to their equivalent masses.
  • W₁/W₂ = E₁/E₂ (where E is equivalent mass).
  • Equivalent mass = Molar mass / Valency factor.

🎙️ The second law comes into play when comparing the products from different electrolytes with the same amount of charge. It tells us that the relative amounts of substances produced are directly related to their equivalent masses, which accounts for the valency changes in the reactions.

What are Solutions?

  • A homogeneous mixture of two or more components.
  • Uniform composition and properties throughout.
  • Components are indistinguishable at the molecular level.

🎙️ A solution is not just any mixture; it's a special type where everything is perfectly blended. Think of sugar dissolving in water – you can't see the individual sugar particles anymore, right? That's homogeneity.

Components of a Solution: Solute & Solvent

  • **Solvent:** The component present in the largest quantity, determines physical state.
  • **Solute:** The component(s) present in smaller quantities, dissolved in the solvent.
  • Example: In salt water, water is the solvent and salt is the solute.

🎙️ Identifying the solute and solvent is key. The solvent is usually the one doing the dissolving and sets the overall physical state of the solution. The solute is what gets dissolved.

Examples of Different Solution Types

  • **Gas in Gas:** Air (Oxygen and other gases in Nitrogen).
  • **Liquid in Liquid:** Alcohol in water (Ethanol in water).

Expressing Concentration of Solutions

  • Concentration quantifies the amount of solute present in a given amount of solvent or solution.
  • It can be expressed qualitatively (dilute, concentrated) or quantitatively.
  • Quantitative methods are crucial for precise chemical work.

🎙️ Knowing the concentration is vital for experiments, manufacturing, and even everyday tasks like cooking. We need precise ways to measure how much solute is actually present in our solution.

Mass Percentage (w/w)

  • Defined as: (Mass of solute / Mass of solution) × 100.
  • Units: Expressed as a percentage (%).
  • Useful when both solute and solvent are solids or their masses are easily measured.

🎙️ Mass percentage is a straightforward way to express concentration. If you have a 10% (w/w) sugar solution, it means 10 grams of sugar are present in 100 grams of the total solution. This is a common method in industrial applications.

Volume Percentage (v/v)

  • Defined as: (Volume of solute / Volume of solution) × 100.
  • Units: Expressed as a percentage (%).
  • Primarily used for solutions of liquids in liquids.

🎙️ Volume percentage is common for liquid-liquid mixtures, such as alcohol solutions. For example, a '40% alcohol by volume' beverage means 40 mL of alcohol per 100 mL of the total beverage. Remember that volumes are not always additive, so total volume is measured.

Mass by Volume Percentage (w/v)

  • Defined as: (Mass of solute / Volume of solution) × 100.
  • Units: Often expressed as g/100 mL or simply %.
  • Widely used in medicine and pharmacy due to ease of measurement.

🎙️ This concentration term is particularly useful in clinical and pharmaceutical settings. It allows for quick preparation of solutions where a specific mass of solute is needed in a certain volume of solution, like preparing intravenous fluids.

Parts Per Million (ppm)

  • Defined as: (Mass/Volume of solute / Mass/Volume of solution) × 10^6.
  • Used for extremely dilute solutions, where solute is present in trace amounts.
  • Common in environmental analysis (e.g., pollution levels) and water quality reports.

🎙️ When you hear about 'parts per million' of a contaminant in water, it signifies a very small quantity. This unit helps us express these minuscule concentrations meaningfully, often avoiding very small decimal numbers.

Mole Fraction (χ)

  • Defined as: Moles of one component / Total moles of all components in the solution.
  • It is a unitless quantity, as it's a ratio of moles.
  • The sum of mole fractions of all components in a solution is always equal to one.

🎙️ Mole fraction is a very fundamental concentration term, especially useful in understanding the behavior of gases and colligative properties. It directly relates to the number of particles, not their mass or volume. Remember, it's a ratio, so no units!

Molarity (M)

  • Defined as: Moles of solute / Volume of solution (in Litres).
  • Units: mol/L or M (Molar).
  • It is temperature dependent because volume changes with temperature.

🎙️ Molarity is one of the most commonly used concentration terms in the lab. However, its temperature dependence means that a solution's molarity can slightly change if its temperature varies significantly. Always specify the temperature if precision is critical.

🌡️
Depends on temperature

Molality (m)

  • Defined as: Moles of solute / Mass of solvent (in kg).
  • Units: mol/kg or m (Molal).
  • It is temperature independent because mass does not change with temperature.

🎙️ Molality is preferred over molarity for experiments where temperature fluctuations are expected, especially when studying colligative properties. Since it relies on mass of solvent, which is constant, molality remains unchanged with temperature.

Common Mistakes: Molarity vs. Molality

  • **Molarity:** Uses volume of *solution* in Litres. Temperature dependent.
  • **Molality:** Uses mass of *solvent* in kg. Temperature independent.
  • Confusing these two is a frequent source of error in calculations.

🎙️ It's crucial to remember the distinction between molarity and molality. One depends on the volume of the entire solution, and the other on the mass of just the solvent. This difference impacts their temperature dependence and when they are best used.

Solubility: How Much Can Dissolve?

  • The maximum amount of solute that can dissolve in a given amount of solvent.
  • Forms a saturated solution at specific temperature and pressure.
  • Factors like nature of solute/solvent, temperature, and pressure influence solubility.

🎙️ Solubility is a key property of solutions. Every substance has a limit to how much it can dissolve in another. Once that limit is reached, we have a saturated solution, and any additional solute will just settle at the bottom.

Dissolving limit

Factors Affecting Solubility: Solids in Liquids

  • **Nature of Solute and Solvent:** 'Like dissolves like' principle (polar in polar, non-polar in non-polar).
  • **Temperature:** For endothermic dissolution, solubility increases with temperature; for exothermic, it decreases.
  • **Pressure:** Has negligible effect on the solubility of solids in liquids.

🎙️ The 'like dissolves like' rule is fundamental here. Think about oil and water – they don't mix because one is non-polar and the other is polar. Temperature's effect depends on whether the dissolving process absorbs or releases heat.

Factors Affecting Solubility: Gases in Liquids (Henry's Law)

  • **Henry's Law:** The partial pressure of the gas above the solution is proportional to its mole fraction in the solution.
  • Formula: p = K_H * χ (where p is partial pressure, K_H is Henry's constant, χ is mole fraction).
  • **Temperature:** Solubility of gases in liquids generally decreases with increasing temperature.

🎙️ Henry's Law explains why carbonated drinks fizz more when you open the bottle – the pressure of CO2 above the liquid decreases, reducing its solubility. It also explains why aquatic life struggles in warmer waters, as oxygen solubility decreases.

Vapour Pressure of Liquid Solutions

  • The pressure exerted by the vapour in equilibrium with the liquid at a given temperature.
  • It depends on the nature of the liquid (intermolecular forces) and temperature.
  • Adding a solute affects the vapour pressure of the solvent.

🎙️ Imagine a closed container with some liquid. Some liquid molecules escape into the gas phase, creating pressure. This is vapour pressure. When we add a solute, especially a non-volatile one, it changes the solvent's ability to vaporize.

Raoult's Law (for Volatile Solutes)

  • States that for a solution of volatile liquids, the partial vapour pressure of each component is proportional to its mole fraction.
  • P_A = χ_A * P_A° and P_B = χ_B * P_B° (where P° is vapour pressure of pure component).
  • Total vapour pressure (P_total) = P_A + P_B.

🎙️ Raoult's Law is a fundamental concept for ideal solutions. It tells us how the presence of another volatile component affects the vapour pressure of each component in the mixture. The more of a component, the higher its partial vapour pressure.

📈
Partial pressure relation

Raoult's Law (for Non-Volatile Solutes)

  • When a non-volatile solute is added, the vapour pressure of the solution is lower than that of the pure solvent.
  • P_solution = χ_solvent * P_solvent°.
  • This lowering of vapour pressure is a colligative property.

🎙️ This is a very important application of Raoult's Law. The non-volatile solute particles occupy some surface area, reducing the number of solvent molecules that can escape into the vapour phase. This leads to a measurable decrease in vapour pressure.

Ideal and Non-Ideal Solutions

  • **Ideal Solutions:** Obey Raoult's Law over the entire range of concentrations.
  • **Ideal Solutions:** ΔH_mix = 0, ΔV_mix = 0 (no heat change, no volume change on mixing).
  • **Non-Ideal Solutions:** Deviate from Raoult's Law (positive or negative deviations).

🎙️ Ideal solutions are theoretical constructs, but some solutions behave nearly ideally. Non-ideal solutions show deviations due to differences in intermolecular forces between solute-solvent, solute-solute, and solvent-solvent interactions. These deviations are crucial for understanding real-world mixtures.

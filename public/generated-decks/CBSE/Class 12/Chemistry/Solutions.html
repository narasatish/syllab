Understanding Solutions: Basics

  • Homogeneous mixture of two or more components
  • Components: Solute (lesser amount) & Solvent (greater amount)
  • Examples: Saltwater, air, alloys
  • Can be solid, liquid, or gaseous

🎙️ A solution is not just any mixture; it's a homogeneous mixture, meaning its composition is uniform throughout. Think of saltwater – you can't distinguish salt from water once dissolved. The component in a smaller amount is the solute, and the one in a larger amount is the solvent.

Types of Solutions by Physical State

  • Depends on the physical state of solute and solvent
  • Gas in Gas (e.g., air)
  • Gas in Liquid (e.g., soda water)
  • Liquid in Liquid (e.g., alcohol in water)
  • Solid in Liquid (e.g., sugar in water)
  • Solid in Solid (e.g., alloys like brass)

🎙️ Solutions aren't just liquids! We classify them based on the physical state of the solvent. For example, air is a gaseous solution, while brass is a solid solution. The solvent's state usually determines the solution's state.

Expressing Solution Concentration

  • Quantifies the amount of solute in a given amount of solvent/solution
  • Crucial for stoichiometry, reaction rates, and properties
  • Different terms for different applications
  • Need to know formulas and units

🎙️ Why do we need so many ways to express concentration? Because different applications demand different units. For instance, in environmental science, very small concentrations are important, whereas in lab preparations, molarity is often preferred for reactions.

Mass Percentage & Volume Percentage

  • Mass % (w/w): (Mass of solute / Mass of solution) × 100
  • Volume % (v/v): (Volume of solute / Volume of solution) × 100
  • Used for expressing concentration of solids in liquids (mass %) or liquids in liquids (volume %)
  • Units: dimensionless, often just '%'

🎙️ These are straightforward ways to express concentration. Mass percentage is useful when both solute and solvent are weighed, while volume percentage is common for liquid-liquid solutions, like alcohol content in beverages.

Parts per Million (ppm) & Mass by Volume Percentage

  • ppm: (Mass of solute / Mass of solution) × 10^6
  • Used for very dilute solutions (e.g., pollutants in water/air)
  • Mass by Volume % (w/v): (Mass of solute / Volume of solution) × 100
  • Common in pharmacy and medicine

🎙️ Parts per million is essential when dealing with trace amounts, like measuring contaminants. Mass by volume percentage bridges the gap, relating mass of solute to the volume of the final solution, often seen in medical prescriptions.

Molarity (M): Moles per Litre

  • Moles of solute per litre of solution
  • Formula: M = (Moles of solute / Volume of solution in L)
  • Units: mol/L or M
  • Temperature dependent (as volume changes with temperature)

🎙️ Molarity is one of the most widely used concentration terms in chemistry, especially for reactions, as it directly relates to the number of moles. Remember, because volume is temperature-dependent, molarity also changes with temperature.

Molality (m): Moles per Kilogram

  • Moles of solute per kilogram of solvent
  • Formula: m = (Moles of solute / Mass of solvent in kg)
  • Units: mol/kg or m
  • Temperature independent (mass does not change with temperature)

🎙️ Unlike molarity, molality is independent of temperature because it's based on mass, not volume. This makes it particularly useful for studies involving colligative properties, where temperature variations need to be accounted for.

Mole Fraction (χ): Relative Moles

  • Ratio of moles of one component to total moles of all components
  • Formula for component A: χA = (nA / (nA + nB + ...))
  • Sum of mole fractions of all components in a solution is always 1
  • Units: dimensionless

🎙️ Mole fraction provides a direct measure of the relative number of molecules of each component. It's particularly useful in gas mixtures and when discussing colligative properties and Raoult's Law, as it relates directly to the number of particles.

Solution to Worked Example 1

  • Mass of solute (NaOH) = 4 g
  • Mass of solvent (water) = 18 g
  • Mass of solution = Mass of solute + Mass of solvent = 4 g + 18 g = 22 g
  • Mass % of NaOH = (Mass of NaOH / Mass of solution) × 100 = (4 g / 22 g) × 100 = 18.18%

🎙️ The key here is to correctly identify the mass of the solute and the total mass of the solution, which is the sum of solute and solvent. Always double-check your units and calculations for accuracy.

Solubility: Dissolving Capacity

  • Maximum amount of solute that can dissolve in a specific amount of solvent
  • At a given temperature and pressure
  • Forms a saturated solution
  • Factors affecting solubility: Nature of solute/solvent, Temperature, Pressure

🎙️ Solubility isn't just about 'if' something dissolves, but 'how much' can dissolve under specific conditions. Once a solution is saturated, no more solute will dissolve, and any added solute will simply settle at the bottom.

Solubility of Solids in Liquids: Factors

  • Like dissolves like: Polar solutes dissolve in polar solvents, non-polar in non-polar
  • Temperature effect: Mostly increases with temperature (endothermic dissolution), but can decrease (exothermic dissolution)
  • Pressure effect: Negligible for solids

🎙️ The 'like dissolves like' rule is fundamental. For most solids, increasing temperature increases solubility as the dissolution process is endothermic. However, there are exceptions where solubility decreases with temperature.

Solubility of Gases in Liquids: Henry's Law

  • States that the partial pressure of a gas (P) is proportional to its mole fraction (χ) in the solution
  • P = KH * χ (where KH is Henry's Law constant)
  • Higher pressure increases solubility of gas
  • Higher temperature decreases solubility of gas

🎙️ Henry's Law is crucial for understanding how gases dissolve in liquids, like oxygen in water. Increasing the pressure of a gas above a liquid forces more gas into the solution, while increasing temperature generally reduces gas solubility.

Henry's Law: Real-Life Applications

  • Soft drinks: CO2 dissolved under high pressure
  • Deep-sea diving: N2 solubility in blood increases under high pressure
  • High altitude sickness (Anoxia): Low atmospheric pressure reduces O2 solubility in blood

🎙️ Henry's Law has significant practical implications. The fizz in your soda is due to high-pressure CO2. Divers need to ascend slowly to avoid 'the bends' caused by nitrogen gas coming out of solution in their blood due to decreasing pressure.

Vapour Pressure & Raoult's Law

  • Vapour pressure: Pressure exerted by vapour in equilibrium with its liquid
  • Raoult's Law for volatile components: PA = χA * P°A
  • Partial vapour pressure of each component is proportional to its mole fraction
  • Applicable to ideal solutions

🎙️ Vapour pressure is a key property of liquids. Raoult's Law extends this to solutions, stating that each volatile component contributes to the total vapour pressure based on its mole fraction and its pure vapour pressure.

Raoult's Law with Non-Volatile Solute

  • When a non-volatile solute is added, solvent's vapour pressure decreases
  • P_solution = χ_solvent * P°_solvent
  • This lowering of vapour pressure is a colligative property
  • The solute particles hinder solvent molecules from escaping

🎙️ This is a crucial extension of Raoult's Law. Adding a non-volatile solute reduces the surface area available for solvent molecules to escape into the vapour phase, thus lowering the overall vapour pressure of the solution.

Ideal & Non-Ideal Solutions

  • Ideal Solutions: Obey Raoult's Law over entire concentration range
  • No enthalpy or volume change on mixing (ΔHmix = 0, ΔVmix = 0)
  • Non-Ideal Solutions: Deviate from Raoult's Law
  • Positive deviation (P_obs > P_Raoult): Weaker A-B interactions
  • Negative deviation (P_obs < P_Raoult): Stronger A-B interactions

🎙️ Ideal solutions are theoretical benchmarks. Real solutions often show deviations. Positive deviation means components like each other less than themselves, leading to higher vapour pressure. Negative deviation implies stronger attraction, leading to lower vapour pressure.

Azeotropes: Constant Boiling Mixtures

  • Binary mixtures that boil at a constant temperature like a pure liquid
  • Distill without change in composition
  • Formed by non-ideal solutions
  • Minimum boiling azeotropes (positive deviation) and Maximum boiling azeotropes (negative deviation)

🎙️ Azeotropes are fascinating! They cannot be separated by fractional distillation because their liquid and vapour compositions are the same at their boiling point. This has significant implications in industrial separations.

Colligative Properties: Number Matters

  • Properties that depend only on the number of solute particles
  • Independent of the nature of the solute
  • Only for dilute solutions and non-volatile solutes
  • Four main types: RLVP, EBP, DFP, Osmotic Pressure

🎙️ Colligative properties are powerful because they allow us to determine molar masses of unknown solutes. The key is that it's the quantity of solute particles, not their identity, that dictates these properties.

Relative Lowering of Vapour Pressure (RLVP) & Elevation in Boiling Point (EBP)

  • RLVP: (P° - P_solution) / P° = χ_solute (from Raoult's Law)
  • EBP: ΔTb = Kb * m (Kb = molal elevation constant)
  • Boiling point increases with addition of non-volatile solute
  • Due to lowered vapour pressure, requiring higher temperature to boil

🎙️ The lowering of vapour pressure directly leads to an elevation in the boiling point. The solution needs to be heated to a higher temperature for its vapour pressure to equal the atmospheric pressure and start boiling.

Depression in Freezing Point (DFP) & Osmotic Pressure (Π)

  • DFP: ΔTf = Kf * m (Kf = molal depression constant)
  • Freezing point decreases with addition of non-volatile solute
  • Osmotic Pressure (Π): Pressure required to stop osmosis
  • Π = iCRT (where i is Van't Hoff factor, R = gas constant, T = temp in K)

🎙️ Adding a solute interferes with the solvent's ability to freeze, thus lowering the freezing point. Osmotic pressure is a significant colligative property, especially in biological systems, governing the movement of solvent across semipermeable membranes.

Van't Hoff Factor (i) & Worked Example 2

  • Accounts for dissociation/association of solute particles in solution
  • i = (Normal molar mass / Observed molar mass) OR (Observed colligative property / Normal colligative property)
  • For non-electrolytes, i = 1; for electrolytes, i > 1 (dissociation) or i < 1 (association)
  • Example: Calculate osmotic pressure of 0.01 M NaCl solution at 300 K (i=2 for NaCl)

🎙️ The Van't Hoff factor is crucial for electrolytes like NaCl, which dissociate into ions, increasing the effective number of particles. For NaCl, 'i' is approximately 2, meaning it produces twice as many particles as a non-electrolyte.

What are Amines? The Basics

  • Organic derivatives of ammonia (NH₃).
  • Formed by replacing one or more hydrogen atoms of ammonia by alkyl or aryl groups.
  • General formula: R-NH₂, R₂NH, R₃N.
  • Contain a nitrogen atom with a lone pair of electrons.

🎙️ Think of amines as ammonia molecules where one or more hydrogens have been swapped out for carbon-containing groups. This simple substitution leads to a diverse family of compounds with unique properties. The lone pair on nitrogen is key to many of their reactions.

Classification of Amines: Primary, Secondary, Tertiary

  • Based on the number of alkyl/aryl groups attached to the nitrogen atom.
  • Primary (1°): One R group (R-NH₂).
  • Secondary (2°): Two R groups (R₂NH).
  • Tertiary (3°): Three R groups (R₃N).

🎙️ This classification is crucial, as 1°, 2°, and 3° amines have distinct chemical properties and reactivities. It's determined by what's directly bonded to the nitrogen, not the carbon it's attached to. Remember this distinction carefully.

Nomenclature of Amines: Naming Conventions

  • Common System: Alkylamines (e.g., Methylamine).
  • IUPAC System: Alkanamines (e.g., Methanamine).
  • For complex amines, N-substituted derivatives are used.
  • Example: (CH₃)₂NH is N-Methylmethanamine.

🎙️ Nomenclature can be tricky, so practice is key. While common names are often used, the IUPAC system provides a systematic way to name even complex amines. Pay close attention to how N-substituents are indicated for secondary and tertiary amines.

Isomerism in Amines: Structural Variations

  • Chain Isomerism: Different carbon chain arrangements.
  • Position Isomerism: Different position of the amino group.
  • Functional Isomerism: Amines can be functional isomers with amides or nitriles (less common).
  • Metamerism: Different alkyl groups attached to the nitrogen atom in 2° and 3° amines.

🎙️ Amines exhibit various types of isomerism, which is important for understanding their diversity. Metamerism is particularly relevant for secondary and tertiary amines, where the distribution of alkyl groups around the nitrogen can change. Ensure you can identify these types.

Structure of Amines: Pyramidal Geometry

  • Nitrogen atom is sp³ hybridized.
  • Three bond pairs and one lone pair of electrons.
  • Geometry is pyramidal, similar to ammonia.
  • Bond angle is approximately 109.5° (e.g., 108° in trimethylamine).

🎙️ The sp³ hybridization and the presence of a lone pair give amines their characteristic pyramidal shape. This lone pair is crucial for their basicity and nucleophilicity, making them reactive species in many organic reactions. Remember this structural detail.

Preparation of Amines - I: Reduction of Nitro Compounds

  • Nitro compounds (R-NO₂) can be reduced to primary amines (R-NH₂).
  • Reagents: H₂/Pd, Pt, or Ni; or Sn/HCl, Fe/HCl.
  • A common method for preparing aromatic primary amines.
  • Example: Nitrobenzene → Aniline.

🎙️ This is a very important method, especially for preparing aromatic amines like aniline. The choice of reducing agent depends on the specific compound and desired conditions, but Sn/HCl or catalytic hydrogenation are frequently used. Make sure you know these reagents.

1Nitro Compound (R-NO₂)
2Reducing Agent (Sn/HCl)
3Primary Amine (R-NH₂)

Preparation of Amines - II: Ammonolysis of Alkyl Halides

  • Alkyl halides (R-X) react with ammonia (NH₃).
  • Nucleophilic substitution reaction.
  • Forms a mixture of 1°, 2°, 3° amines and quaternary ammonium salts.
  • Common mistake: Difficult to obtain a pure primary amine due to polyalkylation.

🎙️ While simple, ammonolysis is limited by its tendency to yield a mixture of products. The primary amine formed can further react with the alkyl halide, leading to secondary, tertiary amines, and eventually quaternary salts. To favor primary amines, use excess ammonia.

1Alkyl Halide + NH₃
21° Amine
32° Amine
43° Amine + Quaternary Salt

Preparation of Amines - III: Gabriel Phthalimide Synthesis

  • Exclusive method for preparing pure primary aliphatic amines.
  • Prevents polyalkylation.
  • Phthalimide is treated with alcoholic KOH, then alkyl halide, then hydrolysis.
  • Cannot be used for aromatic primary amines (aryl halides are unreactive).

🎙️ This is a very important named reaction for preparing pure primary amines. It cleverly avoids the issue of polyalkylation seen in ammonolysis. Remember, it's not suitable for preparing aromatic primary amines because aryl halides do not undergo nucleophilic substitution easily.

1Phthalimide + KOH
2Potassium Phthalimide
3+ R-X
4N-Alkylphthalimide
5Hydrolysis (NaOH/H₂O)
6Pure R-NH₂

Preparation of Amines - IV: Hoffmann Bromamide Degradation

  • Converts an amide into a primary amine with one carbon atom less.
  • Reagents: Br₂ in aqueous or ethanolic NaOH.
  • Useful for 'stepping down' a carbon chain.
  • Example: Ethanamide → Methanamine.

🎙️ The Hoffmann bromamide degradation is another crucial named reaction. Its key feature is the loss of a carbon atom from the carbonyl group, resulting in an amine with one carbon less than the starting amide. This is a very specific and useful transformation.

1Amide (R-CO-NH₂)
2+ Br₂ + NaOH
3Isocyanate intermediate
4Rearrangement & Hydrolysis
5Primary Amine (R-NH₂) + Na₂CO₃

Worked Example: Synthesizing Ethylamine

  • From Bromoethane: Ammonolysis (excess NH₃).
  • From Nitroethane: Reduction with H₂/Pd or Sn/HCl.
  • From Propanamide: Hoffmann bromamide degradation.
  • From Phthalimide: Gabriel phthalimide synthesis (with bromoethane).

Physical Properties - I: Boiling Points

  • Primary and secondary amines can form intermolecular H-bonds.
  • Tertiary amines cannot form H-bonds due to absence of H on N.
  • Order of boiling points: 1° > 2° > 3° (for comparable molar masses).
  • Amines have lower boiling points than alcohols of similar molar mass (weaker H-bonds).

🎙️ Hydrogen bonding significantly influences boiling points. Primary amines have two N-H bonds, secondary have one, and tertiary have none, explaining the trend. Alcohols have stronger H-bonds due to oxygen's higher electronegativity, thus higher boiling points than amines.

Physical Properties - II: Solubility in Water

  • Lower aliphatic amines are soluble in water.
  • Due to their ability to form hydrogen bonds with water molecules.
  • Solubility decreases with increase in molar mass (larger hydrophobic alkyl part).
  • Aromatic amines are generally insoluble in water.

🎙️ Just like boiling points, hydrogen bonding also dictates solubility. Smaller amines can readily form H-bonds with water. However, as the non-polar alkyl chain grows, it dominates, reducing water solubility. Aromatic amines like aniline are largely insoluble.

Chemical Properties - I: Basicity of Amines

  • Amines are basic due to the lone pair of electrons on the nitrogen atom.
  • They can accept a proton (Brønsted bases) or donate the lone pair (Lewis bases).
  • Alkylamines are stronger bases than ammonia due to +I effect of alkyl groups.
  • Aromatic amines are weaker bases than ammonia due to resonance (lone pair delocalized).

🎙️ Basicity is a critical property of amines. The availability of the lone pair on nitrogen determines its strength as a base. Electron-donating groups increase basicity, while electron-withdrawing groups or resonance delocalization decrease it. This trend is crucial for exams.

Chemical Properties - II: Alkylation of Amines

  • Amines react with alkyl halides to form higher amines.
  • This is a nucleophilic substitution reaction.
  • Primary amines form secondary, secondary form tertiary, and tertiary form quaternary salts.
  • Same reaction as ammonolysis, but starting with an amine.

🎙️ Alkylation demonstrates the nucleophilic nature of amines. Just as ammonia can be alkylated, amines themselves can further react with alkyl halides. This is why obtaining a pure primary amine from ammonolysis is challenging, as the product itself is reactive.

Chemical Properties - III: Acylation of Amines

  • Amines react with acid chlorides, anhydrides, or esters.
  • Forms amides (N-substituted amides).
  • Hydrogen atom of the amino group is replaced by an acyl group.
  • Used to protect amino groups or prepare amides.

🎙️ Acylation is a useful reaction for forming amides and can be used to distinguish primary and secondary amines from tertiary amines, as tertiary amines lack the necessary hydrogen on nitrogen. It's also a way to protect the amino group during other reactions.

1Amine (R-NH₂)
2+ Acyl Chloride (R'-CO-Cl)
3Loss of HCl
4Amide (R'-CO-NH-R)

Chemical Properties - IV: Carbylamine Reaction (Isocyanide Test)

  • Specific test for primary amines (aliphatic and aromatic).
  • Reacts with chloroform (CHCl₃) and alcoholic KOH.
  • Forms foul-smelling isocyanides (carbylamines).
  • Secondary and tertiary amines do not give this test.

🎙️ The carbylamine reaction is a very important distinguishing test in organic chemistry due to the unmistakable, offensive odor of the isocyanide product. Remember, only primary amines give this test, making it highly specific for their identification.

Chemical Properties - V: Reaction with Nitrous Acid

  • Used to distinguish between 1°, 2°, and 3° amines.
  • Prepared in situ from NaNO₂ and HCl at 0-5°C.
  • 1° aliphatic amines: Forms diazonium salts (unstable) → N₂ gas evolution.
  • 1° aromatic amines: Forms stable diazonium salts (for further reactions).
  • 2° amines: Forms N-nitrosoamines (yellow oily compounds).
  • 3° aliphatic amines: Forms N-nitrosoammonium salts (soluble in water).
  • 3° aromatic amines: Forms p-nitrosoaniline (green precipitate).

🎙️ The reaction with nitrous acid is a complex but powerful tool for differentiating amines. Pay close attention to the distinct products and observations for each type of amine. The stability of aromatic diazonium salts is particularly important for synthesis.

Chemical Properties - VI: Hinsberg's Test

  • Uses benzenesulphonyl chloride (Hinsberg's reagent).
  • Distinguishes 1°, 2°, and 3° amines.
  • 1° amine: Forms N-alkylbenzenesulphonamide, soluble in NaOH (acidic H).
  • 2° amine: Forms N,N-dialkylbenzenesulphonamide, insoluble in NaOH (no acidic H).
  • 3° amine: Does not react with Hinsberg's reagent (no H on N), insoluble in NaOH.

🎙️ Hinsberg's test is a classic laboratory method for distinguishing between the three classes of amines. The key lies in the presence or absence of an acidic hydrogen on the nitrogen of the sulphonamide product, which determines its solubility in alkali. This is a frequently asked concept.

1Amine + PhSO₂Cl
2Product + NaOH
31°: Soluble
42°: Insoluble
53°: No reaction

Electrophilic Substitution in Aromatic Amines

  • Amino group (-NH₂) is a powerful activating and ortho-para directing group.
  • Aniline undergoes facile electrophilic substitution.
  • Bromination: Forms 2,4,6-tribromoaniline (due to high activation).
  • Nitration: Direct nitration gives tarry products and meta-derivative (due to oxidation and formation of anilinium ion).
  • Sulphonation: Forms sulphanilic acid (zwitterionic form).

🎙️ Aromatic amines are highly reactive towards electrophilic substitution. The strong activating effect of the amino group means reactions like bromination can occur at multiple positions. For nitration, protection of the amino group is often required to control the reaction.

Worked Example: Distinguishing Ethylamine, Diethylamine, Triethylamine

  • Ethylamine (1°), Diethylamine (2°), Triethylamine (3°).
  • Test 1: Carbylamine reaction - Ethylamine gives foul smell.
  • Test 2: Hinsberg's test - Ethylamine forms soluble product, Diethylamine forms insoluble product, Triethylamine does not react.
  • Test 3: Nitrous acid test - Each gives distinct observations (N₂ gas, yellow oil, no reaction/salt).

🎙️ This is a typical exam question. You must know the specific tests and their observations to differentiate between different classes of amines. Practice these distinguishing reactions thoroughly, as they are high-scoring points in board exams.

Real-life Applications of Amines

  • Pharmaceuticals: Many drugs (e.g., adrenaline, novocaine) are amines.
  • Dyes: Aromatic amines are key intermediates in dye synthesis.
  • Polymers: Used in the production of polyurethanes, nylons.
  • Agrochemicals: Herbicides, insecticides contain amine functionalities.

🎙️ Amines are not just theoretical constructs; they are vital in many aspects of our lives. From life-saving medicines to the colors in our clothes and the plastics we use, amines play a crucial role. This highlights their industrial and biological significance.

Recap: Key Concepts of Amines

  • Classification: 1°, 2°, 3° based on substitution at N.
  • Preparation: Reduction of nitro, ammonolysis, Gabriel, Hoffmann.
  • Properties: Basicity (effect of R/Ar groups), H-bonding, solubility.
  • Reactions: Alkylation, acylation, carbylamine, nitrous acid, Hinsberg's.

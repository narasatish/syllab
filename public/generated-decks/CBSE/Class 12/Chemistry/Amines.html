What are Amines?

  • Organic derivatives of ammonia (NH₃).
  • One or more hydrogen atoms replaced by alkyl/aryl groups.
  • General formula: R-NH₂, R₂NH, R₃N.

🎙️ At their core, amines are simply ammonia molecules where one, two, or all three hydrogen atoms are swapped out for organic groups. This simple substitution gives rise to a diverse class of compounds with unique properties.

Classifying Amines: Primary, Secondary, Tertiary

  • Primary (1°): One alkyl/aryl group attached to N.
  • Secondary (2°): Two alkyl/aryl groups attached to N.
  • Tertiary (3°): Three alkyl/aryl groups attached to N.

🎙️ The classification is crucial and depends on the number of alkyl or aryl groups directly bonded to the nitrogen atom, not the nature of the carbon atom. This distinction profoundly impacts their reactivity.

Nomenclature of Amines: Common & IUPAC Systems

  • Common: Alkyl/Aryl amine (e.g., Methylamine, Aniline).
  • IUPAC: Alkanamine (e.g., Methanamine, Ethanamine).
  • For 2°/3°: N-substituted derivatives (e.g., N-Methylmethanamine).

🎙️ Both common and IUPAC names are frequently used. For complex amines, especially secondary and tertiary ones, the IUPAC system provides a systematic way to name them by treating the alkyl groups as N-substituents.

Isomerism in Amines

  • Chain Isomerism: Different carbon chain arrangements.
  • Position Isomerism: Different position of -NH₂ group.
  • Metamerism: Different alkyl groups around the functional group (2°/3° only).

🎙️ Amines exhibit various types of structural isomerism. Chain and position isomerism are straightforward, but remember metamerism is specific to secondary and tertiary amines where the alkyl groups attached to nitrogen can vary.

Structure of Amines: Pyramidal Geometry

  • Nitrogen atom is sp³ hybridized.
  • Three bond pairs and one lone pair of electrons.
  • Geometry is pyramidal, bond angle ~109.5° (slightly less due to lone pair).

🎙️ Similar to ammonia, the nitrogen in amines is sp³ hybridized, leading to a pyramidal geometry. The lone pair on nitrogen is crucial for its basicity and nucleophilic character, making it a key feature of amine reactivity.

Preparation: Reduction of Nitro Compounds

  • Nitro compounds (R-NO₂) reduced to amines (R-NH₂).
  • Reagents: H₂/Pd, Pt, Ni or Sn/HCl, Fe/HCl.
  • Common for preparing aromatic amines like Aniline.

🎙️ This is a very common and efficient method, especially for synthesizing aromatic amines. The choice of reducing agent depends on the functional groups present in the molecule to avoid unwanted side reactions.

Preparation: Ammonolysis of Alkyl Halides

  • Alkyl halide reacts with ammonia (nucleophilic substitution).
  • Forms primary, secondary, tertiary amines, and quaternary ammonium salts.
  • Can be controlled to favor primary amine by excess ammonia.

🎙️ Ammonolysis is a versatile but often problematic method due to overalkylation. The primary amine formed is still nucleophilic and can react further with the alkyl halide, leading to a mixture of products. Using excess ammonia helps to minimize this.

Preparation: Gabriel Phthalimide Synthesis

  • Used for preparing pure primary aliphatic amines.
  • Phthalimide treated with alcoholic KOH, then alkyl halide, then hydrolysis.
  • Aromatic primary amines cannot be prepared by this method.

🎙️ Gabriel synthesis is excellent for pure primary aliphatic amines because it avoids the polyalkylation issues of ammonolysis. Remember, it fails for aromatic amines because aryl halides do not undergo nucleophilic substitution under these conditions.

1Phthalimide + KOH
2Alkyl Halide (R-X)
3Hydrolysis (H₂O/H⁺ or OH⁻)
4Primary Amine (R-NH₂)

Preparation: Hoffmann Bromamide Degradation

  • Amide reacts with Br₂ in aqueous or alcoholic NaOH.
  • Forms a primary amine with one carbon atom less than the parent amide.
  • Useful for descending a homologous series.

🎙️ This reaction is a degradation because it results in the loss of a carbon atom, specifically the carbonyl carbon. It's a very important reaction for synthesizing primary amines and is often tested in exams for its unique carbon-reducing property.

Physical Properties of Amines

  • Lower aliphatic amines are gases, higher ones are liquids/solids.
  • Boiling Point: 1° > 2° > 3° (due to H-bonding).
  • Solubility: Lower amines soluble in water (H-bonding with water).

🎙️ Hydrogen bonding plays a significant role in determining the physical properties of amines. Primary amines can form more extensive hydrogen bonds than secondary amines, while tertiary amines cannot form intermolecular H-bonds among themselves, hence the boiling point trend.

Basicity: Amines vs. Ammonia (Gas Phase)

  • Amines are Lewis bases (lone pair on N).
  • Alkyl groups are electron-donating (+I effect).
  • Alkyl amines are stronger bases than ammonia in the gas phase.

🎙️ The basicity of amines arises from the availability of the lone pair on the nitrogen atom. In the gas phase, the electron-donating inductive effect of alkyl groups increases electron density on nitrogen, making the lone pair more available for protonation.

Electron Donation

Basicity: Aromatic vs. Aliphatic Amines

  • Aliphatic amines are generally stronger bases than aromatic amines.
  • In aromatic amines (e.g., Aniline), the lone pair is delocalized by resonance.
  • Delocalization makes the lone pair less available for protonation.

🎙️ This is a critical comparison. The resonance stabilization of aniline makes its lone pair less available compared to aliphatic amines, where the lone pair is localized on the nitrogen. This significantly reduces the basicity of aromatic amines.

↔️
Resonance Effect

Basicity: Substituent Effects on Aniline

  • Electron-donating groups (EDGs) increase basicity (e.g., -CH₃, -OCH₃).
  • Electron-withdrawing groups (EWGs) decrease basicity (e.g., -NO₂, -Cl).
  • Ortho effect can complicate predictions for ortho-substituted anilines.

🎙️ Understanding how substituents affect basicity is crucial for predicting reactivity. Electron-donating groups stabilize the conjugate acid, making the amine more basic, while electron-withdrawing groups destabilize it, reducing basicity.

Chemical Reactions: Alkylation & Acylation

  • Alkylation: Reaction with alkyl halides, leads to higher amines & quaternary salts.
  • Acylation: Reaction with acid chlorides/anhydrides, forms amides.
  • Hinsberg's reagent (Benzenesulphonyl chloride) distinguishes 1°, 2°, 3° amines.

🎙️ Alkylation is similar to ammonolysis, leading to a mixture. Acylation, however, is a useful reaction to protect the amine group or to synthesize amides. Hinsberg's test is an important practical application to differentiate amines.

Chemical Reactions: Carbylamine Reaction (Isocyanide Test)

  • Characteristic reaction for primary aliphatic and aromatic amines.
  • Reacts with chloroform (CHCl₃) and alcoholic KOH.
  • Forms foul-smelling isocyanides (R-NC).

🎙️ The carbylamine reaction is a highly specific test for primary amines. The extremely unpleasant odor of the isocyanide product makes it a very distinctive and memorable reaction, useful for identification in the lab.

Chemical Reactions: Reaction with Nitrous Acid (HNO₂)

  • 1° Aliphatic Amines: Form unstable diazonium salts, evolve N₂ gas.
  • 1° Aromatic Amines: Form stable diazonium salts (at 0-5°C).
  • 2° Amines: Form N-nitrosoamines (yellow oily compounds).
  • 3° Amines: Form trialkylammonium nitrite salts (aliphatic), or p-nitrosoaniline derivatives (aromatic).

🎙️ This reaction is paramount for distinguishing between different classes of amines. The stability of the diazonium salt is key, being stable only for aromatic primary amines at low temperatures, leading to various observable outcomes.

Chemical Reactions: Electrophilic Substitution of Aniline

  • -NH₂ group is a strong activating and ortho-para directing group.
  • Direct bromination yields 2,4,6-tribromoaniline (due to high activation).
  • Nitration requires protection of -NH₂ group (acetylation) to avoid oxidation & meta product.
  • Sulphonation forms sulphanilic acid (zwitterionic form).

🎙️ The amino group is a powerful activator, leading to multiple substitutions if not controlled. Protection of the amino group is often necessary to achieve monosubstitution or to prevent oxidation during nitration. Sulphonation is unique due to the zwitterionic product.

Worked Example: Basicity Order

  • Question: Arrange the following in increasing order of basicity: Aniline, Ethylamine, Ammonia.
  • Solution: Aniline < Ammonia < Ethylamine.
  • Explanation: Ethylamine (alkyl group +I effect) > Ammonia. Aniline (resonance delocalization) < Ammonia.

🎙️ This example directly applies our understanding of inductive and resonance effects on basicity. Remember, in aqueous phase, solvation effects can slightly alter the order for aliphatic amines, but the general trend with ammonia and aniline remains consistent.

Worked Example: Identifying Amines

  • Question: An organic compound 'A' (C₃H₉N) on reaction with CHCl₃ and alc. KOH gives a foul-smelling compound 'B'. 'A' reacts with NaNO₂/HCl at 0-5°C to give N₂ gas. Identify A and B.
  • Solution: A is Propan-1-amine (a primary amine). B is Propyl isocyanide.
  • Explanation: Carbylamine test for 1° amine, and N₂ evolution with HNO₂ confirms 1° aliphatic amine.

🎙️ This problem integrates knowledge of characteristic reactions. The foul-smelling product points to the carbylamine reaction, and the evolution of nitrogen gas indicates an unstable diazonium salt, both characteristic of primary aliphatic amines.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Confusing 1°, 2°, 3° amine classification with alcohols.
  • Incorrect basicity order, especially for aqueous phase.
  • Forgetting Gabriel synthesis limitation (no aromatic amines).
  • Not protecting -NH₂ group during electrophilic substitution of Aniline.

🎙️ Be careful with classification; it's about the number of alkyl groups on nitrogen, not the carbon it's attached to. Basicity can be tricky, especially comparing gas phase vs. aqueous phase. Always consider the limitations and necessary precautions for reactions.

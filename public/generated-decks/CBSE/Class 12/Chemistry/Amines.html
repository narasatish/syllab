Meet the Amine Family

  • Amines are derivatives of Ammonia (NH₃)
  • One or more hydrogen atoms are replaced by alkyl or aryl groups
  • They are organic compounds containing nitrogen with a lone pair of electrons.

🎙️ Think of ammonia as the parent molecule. When we replace one, two, or all three of its hydrogen atoms with carbon-containing groups, we get amines. This replacement forms the basis of their classification.

Ammonia (NH₃)
Primary Amine (RNH₂)
Secondary Amine (R₂NH)
Tertiary Amine (R₃N)
5 / 30

Classification: Primary, Secondary, Tertiary

  • Based on the number of alkyl/aryl groups attached to the Nitrogen atom
  • Crucially, it's NOT about the carbon atom attached to Nitrogen (unlike alcohols)
  • This distinction affects their properties and reactions.

🎙️ This is a common point of confusion. Remember, for amines, we count how many alkyl or aryl groups are directly bonded to the nitrogen, not the carbon it's attached to. A primary amine has one group, secondary has two, and tertiary has three.

1Primary (1°) R-NH₂
2Secondary (2°) R₂NH
3Tertiary (3°) R₃N

Visualizing Amine Types

  • Primary (1°): One carbon chain attached to N (e.g., CH₃-NH₂)
  • Secondary (2°): Two carbon chains attached to N (e.g., CH₃-NH-CH₃)
  • Tertiary (3°): Three carbon chains attached to N (e.g., (CH₃)₃N)

🎙️ Imagine the nitrogen atom as a central hub. In a primary amine, only one 'arm' is a carbon chain, the other two are hydrogens. For secondary, two arms are carbon chains, and for tertiary, all three arms are carbon chains. This simple visual helps solidify the concept.

Nomenclature: Common System

  • Name the alkyl group(s) attached to nitrogen, followed by 'amine'
  • If multiple alkyl groups, list alphabetically (e.g., Ethylmethylamine)
  • Use prefixes di- or tri- for identical groups (e.g., Diethylamine).

🎙️ The common system is straightforward for simpler amines. You just name the alkyl groups and add 'amine'. It's like giving a nickname based on who's in the family.

🗣️
Everyday Naming

Nomenclature: IUPAC System

  • Replace the 'e' of the parent alkane with 'amine' (e.g., Methanamine)
  • For substituted amines, use 'N-alkyl' or 'N,N-dialkyl' prefixes
  • Locate the amino group on the longest carbon chain.

🎙️ The IUPAC system provides a systematic way to name even complex amines. Remember to identify the longest carbon chain containing the amino group and use 'N-' for substituents directly on the nitrogen atom.

The Structure of Amines

  • Nitrogen atom is sp³ hybridised
  • Pyramidal shape, similar to ammonia
  • One lone pair of electrons on Nitrogen
  • C-N-H bond angles are approximately 109.5° (slightly less due to lone pair repulsion).

🎙️ The lone pair on nitrogen is crucial. It gives amines their characteristic pyramidal geometry and is responsible for their basicity and nucleophilicity. Understanding this structure helps explain many of their properties.

Preparation: Reduction of Nitro Compounds

  • Nitro compounds (R-NO₂) are reduced to primary amines (R-NH₂)
  • Common reagents: H₂/Pd, Sn/HCl, or Fe/HCl
  • This is an important industrial method for aromatic amines (e.g., nitrobenzene to aniline).

🎙️ Imagine taking a nitro group and stripping away its oxygen atoms, replacing them with hydrogens. That's essentially what reduction does, giving us a primary amine. This method is particularly useful for synthesizing aromatic amines.

1Nitro Compound (R-NO₂)
2+ Reducing Agent
3Primary Amine (R-NH₂)
13 / 30

Preparation: Ammonolysis of Alkyl Halides

  • Alkyl halide (R-X) reacts with ammonia (NH₃)
  • Undergoes nucleophilic substitution (SN₂ reaction)
  • Can lead to a mixture of 1°, 2°, 3° amines and quaternary ammonium salts due to over-alkylation.

🎙️ In this reaction, ammonia acts as a nucleophile, attacking the alkyl halide. The challenge here is that the newly formed primary amine is also nucleophilic and can react further, making it difficult to isolate a single product.

🔄
Ammonia's Attack
14 / 30

Preparation: Reduction of Nitriles

  • Nitriles (R-C≡N) are reduced to primary amines (R-CH₂NH₂)
  • Reagents: LiAlH₄/ether or catalytic hydrogenation (H₂/Ni, Pt, or Pd)
  • A useful method for 'ascending the series' – increasing the carbon chain length by one.

🎙️ Think of a nitrile as having a 'hidden' carbon. When you reduce it, that carbon becomes part of the amine, effectively extending the carbon chain by one. This is a great synthetic tool for building larger molecules.

Preparation: Reduction of Amides

  • Amides (R-CONH₂) are reduced to primary amines (R-CH₂NH₂)
  • Reagent: Lithium Aluminium Hydride (LiAlH₄) followed by hydrolysis (H₂O)
  • This method also retains the number of carbon atoms in the chain.

🎙️ Similar to nitriles, amides can also be reduced to amines using strong reducing agents like LiAlH₄. This is a good way to get a primary amine without changing the carbon skeleton.

1Amide (R-CONH₂)
2+ LiAlH₄
3Primary Amine (R-CH₂NH₂)

Preparation: Gabriel Phthalimide Synthesis

  • A highly selective method for preparing pure primary aliphatic amines
  • Phthalimide reacts with alcoholic KOH, then alkyl halide, then hydrolysis
  • Cannot be used for aromatic primary amines (aryl halides don't react).

🎙️ The Gabriel synthesis is a gem in organic chemistry! It's a clever way to 'protect' the amine group and ensure you only get a primary amine, avoiding over-alkylation. Remember, it's specific for aliphatic primary amines.

🛡️
Protected Amine Synthesis
18 / 30

Preparation: Hoffmann Bromamide Degradation

  • Amide (R-CONH₂) reacts with Bromine (Br₂) and NaOH (or KOH)
  • Results in a primary amine (R-NH₂) with one carbon atom less than the parent amide
  • Excellent method for 'descending the series' – shortening the carbon chain.

🎙️ This reaction is unique because it 'degrades' the amide, meaning it removes a carbon atom. If you need to synthesize an amine that has one less carbon than your starting material, Hoffmann degradation is your go-to reaction.

✂️
Carbon Chain Shortening

Physical Properties: Boiling Points

  • Primary (1°) and secondary (2°) amines form intermolecular hydrogen bonds
  • Tertiary (3°) amines do NOT form H-bonds (no H attached to N)
  • Order of boiling points (for comparable molar mass): 1° > 2° > 3°.

🎙️ Hydrogen bonding is the key here. The more H-bonds an amine can form, the more energy is required to break those attractions, leading to higher boiling points. Tertiary amines, lacking an H on nitrogen, cannot form these bonds.

Physical Properties: Solubility in Water

  • Lower aliphatic amines are soluble in water (form H-bonds with water)
  • Solubility decreases rapidly with increasing molar mass (hydrophobic alkyl part)
  • Higher amines and aromatic amines are largely insoluble in water.

🎙️ Just like alcohols, amines can interact with water molecules through hydrogen bonding. However, as the non-polar alkyl chain gets longer, it starts to dominate, making the molecule less soluble in polar water.

Chemical Properties: Basicity of Amines

  • Amines are basic due to the lone pair of electrons on the nitrogen atom
  • They accept a proton (H⁺) from acids, forming ammonium salts
  • Alkyl amines are generally stronger bases than ammonia.

🎙️ The lone pair on nitrogen is like a generous donor, ready to accept a proton. This makes amines excellent bases. The formation of ammonium salts is a classic acid-base reaction.

Basicity: Alkyl Amines vs. Ammonia (Gas Phase)

  • Alkyl groups are electron-donating (+I effect)
  • They increase electron density on the nitrogen, making the lone pair more available
  • Basicity order (gas phase): 3° > 2° > 1° > NH₃.

🎙️ In the gas phase, without solvent interference, the inductive effect is dominant. More alkyl groups mean more electron donation to nitrogen, making it more basic and better at holding a proton. Hence, tertiary amines are strongest.

⚡⚡⚡
Electron Donation
23 / 30

Basicity: Aqueous Phase Considerations

  • In water, solvation effects (H-bonding with water) also play a role
  • Steric hindrance by alkyl groups hinders solvation and protonation
  • Aqueous basicity order: 2° > 1° > 3° for methyl amines; 2° > 3° > 1° for ethyl amines.

🎙️ This is where it gets tricky! In water, the stability of the conjugate acid (ammonium ion) also depends on how well it's solvated by water molecules. Steric hindrance can prevent water from surrounding the ion, reducing stability. This leads to the observed aqueous basicity orders.

🌊
Solvation Effects
24 / 30

Basicity: Aromatic Amines (Aniline)

  • Aniline is a significantly weaker base than ammonia and aliphatic amines
  • The lone pair on nitrogen is delocalised into the benzene ring via resonance
  • This reduces the electron density on nitrogen, making the lone pair less available for protonation.

🎙️ Aromatic amines like aniline are much weaker bases. The resonance stabilization of the benzene ring 'pulls' the lone pair electrons away from the nitrogen, making them less available to accept a proton. This is a crucial concept.

📉

Chemical Properties: Acylation

  • Primary (1°) and secondary (2°) amines react with acid chlorides, anhydrides, or esters
  • Forms amides by replacing a hydrogen atom on nitrogen with an acyl group
  • Tertiary (3°) amines do not undergo acylation (no H on N).

🎙️ Acylation is an important reaction for 1° and 2° amines. It's essentially forming a new amide bond. Remember that tertiary amines can't participate because they lack the necessary hydrogen atom on nitrogen.

🔗
Forming Amides

Chemical Properties: Carbylamine Reaction (Isocyanide Test)

  • A characteristic test for primary amines (aliphatic and aromatic)
  • Amine reacts with chloroform (CHCl₃) and alcoholic KOH (or NaOH)
  • Forms isocyanides (carbylamines), which have an extremely foul smell.

🎙️ This is a classic distinguishing test! If you get a horrible, offensive smell, you likely have a primary amine. Secondary and tertiary amines do not give this reaction. It's a great way to identify primary amines in the lab.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Confusing 1° carbon with 1° nitrogen in amine classification
  • Incorrectly stating basicity order in aqueous vs. gas phase
  • Forgetting chain shortening/lengthening in Hoffmann/Nitrile reduction
  • Misidentifying products or reagents in named reactions.

🎙️ Many students lose marks on these common pitfalls. Always double-check your amine classification, understand the nuances of basicity, and pay close attention to the carbon count change in specific reactions.

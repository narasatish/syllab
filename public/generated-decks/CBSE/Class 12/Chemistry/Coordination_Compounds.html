What are Coordination Compounds?

  • Formed by a central metal atom/ion bonded to a fixed number of ions/molecules.
  • These ions/molecules are called ligands.
  • Ligands donate electron pairs to the central metal atom, forming coordinate bonds.
  • They retain their identity even in solution.

🎙️ Coordination compounds are essentially complex structures where a metal ion is 'coordinated' by several surrounding molecules or ions. The key here is the formation of coordinate bonds, also known as dative bonds, where the ligand acts as a Lewis base and the metal as a Lewis acid.

Double Salts vs. Coordination Compounds

  • Double salts dissociate completely into constituent ions in solution.
  • Coordination compounds retain their identity in solution.
  • This difference is key to their chemical behavior.

🎙️ A common point of confusion is differentiating between double salts and coordination compounds. Remember, double salts like Mohr's salt break down completely, showing tests for all their ions. Coordination compounds, however, maintain their complex structure, only dissociating into the complex ion and counter ions.

Werner's Theory of Coordination Compounds

  • Proposed by Alfred Werner in 1893.
  • Explains bonding in coordination compounds.
  • Introduced concepts of primary and secondary valencies.

🎙️ Werner's theory was revolutionary. It laid the foundation for understanding the structure and bonding of these complexes. He proposed that metals exhibit two types of valencies, which we will discuss in detail.

1Metal has two valencies
2Primary valency is ionizable
3Secondary valency is non-ionizable
4Secondary valency fixes geometry
5 / 25

Werner's Theory: Valencies Explained

  • Primary Valency: Corresponds to oxidation state, ionizable, satisfied by anions.
  • Secondary Valency: Corresponds to coordination number, non-ionizable, satisfied by ligands.
  • Secondary valencies determine the spatial arrangement (geometry) of the complex.

🎙️ Consider [Co(NH₃)₆]Cl₃. The primary valency of Co is +3 (satisfied by 3 Cl⁻ ions). The secondary valency is 6 (satisfied by 6 NH₃ ligands). The 3 Cl⁻ ions outside the bracket are ionizable, while the NH₃ ligands are directly bonded and non-ionizable.

Key Terms: Central Metal Atom/Ion

  • The atom/ion to which a fixed number of ligands are attached.
  • Acts as a Lewis acid (electron pair acceptor).
  • Usually d-block elements (transition metals) due to vacant d-orbitals.

🎙️ The central metal atom or ion is the heart of the coordination compound. Transition metals are ideal for this role because their partially filled d-orbitals can readily accept electron pairs from ligands, forming stable coordinate bonds.

⚛️
Electron acceptor

Key Terms: Ligands

  • Ions or molecules capable of donating electron pairs to the central metal atom/ion.
  • Act as Lewis bases (electron pair donors).
  • Can be neutral (NH₃, H₂O) or charged (Cl⁻, CN⁻).

🎙️ Ligands are the 'partners' of the central metal. They are crucial in determining the properties and stability of the complex. Their ability to donate electron pairs is what forms the coordinate bond.

🤝
Electron donors

Key Terms: Denticity of Ligands

  • The number of donor atoms in a ligand that bind to the central metal atom.
  • Monodentate: One donor atom (e.g., Cl⁻, NH₃, H₂O).
  • Bidentate: Two donor atoms (e.g., ethylenediamine (en), oxalate (ox)).
  • Polydentate: More than two donor atoms (e.g., EDTA - hexadentate).

🎙️ Denticity is a very important concept. A monodentate ligand forms one coordinate bond, a bidentate ligand forms two, and so on. Ligands with higher denticity often form more stable complexes, known as chelates.

🖐️
Number of donor sites

Key Terms: Coordination Number (CN)

  • The total number of ligand donor atoms directly attached to the central metal ion.
  • Determines the geometry of the complex.
  • Common CN values are 4 and 6.

🎙️ The coordination number is not just the number of ligands, but the number of *donor atoms* from those ligands. For example, if a bidentate ligand like 'en' is present, two 'en' ligands will give a CN of 4, not 2.

Total attached donors

Key Terms: Coordination Entity & Sphere

  • Coordination Entity: The central metal atom/ion and the ligands directly attached to it.
  • Enclosed in square brackets [ ].
  • Coordination Sphere: The central metal and ligands forming the coordination entity.

🎙️ The coordination entity is the entire complex ion, whether it's cationic, anionic, or neutral. The coordination sphere is essentially the same concept, emphasizing the immediate environment around the central metal. Anything outside the square brackets is a counter ion.

🌐
The complex core

Key Terms: Counter Ions & Charge

  • Ions outside the coordination sphere that balance the charge of the complex.
  • They are ionizable and not directly bonded to the central metal.
  • Charge on Complex: Sum of charges of the central metal ion and all ligands.

🎙️ Counter ions are crucial for maintaining electrical neutrality. For example, in K₄[Fe(CN)₆], K⁺ ions are counter ions. To find the charge on the complex, sum the oxidation state of the metal and the charges of all ligands. This charge is then balanced by the counter ions.

Nomenclature: IUPAC Rules (Part 1)

  • Naming follows specific IUPAC guidelines.
  • Cation is named first, followed by anion (if present).
  • Ligands are named before the central metal ion.

🎙️ IUPAC nomenclature for coordination compounds is systematic. Always start with the cation, then ligands in alphabetical order, then the metal, and finally the anion. This sequence is fundamental.

1Name Cation (if any)
2Name Ligands (alphabetical)
3Name Central Metal
4Name Anion (if any)
13 / 25

Nomenclature: IUPAC Rules (Part 2)

  • Ligand Names: Anionic ligands end in '-o' (chloro, cyano). Neutral ligands retain their names (ammonia -> ammine, water -> aqua).
  • Prefixes: di-, tri-, tetra- for simple ligands. bis-, tris-, tetrakis- for complex ligands.
  • Central Metal: If complex is anionic, metal name ends in '-ate' (ferrate, cuprate). If cationic/neutral, use common name (cobalt, iron).

🎙️ Pay close attention to ligand naming conventions and the use of prefixes. The metal's name changes depending on whether the complex is cationic/neutral or anionic. Always include the oxidation state of the metal in Roman numerals in parentheses.

1Ligand suffixes
2Numerical prefixes
3Metal name/suffix
4Oxidation state (Roman numeral)

Worked Example: Naming a Cationic Complex

  • Compound: [Co(NH₃)₆]Cl₃
  • Ligands: Six ammine (NH₃) ligands.
  • Central Metal: Cobalt (Co).
  • Oxidation State: Co + 6(0) = +3 => Co is +3.
  • Counter Ion: Chloride (Cl⁻).

🎙️ Let's break this down: 'hexaammine' for six NH₃, 'cobalt' because the complex is cationic, '(III)' for its oxidation state, and 'chloride' for the anion. Remember, the number of chloride ions is not indicated in the name, as it's balanced by the complex charge.

✅
Hexaamminecobalt(III) chloride

Worked Example: Naming an Anionic Complex

  • Compound: K₄[Fe(CN)₆]
  • Ligands: Six cyano (CN⁻) ligands.
  • Central Metal: Iron (Fe).
  • Oxidation State: 4(+1) + Fe + 6(-1) = 0 => Fe is +2.
  • Counter Ion: Potassium (K⁺).

🎙️ Here, 'Potassium' is named first. Then 'hexacyano' for six CN⁻. Since the complex is anionic, 'iron' becomes 'ferrate', and '(II)' is its oxidation state. This is a classic example, so practice it well.

✅
Potassium hexacyanoferrate(II)

Isomerism in Coordination Compounds

  • Compounds with the same chemical formula but different arrangements of atoms.
  • Two main types: Structural Isomerism and Stereoisomerism.
  • Crucial for understanding diversity and properties.

🎙️ Just like organic compounds, coordination compounds also exhibit isomerism. Understanding the different types is vital for predicting their chemical and physical properties. We'll explore structural isomers first, then stereoisomers.

Structural Isomerism: Linkage Isomerism

  • Arises when an ambidentate ligand can bind through different donor atoms.
  • Example: NO₂⁻ (binds via N or O), SCN⁻ (binds via S or N).
  • Results in different bonding modes to the central metal.

🎙️ Ambidentate ligands are the key to linkage isomerism. For instance, in [Co(NH₃)₅NO₂]Cl₂, the NO₂ ligand can attach via the nitrogen atom (nitro) or the oxygen atom (nitrito), leading to distinct compounds.

Structural Isomerism: Hydrate Isomerism

  • Involves the exchange of water molecules between the coordination sphere and as lattice water.
  • Example: [Cr(H₂O)₆]Cl₃ (violet) vs. [Cr(H₂O)₅Cl]Cl₂·H₂O (green).
  • Different number of H₂O molecules inside/outside the coordination sphere.

🎙️ Hydrate isomerism specifically involves water. The total number of water molecules in the compound remains the same, but their location – either as a ligand or as water of crystallization – differs, leading to different properties like color.

Structural Isomerism: Ionisation Isomerism

  • Occurs when the counter ion in the complex can act as a ligand, and vice versa.
  • Example: [Co(NH₃)₅Br]SO₄ and [Co(NH₃)₅SO₄]Br.
  • These isomers give different ions in solution.

🎙️ Ionisation isomers produce different ions when dissolved in water. For example, [Co(NH₃)₅Br]SO₄ will give a test for sulfate ions, while [Co(NH₃)₅SO₄]Br will give a test for bromide ions. This is a common exam question.

Stereoisomerism: Geometrical Isomerism

  • Different spatial arrangements of ligands around the central metal atom.
  • Cis-trans isomerism: Ligands on adjacent (cis) or opposite (trans) positions.
  • Common in square planar (MA₂B₂) and octahedral (MA₄B₂) complexes.

🎙️ Geometrical isomers are not superimposable. For a square planar complex like [Pt(NH₃)₂Cl₂], the two NH₃ ligands can be adjacent (cis) or opposite (trans). Similarly, octahedral complexes like [Co(NH₃)₄Cl₂]⁺ also show cis and trans forms.

Stereoisomerism: Optical Isomerism

  • Exhibited by complexes that are non-superimposable mirror images of each other.
  • These are called enantiomers and rotate plane-polarized light in opposite directions.
  • Common in octahedral complexes with bidentate ligands (e.g., [Co(en)₃]³⁺).

🎙️ Optical isomerism, also known as chirality, is when a molecule and its mirror image cannot be superimposed. Think of your left and right hands. Many coordination compounds, especially those with chelating ligands, exhibit this property.

Bonding in Coordination Compounds: VBT

  • Valence Bond Theory (VBT) explains bonding, geometry, and magnetic properties.
  • Ligands donate electron pairs to vacant metal orbitals.
  • Hybridization of metal orbitals (sp³, dsp², d²sp³, sp³d²) determines geometry.

🎙️ VBT is a fundamental theory for understanding the electronic structure of coordination compounds. It helps us predict the shape of the complex and whether it will be diamagnetic or paramagnetic based on the pairing of electrons in the d-orbitals.

Applications of Coordination Compounds

  • Biological Systems: Chlorophyll (Mg), Haemoglobin (Fe), Vitamin B₁₂ (Co).
  • Industrial: Catalysts (Wilkinson's catalyst), electroplating, analytical reagents.
  • Medicinal: Cisplatin (anti-cancer drug), chelation therapy for heavy metal poisoning.

🎙️ Coordination compounds are not just theoretical constructs; they are vital in many aspects of life and industry. From the green of plants to the red of blood, and in crucial medical treatments, their role is indispensable.

The Heart of the Matter: The Carbonyl Group

  • A carbon atom double-bonded to an oxygen atom (C=O).
  • Present in aldehydes, ketones, carboxylic acids, esters, amides, etc.
  • Highly polar due to electronegativity difference between C and O.

🎙️ Imagine two friends, Carbon and Oxygen, holding hands very tightly with a double bond. This 'handshake' is the carbonyl group, the central feature of all these compounds. This bond is highly polar, making the carbon slightly positive and oxygen slightly negative, which dictates much of their reactivity.

Aldehydes vs. Ketones: Where's the Carbonyl?

  • Aldehydes: Carbonyl carbon bonded to at least one hydrogen atom (R-CHO).
  • Ketones: Carbonyl carbon bonded to two alkyl/aryl groups (R-CO-R').
  • A simple analogy: Aldehydes are at the 'end' of a chain, Ketones are in the 'middle'.

🎙️ Think of a train: if the engine (carbonyl) is at the very front or back, it's an aldehyde. If the engine is somewhere in the middle of the carriages, it's a ketone. This positional difference leads to distinct chemical properties and reactivity.

Nomenclature of Aldehydes: The '-al' Ending

  • IUPAC Name: Alkane name - 'e' + '-al'.
  • Carbonyl carbon is always C1.
  • Examples: Methanal (Formaldehyde), Ethanal (Acetaldehyde), Propanal.

🎙️ Naming aldehydes is straightforward. First, identify the longest carbon chain containing the aldehyde group. The carbonyl carbon is always numbered as 1. Then, simply replace the 'e' of the corresponding alkane name with 'al'. For instance, methane becomes methanal.

2Number from -CHO
3Replace 'e' with 'al'

Nomenclature of Ketones: The '-one' Ending

  • IUPAC Name: Alkane name - 'e' + '-one'.
  • Number chain to give carbonyl carbon the lowest possible number.
  • Examples: Propanone (Acetone), Butan-2-one, Pentan-3-one.

🎙️ For ketones, the process is similar. Find the longest chain containing the carbonyl group. Number the chain such that the carbonyl carbon gets the lowest possible number. Then, replace the 'e' of the alkane name with 'one', indicating the position of the ketone group if necessary.

2Number for C=O
3Replace 'e' with 'one'

Structure of the Carbonyl Group: Planar & Polar

  • Carbonyl carbon is sp2 hybridized.
  • Three sigma bonds in a trigonal planar arrangement (120° bond angles).
  • One pi bond between carbon and oxygen, making it highly polar.

🎙️ The carbonyl group has a flat, trigonal planar geometry around the carbon atom, with bond angles of approximately 120 degrees. This sp2 hybridization means the carbon and oxygen share a strong pi bond, in addition to the sigma bond, contributing to the group's overall stability and reactivity.

Preparation: Oxidation of Alcohols

  • Primary alcohols oxidize to aldehydes (controlled oxidation, e.g., PCC).
  • Secondary alcohols oxidize to ketones (e.g., CrO3, KMnO4).
  • Tertiary alcohols do not undergo oxidation under normal conditions.

🎙️ One of the most common ways to prepare aldehydes and ketones is by oxidizing alcohols. Remember, primary alcohols give aldehydes, but you need a mild oxidizing agent like PCC to prevent further oxidation to carboxylic acids. Secondary alcohols readily form ketones.

Preparation: Ozonolysis of Alkenes

  • Alkenes react with ozone (O3) to form ozonides.
  • Ozonides are then cleaved by Zn/H2O or (CH3)2S to form carbonyl compounds.
  • Symmetric alkenes give a single product; asymmetric give a mixture.

🎙️ Imagine cutting an alkene's double bond right in the middle! Ozonolysis does exactly that. The alkene reacts with ozone, forming an intermediate ozonide, which is then broken down to yield aldehydes or ketones, depending on the substituents around the original double bond.

Breaking Double Bonds

Preparation: Hydration of Alkynes

  • Alkynes react with water in the presence of HgSO4 and H2SO4.
  • Forms an enol intermediate, which tautomerizes to a ketone.
  • Ethyne (acetylene) gives ethanal; other alkynes give ketones.

🎙️ This method is like adding water across a triple bond. For most alkynes, the initial product is an unstable enol, which quickly rearranges through tautomerism to form a more stable ketone. Ethyne is a special case, yielding ethanal.

Preparation: Friedel-Crafts Acylation (for Ketones)

  • Aromatic ketones are prepared by reacting an acyl chloride or anhydride with an aromatic ring.
  • Requires a Lewis acid catalyst like anhydrous AlCl3.
  • Important for synthesizing substituted aromatic ketones.

🎙️ For making aromatic ketones, the Friedel-Crafts acylation is a powerful tool. It involves attaching an acyl group to an aromatic ring using an acyl chloride or anhydride, with anhydrous aluminium chloride acting as a catalyst. This reaction is a cornerstone for building complex organic molecules.

Physical Properties: Boiling Points & Solubility

  • Higher boiling points than hydrocarbons of comparable molecular mass (due to polarity).
  • Lower boiling points than alcohols of comparable molecular mass (no H-bonding).
  • Lower members (up to 4 carbons) are soluble in water (H-bonding with water).

🎙️ Aldehydes and ketones are polar molecules, so they have stronger intermolecular forces than non-polar hydrocarbons, leading to higher boiling points. However, they cannot form hydrogen bonds with each other, unlike alcohols, so their boiling points are lower than alcohols of similar size. Smaller ones are water-soluble because they can form H-bonds with water molecules.

Chemical Reactions: Nucleophilic Addition

  • The most characteristic reaction of aldehydes and ketones.
  • Nucleophiles attack the electrophilic carbonyl carbon.
  • The pi bond breaks, and a new sigma bond forms, followed by protonation.

🎙️ Due to the polarity of the carbonyl group, the carbon atom is electron-deficient and acts as an electrophile. This makes it highly susceptible to attack by nucleophiles. This two-step process, nucleophilic attack followed by protonation, is fundamental to understanding many of their reactions.

Nucleophilic Addition Example: Addition of HCN

  • Aldehydes and ketones react with hydrogen cyanide (HCN) to form cyanohydrins.
  • The reaction is reversible and base-catalyzed.
  • Cyanohydrins are important synthetic intermediates for carboxylic acids and amino acids.

🎙️ Adding HCN across the carbonyl group is a classic nucleophilic addition. The cyanide ion (CN-) is a strong nucleophile that attacks the carbonyl carbon, leading to the formation of a cyanohydrin. These compounds are highly versatile and can be further transformed into other functional groups.

Nucleophilic Addition Example: Grignard Reagents

  • Grignard reagents (RMgX) are strong nucleophiles.
  • Formaldehyde gives primary alcohols.
  • Other aldehydes give secondary alcohols; ketones give tertiary alcohols.

🎙️ Grignard reagents are organic powerhouses! They add to aldehydes and ketones to form alcohols. The type of alcohol depends on the starting carbonyl compound: formaldehyde yields primary alcohols, other aldehydes yield secondary alcohols, and ketones yield tertiary alcohols. This is a crucial reaction for carbon-carbon bond formation.

Reduction Reactions: From Carbonyl to Alcohol/Alkane

  • To Alcohols: NaBH4 (mild), LiAlH4 (strong).
  • To Alkanes: Clemmensen Reduction (Zn-Hg/conc. HCl) or Wolff-Kishner Reduction (NH2NH2/KOH/ethylene glycol).
  • These methods remove the oxygen atom from the carbonyl group.

🎙️ Reduction reactions essentially reverse oxidation. Aldehydes and ketones can be reduced to alcohols using reagents like sodium borohydride or lithium aluminium hydride. For a more drastic reduction, converting the carbonyl group directly to a methylene group, we use Clemmensen or Wolff-Kishner reductions.

Oxidation Reactions: Aldehydes vs. Ketones

  • Aldehydes are easily oxidized to carboxylic acids (even by mild agents like Tollens', Fehling's).
  • Ketones are resistant to oxidation due to the strong C-C bonds.
  • Vigorous conditions (strong oxidizers, high temp) are needed to oxidize ketones, breaking C-C bonds.

🎙️ This is a key distinguishing feature! Aldehydes are like eager students, easily giving up their hydrogen to form a carboxylic acid. Ketones, however, are much more stubborn; their C-C bonds are strong, making them resistant to oxidation under mild conditions. This difference is exploited in qualitative tests.

Reactions due to Alpha-Hydrogen: Aldol Condensation

  • Carbonyl compounds with alpha-hydrogens undergo Aldol condensation.
  • Alpha-hydrogens are acidic due to resonance stabilization of the enolate ion.
  • Forms a β-hydroxy carbonyl compound, which can dehydrate to α,β-unsaturated carbonyl.

🎙️ The hydrogen atoms on the carbon adjacent to the carbonyl group (alpha-hydrogens) are surprisingly acidic. In the presence of a base, they can be removed to form a stable enolate ion. This enolate then acts as a nucleophile, attacking another carbonyl molecule in a process called Aldol condensation, a powerful C-C bond forming reaction.

Aldol vs. Cannizzaro Reaction

  • Aldol Condensation: For aldehydes/ketones with alpha-hydrogens.
  • Cannizzaro Reaction: For aldehydes LACKING alpha-hydrogens (e.g., formaldehyde, benzaldehyde).
  • Cannizzaro involves disproportionation: one molecule is oxidized, another reduced.

🎙️ It's crucial to distinguish between these two reactions. If an aldehyde has alpha-hydrogens, it will undergo Aldol condensation. If it doesn't, under strong basic conditions, it undergoes the Cannizzaro reaction, where one molecule is reduced to an alcohol and another is oxidized to a carboxylic acid salt.

Introduction to Carboxylic Acids: The Carboxyl Group

  • Contains both a carbonyl (C=O) and a hydroxyl (-OH) group.
  • The functional group is -COOH.
  • Responsible for the sour taste of many fruits and fermented products.

🎙️ Now, let's shift our focus to carboxylic acids. These compounds combine the features of a carbonyl group and a hydroxyl group into one unique functional group: the carboxyl group. This combination gives them distinct acidic properties, as seen in the sourness of lemons (citric acid) or vinegar (acetic acid).

Preparation of Carboxylic Acids

  • Oxidation of primary alcohols and aldehydes (strong oxidizers like KMnO4).
  • From Grignard reagents: RMgX + CO2 followed by hydrolysis.
  • Hydrolysis of nitriles (R-CN) in acidic or basic conditions.

🎙️ Carboxylic acids can be prepared through several routes. We can oxidize primary alcohols and aldehydes using strong oxidizing agents. Another important method involves reacting a Grignard reagent with carbon dioxide, followed by hydrolysis. Finally, the hydrolysis of nitriles also yields carboxylic acids.

Physical Properties: Strong Hydrogen Bonding & Solubility

  • Form strong intermolecular hydrogen bonds, often existing as dimers.
  • Higher boiling points than alcohols, aldehydes, and ketones of similar molecular mass.
  • Lower members (up to 4 carbons) are highly soluble in water due to H-bonding.

🎙️ Carboxylic acids exhibit exceptionally strong hydrogen bonding, even forming stable dimers where two molecules are linked by two hydrogen bonds. This leads to significantly higher boiling points compared to other organic compounds of similar molecular weight. Their ability to form H-bonds with water also makes smaller carboxylic acids very soluble.

Acidity of Carboxylic Acids: Resonance Stabilization

  • More acidic than alcohols and phenols.
  • Carboxylate anion (RCOO-) is stabilized by resonance.
  • Electron-withdrawing groups increase acidity; electron-donating groups decrease it.

🎙️ The defining characteristic of carboxylic acids is their acidity. When they lose a proton, the resulting carboxylate ion is highly stabilized by resonance, distributing the negative charge over two oxygen atoms. This resonance stabilization makes them much stronger acids than alcohols or even phenols. Substituents on the carbon chain can further influence this acidity.

Common Mistakes to Avoid in Exams!

  • Confusing Aldol vs. Cannizzaro (alpha-H is key!).
  • Incorrectly identifying products of oxidation/reduction.
  • Errors in IUPAC nomenclature, especially numbering.

🎙️ Students often make mistakes in distinguishing between Aldol and Cannizzaro reactions; always check for alpha-hydrogens! Also, be precise with oxidation and reduction products, knowing which reagents yield alcohols, alkanes, or carboxylic acids. Finally, double-check your IUPAC numbering for correct naming.

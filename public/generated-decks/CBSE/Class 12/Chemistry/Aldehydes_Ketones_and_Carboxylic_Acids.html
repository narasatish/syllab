Introduction to Carbonyl Compounds

  • All three classes share the Carbonyl group (C=O).
  • It's a polar group due to electronegativity difference.
  • Carbonyl carbon is electrophilic, oxygen is nucleophilic.
  • This polarity dictates their reactivity.

🎙️ The carbonyl group is the heart of these compounds. Its polarity means the carbon atom is slightly positive, making it a target for electron-rich species, while the oxygen is slightly negative, attracting electron-deficient species.

Aldehydes: The Terminal Carbonyl

  • Contain a -CHO group (R-CHO).
  • Carbonyl carbon is bonded to at least one hydrogen atom.
  • Always terminal in the carbon chain.
  • Examples: Methanal (Formaldehyde), Ethanal (Acetaldehyde).

🎙️ Aldehydes are characterized by their carbonyl group being at the end of the carbon chain. This unique position gives them specific chemical properties, especially in oxidation reactions.

Ketones: The Internal Carbonyl

  • Contain a -CO- group (R-CO-R').
  • Carbonyl carbon is bonded to two alkyl or aryl groups.
  • Always internal in the carbon chain.
  • Examples: Propanone (Acetone), Butanone.

🎙️ Unlike aldehydes, ketones have their carbonyl group flanked by two other carbon atoms. This structural difference impacts their reactivity, making them generally less reactive towards oxidation.

↔️
Internal -CO- group

Carboxylic Acids: The -COOH Group

  • Contain a -COOH group (R-COOH).
  • A combination of carbonyl and hydroxyl groups.
  • Highly polar, capable of extensive hydrogen bonding.
  • Examples: Methanoic acid, Ethanoic acid.

🎙️ Carboxylic acids are unique because they combine both a carbonyl and a hydroxyl group. This combination leads to strong intermolecular forces and makes them acidic, a key property we'll explore.

Nomenclature: Aldehydes (IUPAC)

  • Longest carbon chain containing -CHO.
  • Replace '-e' of alkane with '-al'.
  • Numbering starts from carbonyl carbon (C-1).
  • Aromatic aldehydes named as benzaldehyde derivatives.

🎙️ For aldehydes, remember that the aldehyde carbon is always assigned the number 1. This simplifies numbering and ensures consistent IUPAC naming across different structures.

Nomenclature: Ketones (IUPAC)

  • Longest carbon chain containing -CO-.
  • Replace '-e' of alkane with '-one'.
  • Number to give carbonyl carbon lowest possible number.
  • Aromatic ketones named as phenone derivatives.

🎙️ With ketones, the position of the carbonyl group must be indicated by a number if there are multiple possibilities. Always choose the numbering that gives the carbonyl carbon the lowest possible number.

1Identify longest chain
2Replace '-e' with '-one'

Nomenclature: Carboxylic Acids (IUPAC)

  • Longest carbon chain containing -COOH.
  • Replace '-e' of alkane with '-oic acid'.
  • Numbering starts from carboxyl carbon (C-1).
  • Aromatic acids named as benzoic acid derivatives.

🎙️ Similar to aldehydes, the carboxyl carbon in carboxylic acids is always C-1. This is a fundamental rule for their IUPAC naming. Practice naming various branched and cyclic structures.

Worked Example: Naming Organic Compounds

  • Name: CH₃CH₂COCH₃
  • Name: CH₃CH(Cl)CHO
  • Name: C₆H₅COOH
  • Name: CH₃CH(OH)CH₂COOH

🎙️ Let's apply what we've learned. For the first compound, it's a ketone, so it's Butan-2-one. The second is an aldehyde with a chloro substituent, so 2-Chloropropanal. The third is Benzoic acid, and the last is 3-Hydroxybutanoic acid. Pay attention to numbering and functional group priority.

Preparation: Oxidation of Alcohols

  • Primary alcohols oxidize to aldehydes, then carboxylic acids.
  • Secondary alcohols oxidize to ketones.
  • Tertiary alcohols do not oxidize easily.
  • Controlled oxidation is key for aldehydes (e.g., PCC).

🎙️ This is a very common method. Remember that strong oxidizing agents will convert primary alcohols directly to carboxylic acids. For aldehydes, we need milder agents like PCC (Pyridinium Chlorochromate) to stop at the aldehyde stage.

2Aldehyde

Preparation: Ozonolysis of Alkenes

  • Alkenes react with ozone (O₃) to form ozonides.
  • Ozonides are then cleaved by Zn/H₂O or NaBH₄.
  • Forms aldehydes and/or ketones, depending on alkene structure.
  • Useful for determining alkene structure.

🎙️ Ozonolysis is a powerful reaction for synthesizing aldehydes and ketones from alkenes. The type of product formed depends on the substitution pattern around the double bond of the starting alkene.

1Alkene + O₃
2Ozonide formation
3Reductive cleavage
4Aldehydes/Ketones

Preparation: Carboxylic Acids

  • Oxidation of primary alcohols and aldehydes (strong oxidants).
  • Hydrolysis of nitriles (R-CN) in acidic or basic medium.
  • Grignard reagents with CO₂ followed by hydrolysis.
  • Hydrolysis of acyl halides and esters.

🎙️ Carboxylic acids can be prepared through various routes. Remember that the oxidation of primary alcohols and aldehydes requires strong oxidizing agents like KMnO₄ or K₂Cr₂O₇. Nitrile hydrolysis is also a common and important method.

1Alcohol/Aldehyde Oxidation
2Nitrile Hydrolysis
3Grignard + CO₂
4Ester Hydrolysis

Physical Properties: Boiling Points

  • Carboxylic acids > Alcohols > Ketones ≈ Aldehydes.
  • Carboxylic acids form strong hydrogen-bonded dimers.
  • Alcohols form intermolecular H-bonds.
  • Aldehydes/Ketones have dipole-dipole interactions, no H-bonds.

🎙️ The ability to form hydrogen bonds significantly impacts boiling points. Carboxylic acids form very stable dimers, leading to exceptionally high boiling points compared to aldehydes and ketones of similar molecular mass.

Physical Properties: Solubility in Water

  • Lower members (up to 4 carbons) are soluble in water.
  • Due to hydrogen bonding with water molecules.
  • Solubility decreases rapidly with increasing chain length.
  • Hydrophobic alkyl part dominates at longer chains.

🎙️ The smaller molecules of aldehydes, ketones, and carboxylic acids can form hydrogen bonds with water, making them soluble. However, as the non-polar hydrocarbon chain gets longer, it starts to repel water, reducing solubility.

Chemical Reactions: Nucleophilic Addition

  • Characteristic reaction of aldehydes and ketones.
  • Electrophilic carbonyl carbon attacked by nucleophiles.
  • Aldehydes are more reactive than ketones.
  • Steric and electronic factors explain reactivity difference.

🎙️ Nucleophilic addition is central to understanding aldehyde and ketone chemistry. The electron-deficient carbon is the site of attack. Aldehydes are generally more reactive due to less steric hindrance and less electron-donating effect from alkyl groups.

Worked Example: Aldol Condensation

  • Aldehydes/ketones with α-hydrogens react.
  • Forms β-hydroxy aldehydes/ketones (aldols).
  • Followed by dehydration to α,β-unsaturated carbonyls.
  • Base-catalyzed or acid-catalyzed.

🎙️ Aldol condensation is a powerful carbon-carbon bond forming reaction. It requires at least one alpha-hydrogen on the carbonyl compound. Be careful with the conditions, as heating can lead to dehydration and formation of the unsaturated product.

Chemical Reactions: Oxidation - Aldehydes vs. Ketones

  • Aldehydes are easily oxidized to carboxylic acids.
  • Ketones resist oxidation under mild conditions.
  • Aldehydes have an oxidizable H-atom on the carbonyl carbon.
  • Ketones require strong conditions, C-C bond cleavage occurs.

🎙️ This difference in oxidation reactivity is a key distinction between aldehydes and ketones. It's the basis for several important identification tests. Remember, the presence of the hydrogen atom directly attached to the carbonyl carbon in aldehydes is the reason for their easy oxidation.

Distinguishing Aldehydes: Tollens' & Fehling's Tests

  • Tollens' Test: Forms 'silver mirror' with aldehydes.
  • Fehling's Test: Produces red precipitate (Cu₂O) with aldehydes.
  • Both tests rely on aldehyde's easy oxidation.
  • Ketones do not give positive results with these reagents.

🎙️ These are classic qualitative tests in organic chemistry. A positive Tollens' test gives a shiny silver mirror, while Fehling's test results in a brick-red precipitate. These are reliable ways to differentiate aldehydes from ketones.

Acidity of Carboxylic Acids

  • More acidic than alcohols and phenols.
  • Due to resonance stabilization of carboxylate anion (RCOO⁻).
  • Electron-withdrawing groups increase acidity.
  • Electron-donating groups decrease acidity.

🎙️ The acidity of carboxylic acids is a direct consequence of the stability of their conjugate base, the carboxylate ion. The negative charge is delocalized over two electronegative oxygen atoms, making it very stable. This is a crucial concept for exams.

Reactions: Esterification

  • Carboxylic acids react with alcohols to form esters.
  • Catalyzed by mineral acids (e.g., H₂SO₄).
  • Reversible reaction, equilibrium shifted by removing water.
  • Esters are known for their pleasant fruity smells.

🎙️ Esterification is an important reaction for synthesizing esters, which are widely used in perfumes and food flavorings. Remember it's a condensation reaction where water is eliminated, and it's reversible.

  • Practice IUPAC nomenclature thoroughly.

🎙️ Students often mix up the conditions for various reactions or forget the specific requirements. Pay close attention to the presence of alpha-hydrogens, oxidizing/reducing agents, and reaction conditions. Practice naming and reaction mechanisms diligently.

Introduction: The Carbonyl Group

  • Functional group: Carbon atom double-bonded to an oxygen atom (C=O)
  • Present in Aldehydes, Ketones, Carboxylic Acids, Esters, Amides, etc.
  • Highly polar due to electronegativity difference between C and O

🎙️ The carbonyl group is the defining feature of this entire chapter. It's a carbon atom double-bonded to an oxygen atom. This group is highly polar, making these compounds very reactive and central to many organic transformations.

Nomenclature of Aldehydes

  • IUPAC: Alkane name - 'e' + 'al' (e.g., Methanal, Ethanal)
  • Carbonyl carbon is always C1; longest chain includes C=O
  • Common names: Formaldehyde, Acetaldehyde

🎙️ For aldehydes, the carbonyl carbon is always given the number 1. We replace the '-e' ending of the corresponding alkane with '-al'. Remember to practice both IUPAC and common names as both are frequently tested.

Nomenclature of Ketones

  • IUPAC: Alkane name - 'e' + 'one' (e.g., Propanone, Butanone)
  • Number chain to give C=O the lowest possible number
  • Common names: Acetone (Propanone), Methyl ethyl ketone (Butanone)

🎙️ Ketones have the carbonyl group within the carbon chain, not at the end. We number the chain to give the carbonyl carbon the lowest possible number. Propanone, commonly known as acetone, is the simplest ketone.

2Number to give C=O lowest number

Nomenclature of Carboxylic Acids

  • IUPAC: Alkane name - 'e' + 'oic acid' (e.g., Methanoic acid, Ethanoic acid)
  • Carboxyl carbon (-COOH) is always C1
  • Common names: Formic acid, Acetic acid

🎙️ Carboxylic acids are characterized by the -COOH group. Similar to aldehydes, the carboxyl carbon is always designated as C1. We replace the '-e' of the alkane with '-oic acid'. Formic acid and acetic acid are very common examples.

Structure of Carbonyl Group: Polarity & Reactivity

  • Carbonyl carbon is sp2 hybridized, trigonal planar geometry
  • C=O bond is polar: oxygen is more electronegative than carbon
  • Carbonyl carbon is electrophilic; carbonyl oxygen is nucleophilic

🎙️ The carbonyl carbon is sp2 hybridized, leading to a trigonal planar geometry around it. The significant polarity of the C=O bond is key to its reactivity. The carbon acts as an electrophile, attracting nucleophiles, while the oxygen acts as a nucleophile.

Preparation of Aldehydes & Ketones - I: Oxidation of Alcohols

  • Primary alcohols (1°) oxidize to aldehydes (using PCC)
  • Secondary alcohols (2°) oxidize to ketones (using CrO3, PCC, KMnO4)
  • Tertiary alcohols (3°) resist oxidation under mild conditions

🎙️ Oxidation of alcohols is a fundamental method. Remember that primary alcohols give aldehydes, but you need a mild oxidizing agent like PCC to stop at the aldehyde stage. Secondary alcohols always give ketones. Tertiary alcohols are generally resistant to oxidation.

Preparation of Aldehydes & Ketones - II: Ozonolysis & Hydration

  • Ozonolysis of alkenes followed by reductive workup gives aldehydes/ketones
  • Hydration of alkynes (H2SO4, HgSO4) gives ketones (except ethyne)
  • Ethyne hydration gives ethanal (acetaldehyde)

🎙️ Ozonolysis is a powerful method to cleave double bonds and form carbonyl compounds. Hydration of alkynes is another important reaction; remember that only ethyne yields an aldehyde, all other alkynes give ketones due to Markovnikov's rule and tautomerism.

Preparation of Aldehydes - III: Rosenmund & Stephen Reactions

  • Rosenmund reduction: Acyl chlorides reduced to aldehydes using H2/Pd-BaSO4
  • BaSO4 acts as a poison to prevent further reduction to alcohol
  • Stephen reaction: Nitriles reduced to imines, then hydrolyzed to aldehydes

🎙️ These are named reactions specifically for aldehyde synthesis. Rosenmund reduction is excellent for converting acyl chlorides to aldehydes, with BaSO4 crucial for preventing over-reduction. The Stephen reaction uses SnCl2/HCl to reduce nitriles.

Preparation of Ketones - IV: From Acyl Chlorides & Nitriles

  • From Acyl Chlorides: React with dialkylcadmium (R2Cd), formed from Grignard
  • From Nitriles: React with Grignard reagents, followed by hydrolysis
  • Friedel-Crafts acylation: Benzene reacts with acyl chloride in presence of AlCl3

🎙️ Ketones can be prepared from acyl chlorides by reacting them with organocadmium compounds, which are less reactive than Grignard reagents. Grignard reagents also react with nitriles to form ketones after hydrolysis. Don't forget Friedel-Crafts acylation for aromatic ketones.

Physical Properties: Aldehydes & Ketones

  • Lower members (methanal, ethanal) are gases/volatile liquids
  • Higher members are liquids or solids
  • Boiling points are higher than non-polar compounds of similar mass (due to polarity)
  • Solubility in water decreases with increasing chain length

🎙️ Aldehydes and ketones have higher boiling points than non-polar compounds of comparable molecular mass due to dipole-dipole interactions. However, they are lower than alcohols because they cannot form intermolecular hydrogen bonds. Solubility decreases as the non-polar alkyl part increases.

Chemical Reactions: Nucleophilic Addition Reactions (NAR)

  • Characteristic reaction of aldehydes and ketones
  • Due to electrophilic nature of carbonyl carbon
  • Aldehydes are generally more reactive than ketones (less steric hindrance, less electron donation)

🎙️ Nucleophilic addition is the most important reaction type for aldehydes and ketones. The electrophilic carbon is attacked by a nucleophile. Remember that aldehydes are generally more reactive than ketones due to both electronic and steric factors.

NAR - Examples I: Addition of HCN & NaHSO3

  • Addition of Hydrogen Cyanide (HCN): Forms cyanohydrins
  • Cyanohydrins are important intermediates for further synthesis
  • Addition of Sodium Hydrogen Sulphite (NaHSO3): Forms crystalline bisulphite adducts

🎙️ The addition of HCN is a key reaction, forming cyanohydrins which can be hydrolyzed to alpha-hydroxy acids. The addition of sodium bisulphite forms a crystalline product, often used for purification of aldehydes and ketones.

Worked Example: NAR Mechanism

  • Step 1: Nucleophile attacks electrophilic carbonyl carbon
  • Step 2: Pi-bond breaks, electrons shift to oxygen, forming alkoxide ion
  • Step 3: Alkoxide ion protonated by H+ to form the final product

🎙️ Let's walk through a general mechanism for nucleophilic addition. First, the nucleophile attacks the partially positive carbonyl carbon. This pushes the pi-electrons onto the oxygen, forming an alkoxide intermediate. Finally, the alkoxide is protonated to yield the stable product.

Preparation of Carboxylic Acids - I: From Primary Alcohols & Aldehydes

  • Primary alcohols (1°) oxidize to carboxylic acids (strong oxidizing agents)
  • Aldehydes oxidize to carboxylic acids (mild or strong oxidizing agents)
  • Common oxidizing agents: KMnO4, K2Cr2O7, CrO3

🎙️ Carboxylic acids can be prepared by the oxidation of primary alcohols or aldehydes. Unlike aldehyde synthesis, here we use strong oxidizing agents like KMnO4 or K2Cr2O7 to ensure complete oxidation to the carboxylic acid.

Preparation of Carboxylic Acids - II: From Nitriles, Grignard Reagents

  • From Nitriles: Hydrolysis of nitriles (R-CN) in acidic or basic conditions
  • From Grignard Reagents: Reaction with carbon dioxide (CO2), followed by hydrolysis
  • From Acyl Halides and Anhydrides: Hydrolysis gives carboxylic acids

🎙️ Hydrolysis of nitriles is a good way to extend the carbon chain. The reaction of Grignard reagents with carbon dioxide is another excellent method, increasing the carbon chain by one carbon atom. Acyl halides and anhydrides also readily hydrolyze to carboxylic acids.

Physical Properties: Carboxylic Acids - Acidity & Boiling Point

  • Higher boiling points than aldehydes, ketones, and even alcohols (due to strong H-bonding)
  • Form dimeric structures in vapor phase and non-polar solvents
  • Soluble in water for lower members (up to 4 carbons) due to H-bonding

🎙️ Carboxylic acids have exceptionally high boiling points because they can form strong intermolecular hydrogen bonds, even forming stable dimeric structures. Their solubility in water is also due to hydrogen bonding, but it decreases as the non-polar alkyl chain grows.

Chemical Reactions - Carboxylic Acids I: Acidity & Salt Formation

  • Acidic nature: Donate H+ ion, forming carboxylate ion
  • Stabilized by resonance, making them stronger acids than alcohols
  • React with bases (NaOH), carbonates (Na2CO3), and bicarbonates (NaHCO3) to form salts

🎙️ The acidity of carboxylic acids is a crucial property, attributed to the resonance stabilization of the carboxylate anion. This makes them stronger acids than alcohols. They readily react with bases, carbonates, and bicarbonates, producing CO2 with the latter two.

Chemical Reactions - Carboxylic Acids II: Esterification & Decarboxylation

  • Esterification: Reaction with alcohols in presence of acid catalyst to form esters
  • Decarboxylation: Removal of CO2, typically by heating with soda lime (NaOH + CaO)
  • Reduction: To primary alcohols using LiAlH4 (strong reducing agent)

🎙️ Esterification is a very important reaction, forming pleasant-smelling esters. Decarboxylation is useful for shortening a carbon chain. Remember that carboxylic acids are resistant to mild reducing agents and require strong ones like LiAlH4 for reduction to alcohols.

Real-Life Applications: Aldehydes, Ketones, Carboxylic Acids

  • Formaldehyde: Preservative, polymer synthesis (Bakelite)
  • Acetone: Solvent, nail polish remover
  • Acetic acid: Vinegar, chemical reagent
  • Citric acid: Food additive, sour taste

🎙️ These compounds are not just theoretical; they are all around us! Formaldehyde is used in preserving biological specimens and making plastics. Acetone is a common solvent. Acetic acid is the main component of vinegar, and citric acid gives many fruits their sour taste.

Common Mistakes & Exam Tips

  • Distinguish between oxidation of 1° alcohol to aldehyde vs. carboxylic acid
  • Understand reactivity order: Aldehyde > Ketone (NAR)
  • Practice named reactions and their specific reagents
  • Focus on mechanisms for key reactions (e.g., NAR, esterification)

🎙️ Be careful with distinguishing between mild and strong oxidizing agents. Always remember that aldehydes are more reactive than ketones in nucleophilic addition.

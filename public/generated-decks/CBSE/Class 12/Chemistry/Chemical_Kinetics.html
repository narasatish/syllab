What is Reaction Rate?

  • Change in concentration of reactant or product per unit time.
  • Expressed as Δ[Concentration]/Δt.
  • Units: mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹ or M s⁻¹.
  • Rate is always positive, even for reactant consumption.

🎙️ The reaction rate quantifies how quickly reactants are consumed or products are formed. It's essentially the change in concentration over a specific time interval. Remember, by convention, the rate is always reported as a positive value.

Average vs. Instantaneous Rate

  • Average Rate: Measured over a long time interval.
  • Average Rate = -Δ[R]/Δt or +Δ[P]/Δt.
  • Instantaneous Rate: Rate at a specific moment in time.
  • Instantaneous Rate = -d[R]/dt or +d[P]/dt (slope of tangent).

🎙️ We distinguish between average rate, which is an overall rate over a period, and instantaneous rate, which gives us the precise speed at a single point in time. Instantaneous rate is often more useful for understanding reaction mechanisms.

Stoichiometry and Reaction Rate

  • Rates of consumption/formation depend on stoichiometric coefficients.
  • For aA + bB → cC + dD:
  • Rate = -1/a d[A]/dt = -1/b d[B]/dt = +1/c d[C]/dt = +1/d d[D]/dt.
  • Ensures a single, consistent rate for the overall reaction.

🎙️ Stoichiometry plays a crucial role in relating the rates of individual species to the overall reaction rate. We divide by the stoichiometric coefficient to normalize the rate, ensuring a consistent value for the entire reaction.

Effect of Concentration

  • Higher concentration, generally faster reaction.
  • More reactant molecules lead to more frequent collisions.
  • Directly incorporated into the Rate Law expression.
  • Not all reactions are equally sensitive to concentration changes.

🎙️ Increasing the concentration of reactants typically increases the reaction rate. This is because there are more reactant particles per unit volume, leading to a higher probability of effective collisions, which are necessary for a reaction to occur.

Rate Law Expression

  • Mathematical expression relating rate to reactant concentrations.
  • Rate = k[A]ˣ[B]ʸ.
  • Exponents x and y are orders with respect to A and B.
  • Determined experimentally, not from stoichiometric coefficients.

🎙️ The Rate Law is a fundamental concept. It's an experimentally determined equation that shows how the reaction rate depends on the concentrations of reactants. Crucially, the exponents in the rate law are not necessarily the stoichiometric coefficients.

Rate Constant (k)

  • Proportionality constant in the rate law.
  • Independent of reactant concentrations.
  • Specific for a reaction at a given temperature.
  • Units depend on the overall order of the reaction.

🎙️ The rate constant 'k' is a measure of the reaction's intrinsic speed. A larger 'k' means a faster reaction. Its value is unique for a particular reaction at a specific temperature and its units vary depending on the reaction order.

Order of Reaction

  • Sum of the exponents in the rate law expression (x + y).
  • Can be zero, integer, or fractional.
  • Determined experimentally.
  • Indicates sensitivity of rate to concentration changes.

🎙️ The order of reaction is a very important characteristic. It tells us how the rate changes when reactant concentrations are varied. Remember, it's an experimentally determined value and can be fractional or even zero.

Molecularity of a Reaction

  • Number of reacting species (atoms, ions, molecules) in an elementary step.
  • Always a positive integer (1, 2, or 3).
  • Cannot be zero, fractional, or greater than three.
  • Defined only for elementary reactions.

🎙️ Molecularity refers to the number of molecules participating in a single step of a reaction mechanism. It's a theoretical concept, always a whole number, and applies only to elementary reactions, not complex ones.

Order vs. Molecularity: Key Differences

  • Order: Experimental, for elementary or complex reactions, can be 0/fractional.
  • Molecularity: Theoretical, only for elementary reactions, always integer.
  • Order refers to overall reaction; Molecularity to each step.
  • Order can change with conditions; Molecularity is fixed.

🎙️ This slide highlights crucial distinctions. Order is an experimental observation, while molecularity is a theoretical concept based on the reaction mechanism. Students often confuse these, so it's important to differentiate clearly.

Integrated Rate Equations: Zero Order

  • Rate is independent of reactant concentration.
  • Rate = k.
  • Integrated form: [R] = [R]₀ - kt.
  • Plot of [R] vs. t is a straight line with slope -k.

🎙️ For a zero-order reaction, the rate remains constant regardless of how much reactant is present. This is common when a catalyst surface is saturated or when one reactant is in vast excess. The integrated form allows us to calculate concentration at any time.

Integrated Rate Equations: First Order

  • Rate is directly proportional to reactant concentration.
  • Rate = k[R].
  • Integrated form: ln[R] = ln[R]₀ - kt.
  • Plot of ln[R] vs. t is a straight line with slope -k.

🎙️ First-order reactions are very common, especially in radioactive decay. The rate depends linearly on the concentration of one reactant. The integrated rate law is logarithmic, making a plot of ln[R] against time a straight line.

Half-Life (t₁/₂)

  • Time required for reactant concentration to reduce to half its initial value.
  • Zero Order: t₁/₂ = [R]₀ / 2k.
  • First Order: t₁/₂ = 0.693 / k (independent of initial concentration).
  • Useful for characterizing reaction speed.

🎙️ Half-life is a practical measure of reaction speed. For first-order reactions, it's constant, which is a powerful characteristic. For zero-order, it depends on the initial concentration.

Worked Example: First Order Half-Life

  • A first-order reaction has a rate constant of 5.5 × 10⁻¹⁴ s⁻¹.
  • Calculate its half-life.
  • Solution: t₁/₂ = 0.693 / k.
  • t₁/₂ = 0.693 / (5.5 × 10⁻¹⁴ s⁻¹) ≈ 1.26 × 10¹³ s.

Effect of Temperature: Arrhenius Equation

  • Rate generally increases with temperature.
  • Arrhenius equation: k = A e^(-Ea/RT).
  • Ea: Activation Energy (minimum energy for effective collision).
  • A: Arrhenius factor/Pre-exponential factor (frequency of collisions).

🎙️ Temperature has a profound effect on reaction rates. The Arrhenius equation quantifies this relationship, introducing the crucial concept of activation energy, which is the energy barrier reactants must overcome to form products.

Activation Energy (Ea)

  • Minimum extra energy required by reacting molecules to form products.
  • Reactants must cross an energy barrier.
  • Higher Ea means slower reaction.
  • Catalysts lower Ea.

🎙️ Think of activation energy as a hill that molecules must climb before they can react. Only a fraction of molecules possess enough energy to overcome this barrier at any given temperature. Catalysts work by providing an alternative, lower energy pathway.

Collision Theory

  • Reactant molecules must collide to react.
  • Collisions must have sufficient energy (Ea) to be effective.
  • Molecules must collide with proper orientation.
  • Explains factors like concentration and temperature.

🎙️ Collision theory provides a microscopic view of reactions. It states that not all collisions lead to a reaction; only those with enough energy and proper orientation are effective. This helps explain why rates increase with concentration and temperature.

Effect of Catalyst

  • Substance that alters reaction rate without being consumed.
  • Provides an alternative reaction pathway with lower activation energy.
  • Does not change ΔG or equilibrium constant.
  • Positive catalysts increase rate; negative catalysts (inhibitors) decrease rate.

🎙️ Catalysts are fascinating! They act as shortcuts, lowering the energy barrier, thereby speeding up reactions. Remember, they participate in the reaction but are regenerated, and they don't affect the thermodynamics of the reaction.

Real-Life Examples of Kinetics

  • Rusting of iron (slow oxidation, prevented by coating).

🎙️ Chemical kinetics isn't just theoretical; it's all around us! From preserving food to designing industrial processes and understanding biological functions, the principles of reaction rates are applied daily.

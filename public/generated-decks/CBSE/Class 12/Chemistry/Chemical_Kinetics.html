Defining Reaction Rate

  • Change in concentration of reactant/product.
  • Per unit time.
  • Expressed as -Δ[R]/Δt or +Δ[P]/Δt.

🎙️ The rate of a reaction quantifies how quickly reactants are consumed or products are formed. It's essentially the change in concentration of a species divided by the time taken for that change.

📈
Concentration over Time

Average vs. Instantaneous Rate

  • Average: Over a finite time interval.
  • Instantaneous: At a specific moment in time.
  • Instantaneous rate is the slope of tangent.

🎙️ Imagine driving a car: your average speed over a journey is different from your speed at a specific moment. Similarly, average rate is calculated over a time period, while instantaneous rate is the rate at a precise point in time, often more relevant for understanding mechanisms.

Units of Reaction Rate

  • Concentration per unit time.
  • Commonly mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹.
  • For gases, atm s⁻¹ or bar s⁻¹.

🎙️ Since rate involves change in concentration over time, its standard unit is moles per liter per second. For gaseous reactions, we often use pressure units like atmospheres or bars per second.

Factor 1: Concentration's Influence

  • Higher concentration, more collisions.
  • Increased collision frequency.
  • Leads to faster reaction rate.

🎙️ Think of a crowded room. The more people there are, the more likely they are to bump into each other. Similarly, higher reactant concentration means more particles per unit volume, leading to more frequent collisions and thus a faster reaction.

🧪🧪🧪🧪🧪🧪🧪🧪
More Particles, More Collisions
9 / 25

Factor 2: Temperature's Influence

  • Higher temperature, higher kinetic energy.
  • More effective collisions.
  • Significant increase in rate (often doubles for every 10°C rise).

🎙️ Increasing temperature gives reactant molecules more kinetic energy. This means they move faster and collide more forcefully. Crucially, a larger fraction of collisions will have energy equal to or greater than the activation energy, leading to a much faster rate.

🔥
Energy Boost

Factor 3: Role of a Catalyst

  • Substance that alters reaction rate.
  • Without being consumed.
  • Provides an alternate reaction pathway.

🎙️ A catalyst is a remarkable substance that can significantly speed up a reaction without itself being used up. It does this by offering a different, lower-energy pathway for the reaction to occur, effectively reducing the activation energy.

⚡
Speed Booster

Factor 4: Surface Area & Pressure

  • Solids: Increased surface area, faster rate.
  • Gases: Increased pressure (concentration), faster rate.
  • More contact points for reaction.

🎙️ For reactions involving solid reactants, breaking them into smaller pieces increases their surface area, providing more sites for reaction. For gaseous reactions, increasing pressure is equivalent to increasing concentration, leading to more frequent collisions.

The Rate Law Expression

  • Mathematical relationship.
  • Relates rate to reactant concentrations.
  • Rate = k[A]ˣ[B]ʸ.

🎙️ The rate law is a crucial mathematical expression that quantitatively links the reaction rate to the concentrations of the reactants. The exponents in the rate law, 'x' and 'y', are not necessarily the stoichiometric coefficients but are determined experimentally.

Order of Reaction

  • Sum of the powers of concentration terms.
  • In the rate law expression.
  • Determined experimentally, can be 0, 1, 2, or fractional.

🎙️ The order of reaction tells us how the rate depends on the concentration of each reactant. It's the sum of the exponents in the rate law and is always determined experimentally, never from the balanced chemical equation directly.

Molecularity vs. Order

  • Molecularity: Number of reacting species in an elementary step.
  • Order: Sum of powers of concentration terms in rate law.
  • Molecularity is theoretical, Order is experimental.

🎙️ This is a common point of confusion. Molecularity refers to the number of molecules involved in a single, elementary step of a reaction mechanism. Order, on the other hand, is an experimentally determined value that describes the overall concentration dependence of the rate.

Zero Order Reactions

  • Rate is independent of reactant concentration.
  • Rate = k[A]⁰ = k.
  • Integrated rate law: [A] = [A]₀ - kt.

🎙️ In a zero-order reaction, the rate remains constant regardless of how much reactant is present, as long as there's some reactant. This often happens when the reaction is limited by something else, like the availability of active sites on a catalyst.

0️⃣
Constant Rate
17 / 25

First Order Reactions

  • Rate is directly proportional to reactant concentration.
  • Rate = k[A]¹.
  • Integrated rate law: ln[A] = ln[A]₀ - kt.

🎙️ First-order reactions are very common, especially in radioactive decay. Here, the rate of reaction decreases as the reactant concentration decreases, meaning it's directly proportional to the amount of reactant present.

Arrhenius Equation: Temperature & Rate

  • Quantifies temperature's effect on rate constant 'k'.
  • k = A e⁻ᴱᵃ/ᴿᵀ.
  • A = Arrhenius factor, Eₐ = Activation energy.

🎙️ The Arrhenius equation is a cornerstone of chemical kinetics, mathematically describing how the rate constant, and thus the reaction rate, changes with temperature. It introduces the concepts of activation energy and the frequency factor, both crucial for understanding reaction mechanisms.

Activation Energy (Eₐ)

  • Minimum energy required for reactants to form products.
  • Energy barrier for a reaction.
  • Catalysts lower activation energy.

🎙️ Think of activation energy as a hill that reactants must climb before they can transform into products. Only collisions with energy equal to or greater than this barrier will be effective in forming products, leading to a successful reaction.

Catalysis: Mechanism & Types

  • Homogeneous: Catalyst in same phase as reactants.
  • Heterogeneous: Catalyst in different phase.
  • Enzymes: Biological catalysts.

🎙️ Catalysis is vital in industry and biology. Catalysts work by providing an alternative reaction pathway with a lower activation energy. We distinguish between homogeneous and heterogeneous catalysis based on the phases of reactants and catalyst, with enzymes being a key biological example.

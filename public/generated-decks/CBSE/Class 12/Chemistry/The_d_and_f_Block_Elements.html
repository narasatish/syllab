Introduction to d-Block Elements (Transition Elements)

  • Elements with incompletely filled d-subshell in their atomic or ionic state.
  • Located between s- and p-block elements in the periodic table (Groups 3-12).
  • Form four series: 3d, 4d, 5d, and 6d series.

🎙️ Transition elements are defined by their partially filled d-orbitals. This unique electronic configuration is responsible for many of their characteristic properties. They act as a bridge between the highly electropositive s-block and the electronegative p-block elements.

Electronic Configuration of d-Block Elements

  • General configuration: (n-1)d¹⁻¹⁰ ns¹⁻².
  • Exceptions occur due to stability of half-filled or completely filled d-orbitals.
  • Examples: Cr ([Ar]3d⁵4s¹) and Cu ([Ar]3d¹⁰4s¹).

🎙️ The general electronic configuration is key, but remember the exceptions like Chromium and Copper. These exceptions arise because half-filled or completely filled d-orbitals provide extra stability to the atom. Always prioritize stability when writing configurations.

Worked Example: Electronic Configuration of Mn and Fe²⁺

  • Manganese (Mn, Z=25): [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s².
  • Iron (Fe, Z=26): [Ar] 3d⁶ 4s².
  • Fe²⁺ ion: Electrons are removed from the 4s orbital first, then 3d. So, [Ar] 3d⁶.

🎙️ Let's apply this. For Mn, we fill 3d and 4s as per Aufbau principle. For ions like Fe²⁺, remember that electrons are always removed from the outermost shell first, which is the 4s orbital in this case, even though 3d is filled after 4s.

Common Mistake: Removing Electrons from d-Block Ions

  • Incorrectly removing electrons from (n-1)d before ns.
  • Always remove electrons from the ns orbital first, as it is the outermost shell.
  • Example: For Fe²⁺, 4s electrons are lost, not 3d.

🎙️ This is a frequent error in exams. When forming ions, the electrons are lost from the highest principal quantum number shell first. For transition metals, this means the ns electrons are removed before the (n-1)d electrons. Be very careful here!

Atomic and Ionic Sizes of d-Block Elements

  • Generally decrease across a period due to increasing nuclear charge.
  • Increases down a group due to addition of new shells.
  • 5d series elements are very similar in size to 4d due to Lanthanoid Contraction.

🎙️ Across a period, the effective nuclear charge increases, pulling the electrons closer and decreasing size. Down a group, new shells are added, increasing size. However, the 5d series shows a unique behavior due to the intervention of f-block elements, leading to Lanthanoid Contraction.

Metallic Character of d-Block Elements

  • All d-block elements are typical metals.
  • High tensile strength, ductility, malleability.
  • High thermal and electrical conductivity, metallic luster.

🎙️ Transition metals exhibit all the classic properties of metals: they are strong, can be drawn into wires or hammered into sheets, and conduct heat and electricity well. This is due to the presence of delocalized electrons in their metallic lattice.

Ionization Enthalpy of d-Block Elements

  • Generally increases across a period, but irregularly.
  • Successive ionization enthalpies show significant jumps.
  • Higher IE values compared to s-block but lower than p-block.

🎙️ Ionization enthalpy generally increases across a period due to increasing nuclear charge, but the trend is not as smooth as in s-block elements due to varying shielding by d-electrons. The removal of successive electrons requires significantly more energy.

Variable Oxidation States of d-Block Elements

  • Exhibit multiple oxidation states, unlike s- and p-block elements.
  • Due to participation of both ns and (n-1)d electrons in bonding.
  • Highest oxidation state often seen in the middle of a series (e.g., Mn +7).

🎙️ The ability to show variable oxidation states is a hallmark of transition metals. This arises because the energy difference between the ns and (n-1)d orbitals is very small, allowing both sets of electrons to participate in bonding. This leads to a wide range of chemical compounds.

Redox reactions involve electron transfer between reactants. Oxidation = loss of electrons

Worked Example: Highest Oxidation State of Mn

  • Manganese (Mn, Z=25) configuration: [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s².
  • It can lose 2 electrons from 4s and all 5 electrons from 3d.
  • Maximum oxidation state of Mn is +7 (in KMnO₄).

🎙️ Consider Manganese. It has 7 valence electrons (2 from 4s and 5 from 3d). It can lose all of them to achieve a stable configuration, resulting in a +7 oxidation state, as seen in potassium permanganate. This illustrates the maximum variability.

Magnetic Properties of d-Block Elements

  • Paramagnetism: Due to presence of unpaired electrons, attracted by magnetic field.
  • Diamagnetism: All electrons are paired, repelled by magnetic field.
  • Magnetic moment (μ) calculated using spin-only formula: μ = √n(n+2) BM.

🎙️ The magnetic properties of transition metal compounds are directly related to the number of unpaired electrons. Paramagnetic substances are attracted to a magnetic field, while diamagnetic substances are repelled. The magnetic moment helps us quantify this behavior and deduce the number of unpaired electrons.

Colour of Transition Metal Ions

  • Most transition metal compounds are coloured in solid or solution state.
  • Due to d-d transitions: absorption of light energy causes electrons to jump between d-orbitals.
  • The colour observed is the complementary colour of the light absorbed.

🎙️ The vibrant colours of transition metal compounds are one of their most striking features. This phenomenon is explained by d-d transitions, where electrons absorb specific wavelengths of visible light to move to higher energy d-orbitals. The colour we perceive is the light that is not absorbed.

Catalytic Properties of d-Block Elements

  • Many transition metals and their compounds act as excellent catalysts.
  • Due to variable oxidation states and ability to form unstable intermediates.
  • Large surface area and adsorption of reactants also contribute.

🎙️ Transition metals are widely used as catalysts in various industrial processes, such as the Haber process or hydrogenation reactions. Their variable oxidation states allow them to readily change their redox state, forming temporary bonds with reactants and lowering activation energy.

Metals form positively charged ions by losing electrons. Reactive metals react with oxygen

Formation of Interstitial Compounds

  • Small atoms (H, C, N, B) get trapped in the interstitial voids of metal lattices.
  • Formed by transition metals due to their relatively large atomic sizes.
  • These compounds are non-stoichiometric, hard, high melting, and chemically inert.

🎙️ Interstitial compounds are fascinating. Small non-metal atoms fit into the spaces within the crystal lattice of transition metals. This significantly alters the metal's properties, making them harder and more resistant to chemical attack, without forming true chemical bonds.

Formation of Alloys

  • Transition metals readily form alloys with other transition metals.
  • Due to similar atomic sizes and metallic characteristics.
  • Alloys often have superior properties (e.g., strength, corrosion resistance) than pure metals.

🎙️ The similar atomic radii of transition metals allow them to substitute for each other in the crystal lattice, forming solid solutions or alloys. These alloys are crucial in engineering and industry, offering enhanced properties like increased strength or corrosion resistance.

Introduction to f-Block Elements (Inner Transition Elements)

  • Elements where the 4f or 5f orbitals are progressively filled.
  • Divided into two series: Lanthanoids (4f series) and Actinoids (5f series).
  • Placed separately at the bottom of the periodic table.

🎙️ The f-block elements are also known as inner transition elements because their differentiating electrons enter the antepenultimate, or (n-2)f, subshell. They are divided into two distinct series based on the filling of 4f and 5f orbitals.

Lanthanoid Contraction

  • Gradual decrease in atomic and ionic radii across the lanthanoid series.
  • Due to poor shielding effect of 4f electrons.
  • Consequences: Similar radii of 4d and 5d elements, difficulty in separation.

🎙️ Lanthanoid contraction is a very important concept. The 4f electrons are very diffuse and provide poor shielding of the nuclear charge. As the atomic number increases, the effective nuclear charge increases, pulling the outer electrons closer and causing a steady decrease in size.

Electronic Configuration of f-Block Elements

  • General configuration: (n-2)f¹⁻¹⁴ (n-1)d⁰⁻¹ ns².
  • For Lanthanoids: [Xe] 4f¹⁻¹⁴ 5d⁰⁻¹ 6s².
  • For Actinoids: [Rn] 5f¹⁻¹⁴ 6d⁰⁻¹ 7s².

🎙️ The electronic configuration of f-block elements is complex due to the close energy levels of f, d, and s orbitals. For lanthanoids, the 5d orbital often remains empty or has only one electron before the 4f orbitals are completely filled.

Oxidation States of f-Block Elements

  • Lanthanoids: +3 is the most common and stable oxidation state.
  • Some show +2 and +4 due to stability of empty, half-filled, or full f-orbitals.
  • Actinoids: Exhibit a wider range of oxidation states (e.g., +3, +4, +5, +6, +7).

🎙️ For lanthanoids, +3 is overwhelmingly the most stable oxidation state. However, some elements can exhibit +2 or +4 if it leads to an empty, half-filled, or completely filled f-subshell. Actinoids, in contrast, show much greater variability in oxidation states.

Actinoids: Properties and Comparison with Lanthanoids

  • Radioactive, larger atomic/ionic sizes than corresponding lanthanoids.
  • Greater tendency for complex formation and variable oxidation states.
  • Actinoid contraction is more pronounced than lanthanoid contraction.

🎙️ Actinoids are distinct from lanthanoids in several ways. They are all radioactive, and their chemistry is more complex due to the more diffuse 5f orbitals participating readily in bonding. Their contraction effect is also more significant.

Recap: Key Takeaways from d- and f- Block Elements

  • d-block: Variable oxidation states, coloured ions, magnetic properties, catalytic activity.
  • f-block: Lanthanoid contraction, +3 common oxidation state, radioactivity of actinoids.
  • Electronic configuration and exceptions are critical for both.

The 'Bridge' Elements: Understanding d-Block

  • d-block elements are located between s- and p-blocks in the periodic table.
  • Often called 'Transition Elements' due to their intermediate properties.
  • Characterized by incompletely filled d-orbitals in their ground state or common oxidation states.

🎙️ Just like a bridge connects two landmasses, d-block elements connect the highly reactive s-block metals with the more non-metallic p-block elements. They exhibit properties that are 'transitional' between the two.

Why Some Elements Break the Rules? (Cr, Cu)

  • Chromium (Cr) and Copper (Cu) are common exceptions.
  • Cr: [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹ (instead of 3d⁴ 4s²)
  • Cu: [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹ (instead of 3d⁹ 4s²)
  • Reason: Extra stability of half-filled (d⁵) and completely filled (d¹⁰) d-orbitals.

🎙️ This is a very common exam question! Always remember these exceptions and the reason behind them. Half-filled and fully-filled orbitals lead to greater symmetry and exchange energy, making them more stable.

Strong Bonds: The Metallic Nature of d-Block

  • All d-block elements are metals.
  • High tensile strength, ductility, malleability.
  • High thermal and electrical conductivity.
  • High melting and boiling points due to strong metallic bonding (delocalized d-electrons).

🎙️ Imagine a team where everyone contributes their strength; similarly, the delocalized d-electrons contribute to very strong metallic bonds, leading to their characteristic properties like high melting points.

Shrinking & Growing: Radii Trends Across the Series

  • Atomic radii generally decrease across a period initially.
  • This is due to increasing nuclear charge and poor shielding by d-electrons.
  • Towards the end of the series, repulsion between paired d-electrons causes a slight increase.

🎙️ The trend isn't as straightforward as in s- or p-blocks. While effective nuclear charge increases, the shielding effect of d-electrons is not perfect, leading to a net contraction initially, followed by a slight expansion.

Energy to Remove: Ionisation Enthalpy

  • Ionisation enthalpy generally increases across a transition series.
  • This is due to increasing nuclear charge and decreasing atomic size.
  • Irregularities arise from stable d⁵ and d¹⁰ configurations.

🎙️ Think of it like trying to pull a magnet away from a surface. The stronger the attraction (higher nuclear charge, smaller size), the more energy you need. Stable electronic configurations also make it harder to remove electrons.

Many Faces: Variable Oxidation States

  • d-block elements exhibit multiple oxidation states.
  • Both ns and (n-1)d electrons are available for bonding.
  • The number of oxidation states increases up to the middle of the series (e.g., Mn) and then decreases.

🎙️ This is a hallmark property of transition elements. Unlike s-block elements with typically one oxidation state, d-block elements can change their 'face' by using different combinations of ns and (n-1)d electrons in chemical reactions.

The Rainbow Effect: Why d-Block Ions are Coloured

  • Most transition metal compounds are coloured in solid or solution state.
  • This is due to d-d transitions.
  • When white light falls on the compound, it absorbs certain wavelengths and transmits the complementary colour.

🎙️ Imagine a prism splitting light. Similarly, when electrons in d-orbitals absorb energy (from visible light) and jump to higher energy d-orbitals, the unabsorbed light is transmitted, giving the compound its characteristic colour. No d-electrons (d⁰) or fully filled d-orbitals (d¹⁰) mean no d-d transitions, hence colourless ions.

Attraction & Repulsion: Paramagnetism & Diamagnetism

  • Paramagnetic substances are weakly attracted by a magnetic field.
  • They have unpaired electrons.
  • Diamagnetic substances are weakly repelled by a magnetic field.
  • They have all electrons paired.

🎙️ The presence of unpaired electrons gives rise to a magnetic moment, making the substance paramagnetic. The more unpaired electrons, the stronger the paramagnetism. Zinc, with its fully filled d-orbitals, is diamagnetic.

Speeding Up Reactions: d-Block as Catalysts

  • Many d-block elements and their compounds act as excellent catalysts.

  • Reasons: Variable oxidation states, large surface area, ability to form intermediate compounds.

🎙️ Think of a catalyst as a matchmaker, bringing reactants together and speeding up their reaction without being consumed itself. Transition metals are perfect for this due to their unique electronic structure.

Tiny Guests: Interstitial Compound Formation

  • Transition metals form interstitial compounds with small non-metals (H, C, N, B).
  • These small atoms occupy the interstitial voids (gaps) in the metal lattice.
  • Result in increased hardness, density, and retained metallic conductivity.

🎙️ Imagine a wall made of large bricks, and then small pebbles filling the tiny gaps between them. These 'tiny guests' don't chemically bond in the traditional sense but significantly alter the physical properties of the metal.

Electron Exchange: Standard Electrode Potentials (E°)

  • E° values indicate the tendency of an element to gain or lose electrons.
  • Generally, E° values are negative, indicating a tendency to lose electrons (oxidize).
  • Irregularities exist due to factors like hydration enthalpy and ionisation enthalpy.

🎙️ Standard electrode potentials help us understand the reactivity of these metals. While most transition metals are good reducing agents, the exact value depends on a delicate balance of energy changes involved in forming the aqueous ion.

The 'Inner' Transition Elements: Understanding f-Block

  • f-block elements are located separately at the bottom of the periodic table.
  • Divided into Lanthanoids (4f series) and Actinoids (5f series).
  • Characterized by filling of f-orbitals.

🎙️ If d-block elements are like the outer layers of an onion, f-block elements are the innermost, deeply buried layers. Their electrons are even more shielded, leading to some very unique properties.

Lanthanoids: Filling the 4f Orbitals

  • Elements from Cerium (Ce) to Lutetium (Lu).
  • General electronic configuration: [Xe] 4f¹⁻¹⁴ 5d⁰⁻¹ 6s².
  • Most common oxidation state is +3.

🎙️ These elements are also known as rare earth elements, though they are not as rare as their name suggests. Their chemistry is largely dominated by the +3 oxidation state, as the 4f electrons are well-shielded.

The Lanthanoid Shrink: Understanding Contraction

  • Steady decrease in atomic and ionic radii (M³⁺) from Ce to Lu.
  • Reason: Poor shielding effect of 4f electrons.
  • Effective nuclear charge increases, pulling electrons closer to the nucleus.

🎙️ Imagine a rope tightening around a bundle. As you add more nuclear charge, the 4f electrons, being poor at shielding each other, fail to effectively screen the outer electrons. This causes a gradual, but significant, decrease in size.

Impact of Contraction: Similarities in 2nd & 3rd Series

  • Consequence 1: Similar radii of elements in 2nd (4d) and 3rd (5d) transition series.
  • Example: Zr (Zirconium) and Hf (Hafnium) have almost identical radii.
  • This leads to very similar chemical properties, making their separation difficult.

🎙️ This is a critical concept for exams! The lanthanoid contraction effectively cancels out the expected increase in size when moving from the 4d to 5d series. This makes elements like Zirconium and Hafnium behave almost identically.

Actinoids: Filling the 5f Orbitals

  • Elements from Thorium (Th) to Lawrencium (Lr).
  • General electronic configuration: [Rn] 5f¹⁻¹⁴ 6d⁰⁻¹ 7s².
  • Exhibit a wider range of oxidation states compared to lanthanoids.

🎙️ Actinoids are often radioactive and their chemistry is more complex due to the participation of 5f, 6d, and 7s electrons in bonding, leading to a wider variety of oxidation states.

Lanthies vs. Actinies: Key Differences

  • Lanthanoids: Poor shielding by 4f, common +3 oxidation state, less tendency to form complexes.
  • Actinoids: Even poorer shielding by 5f, wider range of oxidation states, highly radioactive, form complexes readily.

🎙️ While both exhibit contraction, the 5f electrons in actinoids are even less effective at shielding than 4f electrons. This leads to more prominent contraction and greater variability in their chemical behaviour.

Don't Get Tricked! Common Mistakes

  • Confusing d-block with f-block properties (e.g., shielding effects).
  • Forgetting electronic configuration exceptions (Cr, Cu).
  • Misunderstanding the consequences of lanthanoid contraction (similarities of 4d & 5d elements).
  • Incorrectly identifying paramagnetic vs. diamagnetic species.

🎙️ Students often lose marks on these tricky points. Always double-check electronic configurations and remember the specific reasons for trends and exceptions. Practice identifying unpaired electrons for magnetic properties.

Practice Problem: Calculating Magnetic Moment

  • Calculate the spin-only magnetic moment of Mn²⁺ ion.
  • Atomic number of Mn = 25.
  • Formula: μ = √n(n+2) BM, where n = number of unpaired electrons.

🎙️ Let's work this out. Mn has [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s². For Mn²⁺, two electrons are lost from 4s, so it becomes [Ar] 3d⁵. This means n=5 unpaired electrons. Plugging into the formula, μ = √5(5+2) = √35 ≈ 5.92 BM.

Chapter in a Nutshell: Key Takeaways

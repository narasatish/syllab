What are Biomolecules?

  • Complex organic molecules forming living organisms.
  • Essential for life processes like growth, metabolism, and reproduction.
  • Include carbohydrates, proteins, nucleic acids, lipids, and vitamins.

🎙️ Biomolecules are essentially the organic compounds found within living organisms. They are the chemical machinery that allows life to exist and function, from the smallest cell to the largest organism. We will focus on the main types in detail.

Carbohydrates: Energy Houses

  • Polyhydroxy aldehydes or ketones, or compounds that yield them on hydrolysis.
  • General formula: Cx(H2O)y (often, not always).
  • Primary source of energy for living systems.

🎙️ Carbohydrates are one of the most abundant biomolecules on Earth. Think of them as the body's primary fuel. They are broadly classified based on the number of sugar units they contain after hydrolysis.

Monosaccharides: Simple Sugars

  • Simplest carbohydrates, cannot be hydrolyzed further.
  • Examples: Glucose (aldohexose), Fructose (ketohexose), Ribose.
  • Classified by carbon atoms: trioses, tetroses, pentoses, hexoses.

🎙️ Monosaccharides are the basic building blocks of all carbohydrates. Glucose is the most important one, often called dextrose, and is found in blood sugar. Fructose is known as fruit sugar and is the sweetest natural sugar.

Disaccharides: Two Sugars

  • Formed by two monosaccharide units joined by a glycosidic linkage.
  • Examples: Sucrose (glucose + fructose), Lactose (glucose + galactose), Maltose (glucose + glucose).
  • Hydrolyze to yield two monosaccharide molecules.

🎙️ Disaccharides are formed when two monosaccharides link together, losing a water molecule. Sucrose, or common table sugar, is a non-reducing sugar. Lactose is milk sugar, and Maltose is found in germinating grains.

Polysaccharides: Complex Chains

  • Contain a large number of monosaccharide units joined by glycosidic linkages.
  • Examples: Starch (plant energy storage), Cellulose (plant structural material), Glycogen (animal energy storage).
  • Often tasteless and insoluble in water.

🎙️ Polysaccharides are polymers of monosaccharides. Starch is a major dietary source and consists of amylose and amylopectin. Cellulose provides structural rigidity to plants, while glycogen is the animal equivalent of starch, stored mainly in liver and muscles.

Structure of Glucose: Open Chain & Cyclic

  • Open chain structure: Pentahydroxy aldehyde (CHO group, 5 -OH groups).
  • Cyclic structure: Formed by intramolecular hemiacetal formation (α- and β-anomers).
  • Cyclic form explains mutarotation and stability.

🎙️ While glucose exists in an open-chain form, its cyclic structures, specifically the α and β anomers, are more predominant in aqueous solutions. The cyclic structure is a six-membered ring called pyranose, formed by the reaction of the aldehyde group with the C5 hydroxyl group.

Worked Example: Glucose Reactions

  • Oxidation: With bromine water gives gluconic acid (aldose test).
  • Reduction: With HI and red P gives n-hexane.
  • Fehling's/Tollens' test: Glucose is a reducing sugar, gives positive test.

🎙️ Understanding glucose's reactions helps confirm its structure. Its oxidation to gluconic acid by a mild oxidizing agent like bromine water indicates the presence of an aldehyde group. Reduction to n-hexane confirms the straight-chain nature of carbon atoms.

Proteins: The Workhorses of Life

  • Essential for structure, function, and regulation of body tissues and organs.
  • Highly specific biological functions: enzymes, hormones, antibodies.

🎙️ Proteins are incredibly diverse and perform a vast array of functions in the body. They are literally the workhorses, building structures, catalyzing reactions, transporting molecules, and defending against pathogens. Their primary building blocks are amino acids.

Amino Acids: Building Blocks

  • Contain both an amino (-NH2) and a carboxyl (-COOH) group.
  • α-amino acids have both groups attached to the same carbon atom (α-carbon).
  • Classified as essential (from diet) or non-essential (synthesized in body).

🎙️ There are 20 common α-amino acids that make up proteins. Essential amino acids like valine, leucine, and lysine must be obtained from our diet as our bodies cannot synthesize them. Non-essential ones, like glycine and alanine, can be made by the body.

Peptide Bond Formation

  • Formed between the carboxyl group of one amino acid and the amino group of another.
  • A covalent amide linkage (-CO-NH-).
  • Water molecule is eliminated during formation.

🎙️ When two amino acids react, they form a dipeptide. The bond formed is called a peptide bond. This process can repeat, forming long chains called polypeptides, which eventually fold into functional proteins.

Levels of Protein Structure

  • Primary: Sequence of amino acids.
  • Secondary: Local folding (α-helix, β-pleated sheet) due to H-bonds.
  • Tertiary: 3D folding of the polypeptide chain.
  • Quaternary: Arrangement of multiple polypeptide subunits.

🎙️ Protein function is intimately linked to its precise three-dimensional structure. Each level of structure is crucial; even a small change in the primary sequence can drastically alter the protein's function, leading to diseases like sickle cell anemia.

Denaturation of Proteins

  • Loss of a protein's secondary, tertiary, or quaternary structure.
  • Caused by changes in pH, temperature, or presence of salts/solvents.
  • Results in loss of biological activity, often irreversible.

🎙️ Denaturation is the process where a protein loses its native shape and thus its biological activity. Think of boiling an egg – the clear liquid protein (albumin) turns solid and opaque, a classic example of irreversible denaturation. This is because the delicate interactions maintaining its 3D structure are disrupted.

Vitamins: Essential Micronutrients

  • Organic compounds required in small amounts for normal growth and metabolism.
  • Cannot be synthesized by the body (or in sufficient quantity).
  • Classified as fat-soluble (A, D, E, K) or water-soluble (B-complex, C).

🎙️ Vitamins are crucial for maintaining good health, though needed in tiny amounts. Their deficiency can lead to specific diseases. Fat-soluble vitamins can be stored in the body, while water-soluble ones are generally excreted, requiring regular intake.

Nucleic Acids: Genetic Information Carriers

  • Polymers of nucleotides.
  • Responsible for heredity, protein synthesis, and genetic information storage.
  • Two main types: Deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) and Ribonucleic acid (RNA).

🎙️ Nucleic acids are the blueprint of life. DNA carries the genetic instructions for the development, functioning, growth, and reproduction of all known organisms. RNA plays a vital role in expressing these instructions, primarily in protein synthesis.

Components of Nucleic Acids

  • Pentose Sugar: Deoxyribose (in DNA) or Ribose (in RNA).
  • Nitrogenous Bases: Purines (Adenine, Guanine) and Pyrimidines (Cytosine, Thymine, Uracil).
  • Phosphate Group: Links sugar units together.

🎙️ Each nucleotide is made of three parts: a sugar, a base, and a phosphate. The sugar and phosphate form the backbone, while the nitrogenous bases carry the genetic code. DNA has Thymine, while RNA has Uracil instead.

DNA Structure: The Double Helix

  • Proposed by Watson and Crick.
  • Two polynucleotide strands coiled around a common axis.
  • Strands are antiparallel and held by hydrogen bonds between complementary bases.
  • A pairs with T (2 H-bonds), G pairs with C (3 H-bonds).

🎙️ The double helix structure of DNA is one of the most significant discoveries in biology. The specific base pairing ensures accurate replication and transcription of genetic information. This elegant structure is fundamental to heredity.

RNA Types and Functions

  • Messenger RNA (mRNA): Carries genetic code from DNA to ribosomes.
  • Ribosomal RNA (rRNA): Forms ribosomes, sites of protein synthesis.
  • Transfer RNA (tRNA): Carries specific amino acids to ribosomes for protein synthesis.

🎙️ Unlike DNA, RNA is typically single-stranded and has diverse functions. Each type of RNA plays a distinct and crucial role in the central dogma of molecular biology: DNA to RNA to Protein. They are all essential for life.

Hormones & Enzymes: Biocatalysts

  • Enzymes: Biological catalysts, mostly globular proteins.
  • Accelerate biochemical reactions without being consumed.
  • Hormones: Chemical messengers, produced by endocrine glands.
  • Regulate physiological processes (e.g., insulin, adrenaline).

🎙️ Enzymes are highly specific and efficient, making life processes possible at body temperature. Hormones act as signaling molecules, maintaining homeostasis and coordinating various bodily functions. Both are crucial for organism survival.

Worked Example: Identifying Biomolecules

  • Q: A biomolecule gives a positive Tollens' test and on hydrolysis gives only glucose. Identify it.
  • A: Positive Tollens' test implies it's a reducing sugar. Hydrolysis yielding only glucose suggests it's a polysaccharide like starch or glycogen, or a disaccharide like maltose. Given 'only glucose', Maltose is a strong candidate.

🎙️ This type of question tests your understanding of both structure and reactions. Remember that starch and glycogen yield many glucose units, whereas maltose yields two. The Tollens' test confirms the reducing nature.

Common Mistakes & Exam Tips

  • Confusing reducing/non-reducing sugars (e.g., Sucrose is non-reducing).
  • Incorrectly drawing cyclic structures of glucose (α vs β anomers).
  • Mixing up DNA/RNA components (Thymine vs Uracil, Deoxyribose vs Ribose).
  • Remember definitions, classifications, and key examples for each biomolecule.

🎙️ Be precise with your terminology and structures. Practice drawing the cyclic forms of glucose and identifying the anomeric carbon. Pay close attention to the differences between DNA and RNA to avoid common errors in exams.

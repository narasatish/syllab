Carbohydrates - The Energy Source

  • Polyhydroxy aldehydes or ketones
  • General formula: CnH2nOn or CnH2O)n
  • Primary source of energy for living organisms

🎙️ Carbohydrates are the most abundant biomolecules on Earth, commonly known as sugars and starches. Chemically, they are polyhydroxy aldehydes or ketones. Their general formula helps us identify them, and remember, they are our body's preferred fuel source.

Classification of Carbohydrates

  • Monosaccharides: Simple sugars (glucose, fructose)
  • Disaccharides: Two monosaccharides (sucrose, maltose)
  • Polysaccharides: Many monosaccharides (starch, cellulose)

🎙️ Carbohydrates are classified based on their hydrolysis products. Monosaccharides are the simplest and cannot be hydrolyzed further. Disaccharides yield two monosaccharides upon hydrolysis, and polysaccharides yield many. This distinction is crucial for understanding their properties.

Monosaccharides - Glucose & Fructose

  • Glucose: Aldohexose, 'blood sugar'
  • Fructose: Ketohexose, 'fruit sugar'
  • Both are reducing sugars

🎙️ Glucose and fructose are two common monosaccharides. Glucose is an aldohexose, meaning it contains an aldehyde group and six carbon atoms. Fructose is a ketohexose, with a ketone group. Both are vital energy sources and are reducing sugars, which is an important chemical property.

Structure of Glucose

  • Open-chain structure: Straight chain of 6 carbons
  • Cyclic structure: Hemiacetal formation
  • Anomers: α- and β-glucose forms

🎙️ Glucose exists in both open-chain and cyclic forms. The cyclic structure is more stable and is formed by the reaction between the aldehyde group and a hydroxyl group. This cyclization leads to the formation of new stereoisomers called anomers, specifically α and β forms, differing at C1.

Disaccharides - Sucrose & Maltose

  • Sucrose: Glucose + Fructose (non-reducing)
  • Maltose: Glucose + Glucose (reducing)
  • Lactose: Glucose + Galactose (reducing)

🎙️ Disaccharides are formed by glycosidic linkage between two monosaccharide units. Sucrose, common table sugar, is unique because it's non-reducing due to the involvement of both anomeric carbons in the bond. Maltose and lactose, however, are reducing sugars as they have a free anomeric carbon.

Polysaccharides - Starch & Cellulose

  • Starch: Plant energy storage (amylose + amylopectin)
  • Cellulose: Plant structural component (β-glucose units)
  • Glycogen: Animal energy storage

🎙️ Polysaccharides are polymers of many monosaccharide units. Starch is the primary energy reserve in plants, composed of amylose and amylopectin. Cellulose provides structural rigidity to plants and is made of β-glucose units, which humans cannot digest. Glycogen is the animal equivalent of starch.

  • Essential for structure, function, and regulation
  • Contain C, H, O, N (and sometimes S)

🎙️ Proteins are incredibly versatile biomolecules, often called the workhorses of the cell. They are complex polymers built from smaller units called alpha-amino acids. Proteins are involved in almost every biological process, from structural support to catalyzing reactions.

Amino Acids - Building Blocks of Proteins

  • Contain an amino group (-NH2) and a carboxyl group (-COOH)
  • Zwitterionic form in aqueous solution
  • Essential vs. Non-essential amino acids

🎙️ Amino acids are the monomers of proteins. Each amino acid has a central carbon atom bonded to an amino group, a carboxyl group, a hydrogen atom, and a unique side chain (R group). In solution, they exist as zwitterions. Remember the distinction between essential amino acids, which our body cannot synthesize, and non-essential ones.

Peptide Bond Formation

  • Condensation reaction between -COOH and -NH2
  • Forms an amide linkage (-CO-NH-)
  • Release of a water molecule

🎙️ Amino acids link together to form proteins via a peptide bond. This is a condensation reaction where the carboxyl group of one amino acid reacts with the amino group of another, releasing a molecule of water. This forms a strong amide linkage, building the polypeptide chain.

Levels of Protein Structure

  • Primary: Sequence of amino acids
  • Secondary: Alpha-helix, Beta-pleated sheet
  • Tertiary: 3D folding of polypeptide chain
  • Quaternary: Arrangement of multiple polypeptide units

🎙️ Proteins have complex structures described at four levels. The primary structure is simply the linear sequence of amino acids. Secondary structure involves local folding like alpha-helices and beta-pleated sheets. Tertiary structure is the overall 3D shape, and quaternary structure involves the arrangement of multiple polypeptide chains.

Denaturation of Proteins

  • Loss of 3D structure (secondary, tertiary, quaternary)
  • Caused by heat, pH changes, chemicals
  • Leads to loss of biological activity

🎙️ Denaturation is a critical concept. It refers to the disruption of a protein's higher-order structures—secondary, tertiary, and quaternary—without breaking the primary peptide bonds. This is often irreversible and leads to the loss of the protein's biological function, like when an egg white cooks.

Functions of Proteins

  • Enzymes: Catalyze biochemical reactions
  • Hormones: Regulate body processes

🎙️ Proteins perform an astonishing array of functions. Enzymes are protein catalysts that speed up reactions. Hormones like insulin are proteins. Structural proteins like collagen provide strength, and transport proteins like hemoglobin carry oxygen. Their roles are truly diverse and essential.

Nucleic Acids - The Genetic Code

  • Polymers of nucleotides
  • Carry genetic information
  • DNA (Deoxyribonucleic Acid) and RNA (Ribonucleic Acid)

🎙️ Nucleic acids are the carriers of genetic information and are crucial for heredity and protein synthesis. They are polymers made up of repeating units called nucleotides. The two main types are DNA and RNA, each playing distinct roles in the cell.

Components of Nucleotides

  • Pentose Sugar: Deoxyribose (DNA) or Ribose (RNA)
  • Nitrogenous Base: Purines (A, G) & Pyrimidines (C, T/U)
  • Phosphate Group

🎙️ Each nucleotide is composed of three parts: a pentose sugar, a nitrogenous base, and a phosphate group. The sugar differs between DNA (deoxyribose) and RNA (ribose). The nitrogenous bases are either purines (Adenine, Guanine) or pyrimidines (Cytosine, Thymine in DNA, Uracil in RNA).

Double Helix Structure of DNA

  • Two polynucleotide strands coiled around each other
  • Sugar-phosphate backbone on the outside
  • Nitrogenous bases pair in the interior (A-T, G-C)
  • Stabilized by hydrogen bonds

🎙️ The iconic double helix structure of DNA, proposed by Watson and Crick, is fundamental. It consists of two antiparallel strands, with the sugar-phosphate backbone forming the outer helix. The bases pair specifically in the interior – Adenine with Thymine, and Guanine with Cytosine – held together by hydrogen bonds.

Functions of Nucleic Acids

  • DNA: Storage and transmission of genetic information
  • RNA: Involved in protein synthesis (mRNA, tRNA, rRNA)
  • Gene expression regulation

🎙️ The primary function of DNA is to store and transmit genetic information from one generation to the next. RNA, on the other hand, plays multiple crucial roles in expressing this genetic information, including carrying messages, transporting amino acids, and forming ribosomes for protein synthesis.

Vitamins - Essential Micronutrients

  • Organic compounds required in small amounts
  • Cannot be synthesized by the body
  • Fat-soluble (A, D, E, K) and Water-soluble (B, C)

🎙️ Vitamins are organic compounds that are essential for normal growth and metabolism, though required in very small quantities. Our bodies generally cannot synthesize them, so they must be obtained from the diet. They are broadly classified as fat-soluble or water-soluble, which affects their storage and absorption.

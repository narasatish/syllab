What are Semiconductors?

  • Materials with electrical conductivity between conductors and insulators.
  • Their conductivity can be controlled by external factors like temperature or impurities.
  • Essential for building transistors, diodes, and integrated circuits.

🎙️ Semiconductors are truly special materials. Unlike conductors that always conduct or insulators that never do, semiconductors offer a controllable conductivity. This unique property makes them invaluable for creating the logic and memory of electronic devices.

Conductors, Insulators, Semiconductors: A Comparison

  • **Conductors:** Free electrons, low resistance, easy current flow (e.g., Copper).
  • **Insulators:** Tightly bound electrons, high resistance, no current flow (e.g., Glass).
  • **Semiconductors:** Conductivity tunable, intermediate resistance (e.g., Silicon, Germanium).

🎙️ The key difference lies in their electron availability for conduction. Conductors have many free electrons, insulators have almost none, and semiconductors fall in between, allowing us to manipulate their electron availability.

Energy Bands in Solids

  • **Valence Band:** Contains valence electrons, usually full or partially full.
  • **Conduction Band:** Contains free electrons, responsible for current flow.
  • **Forbidden Energy Gap (Eg):** Energy required for an electron to jump from valence to conduction band.

🎙️ Understanding energy bands is fundamental. The forbidden gap, or band gap, dictates a material's electrical properties. A large gap means an insulator, a small gap or overlapping bands mean a conductor, and an intermediate gap means a semiconductor.

Intrinsic Semiconductors

  • Pure form of semiconductor material (e.g., pure Silicon or Germanium).
  • No impurities added; all atoms are of the same type.
  • Number of free electrons (ne) equals number of holes (nh).

🎙️ Intrinsic semiconductors are the purest form. In these materials, conduction occurs due to thermally generated electron-hole pairs. At absolute zero, they behave as perfect insulators, but at room temperature, some bonds break, creating carriers.

Intrinsic Semiconductor at 0K and Room Temperature

  • **At 0K:** All valence electrons are tightly bound, no free carriers; behaves as an insulator.
  • **At Room Temperature:** Thermal energy breaks some covalent bonds, creating electron-hole pairs.
  • Conductivity is low but increases with temperature.

🎙️ This temperature dependency is a key characteristic. As temperature rises, more electron-hole pairs are generated, increasing conductivity. However, for practical applications, their conductivity is too low and uncontrollable.

Extrinsic Semiconductors: Doping

  • Intrinsic semiconductors made conductive by adding controlled impurities.
  • Process called **doping**, impurities are **dopants**.
  • Creates two types: N-type (excess electrons) and P-type (excess holes).

🎙️ Doping is the magic behind controlling semiconductor properties. By introducing specific impurities, we can create an abundance of either free electrons or holes, making the material much more conductive and useful.

N-type Semiconductor

  • Doped with **pentavalent** impurities (e.g., Phosphorus, Arsenic).
  • These dopants are called **donor impurities** as they donate an extra electron.
  • **Electrons** are majority carriers, **holes** are minority carriers.

🎙️ In N-type semiconductors, the pentavalent impurity atoms form four covalent bonds with silicon atoms, leaving one extra electron free for conduction. This dramatically increases the electron concentration, making them the primary charge carriers.

P-type Semiconductor

  • Doped with **trivalent** impurities (e.g., Boron, Aluminium).
  • These dopants are called **acceptor impurities** as they accept an electron, creating a hole.
  • **Holes** are majority carriers, **electrons** are minority carriers.

🎙️ For P-type, the trivalent impurity forms three covalent bonds, leaving one bond incomplete and creating a 'hole'. These holes can then accept electrons, acting as positive charge carriers. Here, holes dominate the conduction process.

N-type vs. P-type: Key Differences

  • **N-type:** Majority carriers are electrons, dopants are donors.
  • **P-type:** Majority carriers are holes, dopants are acceptors.
  • Fermi level shifts towards conduction band in N-type, towards valence band in P-type.

🎙️ Remember, both N-type and P-type semiconductors are electrically neutral overall, as the dopant atoms are ionized but still have a balanced number of protons and electrons. The difference is in the *mobile* charge carriers.

Worked Example 1: Identifying Semiconductor Type

  • **Question:** A pure silicon crystal is doped with Indium. What type of semiconductor is formed?
  • **Solution:** Indium is a Group 13 element, meaning it is trivalent.
  • When a trivalent impurity is added to silicon (Group 14), it creates a deficiency of one electron, forming a hole.
  • Therefore, a **P-type semiconductor** is formed.

🎙️ This is a common exam question. Always identify the group of the dopant element. Trivalent (Group 13) leads to P-type, and Pentavalent (Group 15) leads to N-type. Make sure to clearly state your reasoning.

p-n Junction Formation

  • When a P-type semiconductor is joined with an N-type semiconductor.
  • **Diffusion:** Majority carriers (holes from P, electrons from N) cross the junction.
  • **Drift:** Electric field established in depletion region opposes further diffusion.

🎙️ The p-n junction is the heart of most semiconductor devices. The initial diffusion creates a region devoid of mobile charge carriers, known as the depletion region, which then sets up an electric field opposing further carrier movement.

Depletion Region and Potential Barrier

  • Region near the junction where mobile charge carriers are absent.
  • Contains immobile positive donor ions on N-side and negative acceptor ions on P-side.
  • This charge separation creates an electric field and a **potential barrier** (Vb).

🎙️ The depletion region acts like an insulator. The potential barrier needs to be overcome for current to flow across the junction. Its width and height depend on doping concentration and temperature.

Forward Biasing of p-n Junction

  • Positive terminal of battery connected to P-side, negative to N-side.
  • Applied voltage opposes the potential barrier, reducing its width.
  • Current flows easily once barrier is overcome (above threshold voltage).

🎙️ In forward bias, the external voltage pushes majority carriers towards the junction, reducing the depletion region width and lowering the potential barrier. This allows a significant current to flow with a small increase in voltage after the knee voltage.

➡️
Current Flow Enabled

Reverse Biasing of p-n Junction

  • Negative terminal of battery connected to P-side, positive to N-side.
  • Applied voltage supports the potential barrier, increasing its width.
  • Only a very small **reverse saturation current** flows due to minority carriers.

🎙️ Reverse bias pulls majority carriers away from the junction, widening the depletion region and increasing the potential barrier. This effectively blocks current flow, except for a tiny current due to thermally generated minority carriers. A common mistake is connecting the battery incorrectly.

⬅️

V-I Characteristics of a p-n Junction Diode

  • **Forward Bias:** Current increases exponentially after a threshold voltage (0.7V for Si, 0.3V for Ge).
  • **Reverse Bias:** Very small, almost constant reverse saturation current.
  • **Breakdown Voltage:** At high reverse voltage, current increases sharply due to avalanche/Zener breakdown.

🎙️ The V-I characteristic curve is essential for understanding diode behavior. Note the non-linear relationship and the distinct behavior in forward and reverse bias. The breakdown region is usually avoided in normal diode operation, but utilized in Zener diodes.

p-n Junction Diode as a Half-Wave Rectifier

  • Converts alternating current (AC) into pulsating direct current (DC).
  • Diode conducts only during positive half-cycles of the AC input.
  • Only half of the AC waveform reaches the load.

🎙️ Rectification is the process of converting AC to DC. A single p-n junction diode can achieve half-wave rectification, where only one half of the input AC signal is passed through. This is a simple but less efficient method.

p-n Junction Diode as a Full-Wave Rectifier

  • Converts both positive and negative half-cycles of AC into pulsating DC.
  • Uses two diodes (center-tap) or four diodes (bridge rectifier).
  • More efficient conversion, smoother output than half-wave.

🎙️ Full-wave rectification makes better use of the AC input, converting both halves into useful DC pulses. This results in a higher average DC voltage and is more commonly used in power supplies. The bridge rectifier is particularly popular due to not needing a center-tapped transformer.

Special Purpose Diodes: Zener Diode

  • Heavily doped p-n junction designed to operate in reverse breakdown region.
  • Maintains a constant voltage across its terminals despite varying current.
  • Used primarily as a **voltage regulator**.

🎙️ Unlike normal diodes, Zener diodes are specifically designed to safely operate in the reverse breakdown region. This unique characteristic makes them perfect for stabilizing voltage in power supplies, protecting sensitive components from voltage fluctuations.

Special Purpose Diodes: LED and Photodiode

  • **LED (Light Emitting Diode):** Converts electrical energy into light energy (forward biased).
  • **Photodiode:** Converts light energy into electrical energy (reverse biased).
  • Both are optoelectronic devices, demonstrating light-matter interaction.

🎙️ LEDs are ubiquitous, from indicator lights to display screens. Photodiodes are used in light detectors, solar cells, and optical communication. Remember, LEDs are forward biased to emit light, while photodiodes are reverse biased to detect it.

Worked Example 2: Diode Circuit Analysis

  • **Question:** A silicon diode (Vf = 0.7V) is in series with a 100 Ω resistor and a 5V battery. Calculate the current.
  • **Solution:** The diode is forward biased. Voltage across resistor = Battery Voltage - Diode Drop.
  • VR = 5V - 0.7V = 4.3V.
  • Current I = VR / R = 4.3V / 100Ω = 0.043 A or 43 mA.

🎙️ For silicon diodes, always account for the 0.7V forward voltage drop. For germanium, it's approximately 0.3V. Neglecting this drop is a common mistake that leads to incorrect current calculations in diode circuits.

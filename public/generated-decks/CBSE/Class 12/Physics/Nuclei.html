Atomic Mass Unit (amu)

  • Standard unit for expressing atomic and nuclear masses.
  • Defined as 1/12th the mass of a carbon-12 atom.
  • 1 amu ≈ 1.6605 x 10⁻²⁷ kg.
  • Often denoted by 'u'.

🎙️ Since nuclear masses are incredibly small, using kilograms directly is cumbersome. The atomic mass unit, or amu, provides a convenient scale. Remember its definition and conversion to kg.

Composition of the Nucleus

  • The nucleus is composed of protons and neutrons.
  • Protons carry a positive charge (+e), neutrons are neutral.
  • Both protons and neutrons are collectively called nucleons.
  • Strong nuclear force binds nucleons together.

🎙️ Imagine the nucleus as a tiny, dense ball of protons and neutrons. These particles, called nucleons, are held together by the strongest force in nature: the strong nuclear force, overcoming electrostatic repulsion between protons.

Atomic Number (Z) and Mass Number (A)

  • Atomic Number (Z): Number of protons in the nucleus.
  • Determines the element's identity and chemical properties.
  • Mass Number (A): Total number of protons and neutrons (nucleons).
  • Number of neutrons = A - Z.

🎙️ Z is like the element's ID card – it tells you what element it is. A tells you the total count of particles in the nucleus. These two numbers are fundamental to describing any nucleus.

Isotopes, Isobars, and Isotones

  • Isotopes: Same Z, different A (same element, different neutrons). E.g., ¹H, ²H, ³H.
  • Isobars: Same A, different Z (different elements, same mass number). E.g., ⁴⁰S, ⁴⁰Cl, ⁴⁰Ar.
  • Isotones: Same number of neutrons (A-Z), different Z and A. E.g., ¹⁴C and ¹⁶O (both have 8 neutrons).

🎙️ These terms are crucial for classifying different types of nuclei. Understand the defining characteristic for each: Isotopes have the same Z, Isobars have the same A, and Isotones have the same number of neutrons.

Isotopes have same atomic number but different mass numbers (different neutrons). Isobars

Size of the Nucleus

  • Nuclei are extremely small, typically 10⁻¹⁵ m (femtometers or fermis).
  • Experimentally determined using scattering experiments (e.g., Rutherford's alpha scattering).
  • Nuclear radius (R) is approximately proportional to A¹ᐟ³.
  • R = R₀ A¹ᐟ³, where R₀ ≈ 1.2 x 10⁻¹⁵ m (empirical constant).

🎙️ The nucleus is incredibly tiny, much smaller than the atom itself. Remember the empirical formula for nuclear radius and the constant R₀. This relationship shows that as mass number increases, the nucleus gets larger, but not linearly.

Nuclear Density

  • Calculated by dividing nuclear mass by nuclear volume.
  • Nuclear mass ≈ A × mₚ (where mₚ is proton mass).
  • Nuclear volume ≈ (4/3)πR³ = (4/3)π(R₀A¹ᐟ³ )³ = (4/3)πR₀³A.
  • Nuclear density is approximately constant for all nuclei (≈ 2.3 x 10¹⁷ kg/m³).

🎙️ One of the most astonishing facts about nuclei is their incredibly high and nearly constant density. This implies that nuclear matter is incompressible and packed extremely tightly, regardless of the element.

Mass-Energy Equivalence: E=mc²

  • Einstein's famous equation relating mass and energy.
  • Mass can be converted into energy and vice-versa.
  • A small amount of mass corresponds to a huge amount of energy due to c² (speed of light squared).
  • Fundamental to understanding nuclear reactions and binding energy.

🎙️ This equation is the cornerstone of nuclear physics. It tells us that mass is just another form of energy. When we talk about nuclear reactions, we are essentially talking about the conversion of a tiny bit of mass into a tremendous amount of energy.

💥

Mass Defect (Δm)

  • The difference between the sum of masses of individual nucleons and the actual mass of the nucleus.
  • Δm = [ Zmₚ + (A-Z)mₙ ] - M(A,Z) where M(A,Z) is actual nuclear mass.
  • This 'missing' mass is converted into energy to bind the nucleons together.
  • Always a positive value for stable nuclei.

🎙️ If you weigh the individual protons and neutrons separately and then weigh the nucleus they form, you'll find the nucleus is slightly lighter. This difference, the mass defect, is critical for understanding nuclear stability.

📉
Mass Loss
10 / 27

Binding Energy (Eᵇ)

  • The energy required to break a nucleus into its constituent protons and neutrons.
  • It is the energy equivalent of the mass defect: Eᵇ = Δm c².
  • Also, the energy released when nucleons combine to form a nucleus.
  • Higher binding energy indicates a more stable nucleus.

🎙️ The binding energy is essentially the 'glue' that holds the nucleus together. It's directly calculated from the mass defect using E=mc². A larger binding energy means the nucleus is more tightly bound and therefore more stable.

💪
Nuclear Strength
11 / 27

Binding Energy Per Nucleon

  • Binding Energy Per Nucleon (Eᵇ/A) = Eᵇ / A.
  • A measure of the stability of a nucleus.
  • Higher Eᵇ/A means greater stability.
  • The curve of Eᵇ/A vs. A peaks around A=56 (Iron), indicating maximum stability.

🎙️ While total binding energy increases with A, the binding energy *per nucleon* gives us a better idea of individual nucleon stability. The peak at iron explains why iron is so stable and why both fission and fusion release energy.

1Light nuclei (low Eᵇ/A)
2Peak around Fe (max Eᵇ/A)
3Heavy nuclei (decreasing Eᵇ/A)

Worked Example 1: Mass Defect & Binding Energy

  • Calculate the mass defect and binding energy for a Helium-4 nucleus (⁴₂He).
  • Given: m(⁴₂He) = 4.002603 u, mₚ = 1.007825 u, mₙ = 1.008665 u.
  • 1 u = 931.5 MeV/c².

🎙️ Let's work through a typical problem. First, identify the number of protons and neutrons. Then, calculate the total mass of the individual nucleons and compare it to the actual nuclear mass to find the mass defect.

13 / 27

Solution to Example 1

  • For ⁴₂He: Z=2, N=2. Sum of individual masses = 2(1.007825 u) + 2(1.008665 u) = 4.032980 u.
  • Mass Defect (Δm) = 4.032980 u - 4.002603 u = 0.030377 u.
  • Binding Energy (Eᵇ) = Δm × 931.5 MeV/u = 0.030377 u × 931.5 MeV/u = 28.29 MeV.
  • Binding Energy per Nucleon = 28.29 MeV / 4 = 7.07 MeV/nucleon.

🎙️ Make sure to use the correct masses for protons and neutrons. The conversion factor from amu to MeV is crucial here. A common mistake is forgetting to convert mass defect into energy using the correct factor.

14 / 27

Common Mistake: Units in E=mc²

  • Using mass in kg and c in m/s gives energy in Joules (J).
  • For nuclear physics, mass is often in amu (u) and energy in Mega-electron Volts (MeV).
  • Remember the conversion: 1 u = 931.5 MeV/c².
  • Do not mix units! Be consistent or convert appropriately.

🎙️ This is a very frequent source of errors in exams. If your mass defect is in 'u', directly multiply by 931.5 MeV/u to get energy in MeV. If you convert 'u' to 'kg', then use E=mc² with c in m/s to get energy in Joules.

Radioactivity: Spontaneous Nuclear Decay

  • The spontaneous emission of radiation (alpha, beta, gamma) from unstable nuclei.
  • Discovered by Henri Becquerel in 1896.
  • Occurs when nuclei are too large or have an unfavorable neutron-to-proton ratio.
  • Results in the transformation of one element into another (transmutation).

🎙️ Radioactivity is nature's way for unstable nuclei to achieve stability. It's a spontaneous process, meaning we cannot predict when a particular nucleus will decay, but we can predict the rate of decay for a large sample.

Laws of Radioactive Decay

  • The rate of decay is proportional to the number of undecayed nuclei present.
  • dN/dt = -λN, where N is number of nuclei, λ is decay constant.
  • Integrated form: N(t) = N₀e^(-λt).
  • N₀ is initial number of nuclei, N(t) is number remaining at time t.

🎙️ This exponential decay law is fundamental. It means that the more radioactive nuclei you have, the faster they decay. The decay constant (lambda) is characteristic of a particular radioactive isotope.

Radioactive decay follows exponential decay law; alpha/beta/gamma radiation emitted from u

Half-Life (T½)

  • The time required for half of the radioactive nuclei in a sample to decay.
  • T½ = ln(2) / λ = 0.693 / λ.
  • After 'n' half-lives, the remaining fraction is (1/2)ⁿ.
  • A constant value for a given radionuclide.

🎙️ Half-life is a very intuitive concept. It's the time it takes for the activity of a sample to drop by half. It's independent of the initial amount and external conditions, making it a reliable characteristic.

🕰️
Time for Half to Decay
18 / 27

Mean Life (τ)

  • The average lifetime of a radioactive nucleus.
  • τ = 1 / λ.
  • Related to half-life by τ = T½ / ln(2) = T½ / 0.693 ≈ 1.44 T½.
  • Represents the average duration for which an atom exists before decaying.

🎙️ While half-life is about half the sample, mean life is the average lifespan of an individual nucleus. Both are inversely proportional to the decay constant, highlighting their relationship to the decay rate.

Worked Example 2: Half-Life Calculation

  • A radioactive substance has a half-life of 10 days.
  • What fraction of the original sample will remain after 30 days?
  • How many half-lives have passed?

🎙️ Let's apply the half-life concept to a problem. This type of question frequently appears in exams and tests your understanding of exponential decay.

20 / 27

Solution to Example 2

  • Number of half-lives (n) = Total time / Half-life = 30 days / 10 days = 3.
  • Fraction remaining = (1/2)ⁿ = (1/2)³ = 1/8.
  • So, 1/8th of the original sample will remain after 30 days.
  • Alternatively, N = N₀e^(-λt). First find λ = 0.693/10 = 0.0693 day⁻¹.

🎙️ This problem is straightforward if you understand the concept of 'n' half-lives. You can also solve it using the exponential decay formula, but the half-life method is often quicker for these types of questions.

Alpha (α) Decay

  • Emission of an alpha particle (⁴₂He nucleus) from a heavy unstable nucleus.
  • Parent nucleus (A,Z) transforms into daughter nucleus (A-4, Z-2).
  • Mass number A decreases by 4, Atomic number Z decreases by 2.
  • Example: ²³⁸₉₂U → ²³⁴₉₀Th + ⁴₂He.

🎙️ Alpha decay reduces both the mass number and atomic number of the parent nucleus. It's a common decay mode for very heavy nuclei to reduce their size and achieve greater stability.

1Heavy Parent Nucleus (A, Z)
2Emits Alpha Particle (⁴₂He)
3Daughter Nucleus (A-4, Z-2)
22 / 27

Beta (β) Decay (β⁻ and β⁺)

  • Beta-minus (β⁻) decay: Neutron converts to proton, electron (e⁻) and antineutrino (ν̄) emitted.
  • Parent (A,Z) → Daughter (A, Z+1). A is same, Z increases by 1. Example: ¹⁴₆C → ¹⁴₇N + e⁻ + ν̄.
  • Beta-plus (β⁺) decay: Proton converts to neutron, positron (e⁺) and neutrino (ν) emitted.
  • Parent (A,Z) → Daughter (A, Z-1). A is same, Z decreases by 1. Example: ²²₁₁Na → ²²₁₀Ne + e⁺ + ν.

🎙️ Beta decay involves the transformation of a nucleon within the nucleus. The mass number remains constant, but the atomic number changes, leading to a different element. Remember the emission of neutrinos/antineutrinos for conservation laws.

1Neutron (n) transforms
2Proton (p) + Electron (e⁻) + Antineutrino (ν̄)
23 / 27

Gamma (γ) Decay

  • Emission of high-energy photons (gamma rays) from an excited nucleus.
  • Occurs when a nucleus, after alpha or beta decay, is left in an excited state.
  • No change in mass number (A) or atomic number (Z).
  • The nucleus simply transitions to a lower energy state.
  • Example: ⁶⁰₂₇Co* → ⁶⁰₂₇Co + γ (where * denotes excited state).

🎙️ Gamma decay is different from alpha and beta decay as it doesn't change the identity of the nucleus. It's purely an energy transition, like an electron dropping to a lower orbital, but for the nucleus.

Applications of Radioactivity

  • Medical: Cancer therapy (radiotherapy), diagnostic imaging (PET scans), sterilization of equipment.
  • Industrial: Thickness gauges, smoke detectors, carbon dating, tracing leaks in pipes.
  • Power Generation: Nuclear reactors for electricity.
  • Research: Tracers in biological and chemical studies.

🎙️ Radioactivity, while often associated with danger, has numerous beneficial applications across various fields, from saving lives in medicine to generating electricity and aiding scientific research.

Electrostatic Potential (V)

  • Work done per unit positive test charge.
  • Bringing a charge from infinity to a point.
  • Scalar quantity, SI unit: Volt (V).
  • V = W/q₀

🎙️ Electrostatic potential at a point is defined as the work done by an external force in bringing a unit positive test charge from infinity to that point without acceleration. Remember, it's a scalar quantity, so direction doesn't matter, only magnitude.

Potential Difference (ΔV)

  • Work done moving charge between two points.
  • ΔV = V_B - V_A = W_AB / q₀
  • Independent of the path taken.
  • Conservative nature of electrostatic forces.

🎙️ Potential difference is the work done per unit positive test charge in moving it from one point to another in an electric field. Since the electrostatic force is conservative, this work is independent of the path taken, only depending on the initial and final positions.

Electrostatic Potential due to a Point Charge

  • V = kQ/r
  • k = 1/(4πε₀) is Coulomb's constant.
  • Potential decreases with distance.
  • Positive charge -> positive potential; Negative charge -> negative potential.

🎙️ The electrostatic potential due to a single point charge Q at a distance r from it is given by V = kQ/r. Notice the inverse relationship with distance 'r', unlike the E-field which goes as 1/r². Pay attention to the sign of the charge Q.

Potential due to an Electric Dipole

  • Combination of two equal and opposite charges.
  • V = kp cosθ / r²
  • Axial line: θ=0°, V = kp/r²
  • Equatorial line: θ=90°, V = 0

🎙️ An electric dipole consists of two equal and opposite charges separated by a small distance. The potential due to a dipole varies as 1/r², which is faster than a single point charge. Importantly, the potential is zero on the equatorial line.

Potential due to a System of Charges

  • Superposition Principle applies.
  • Total potential is the algebraic sum.
  • V_total = V₁ + V₂ + V₃ + ...
  • V_total = Σ (kQ_i / r_i)

🎙️ When multiple charges are present, the total electrostatic potential at any point is simply the algebraic sum of the potentials due to individual charges at that point. This is the beauty of the superposition principle for scalar quantities.

Equipotential Surfaces

  • Surface with constant potential.
  • No work done moving charge on it.
  • Electric field lines are perpendicular.
  • Never intersect each other.

🎙️ Equipotential surfaces are imaginary surfaces where the electrostatic potential is the same at every point. A key property is that no work is done in moving a charge along such a surface, as the potential difference is zero. Electric field lines always cross them perpendicularly.

Relationship between E and V

  • Electric field is negative gradient of potential.
  • E = -dV/dr (for 1D)
  • E = -∇V (in 3D, gradient operator)
  • Points in direction of decreasing potential.

🎙️ The electric field is directly related to the change in potential. It points in the direction where the potential decreases most rapidly. This negative gradient relationship is crucial for understanding how electric fields arise from potential variations.

Common Mistake: Sign Convention

  • Potential is scalar, but sign matters!
  • V for positive charge is positive.
  • V for negative charge is negative.
  • Don't confuse with electric field (vector).

🎙️ A very common error is mixing up the sign convention for potential. For potential, positive charges create positive potential, and negative charges create negative potential. Unlike electric field, we directly add these signed scalar values.

Electrostatic Potential Energy (U)

  • Work done to assemble charges.
  • Stored energy in a system of charges.
  • U = kQ₁Q₂/r (for two charges)
  • Scalar quantity, SI unit: Joule (J).

🎙️ Electrostatic potential energy is the work required to bring a system of charges together from infinity to their current configuration. It represents the energy stored within the electric field of the system. For two charges, it's simply kQ₁Q₂/r.

Potential Energy for a System of Charges

  • Pairwise sum of potential energies.
  • U = Σ (kQ_i Q_j / r_ij) for all unique pairs.
  • Example: For 3 charges, U = U₁₂+U₁₃+U₂₃
  • No self-energy term.

🎙️ For a system with more than two charges, the total potential energy is the sum of the potential energies for every unique pair of charges. Be careful not to double count pairs or include self-energy terms.

Conductors in Electrostatic Field

  • E-field inside is zero.
  • Net charge resides on surface.
  • Potential is constant throughout.
  • E-field is perpendicular to surface.

🎙️ Conductors behave uniquely in electrostatic fields. Inside a conductor, the electric field is always zero, and the entire volume is at the same electrostatic potential. Any excess charge resides on its outer surface.

Dielectrics and Polarization

  • Insulating materials.
  • No free electrons.
  • Polarization: alignment of molecular dipoles.
  • Reduces net electric field inside.

🎙️ Dielectrics are insulators that don't conduct electricity but can be 'polarized' by an external electric field. This alignment of their molecular dipoles reduces the effective electric field within the material, a property crucial for capacitors.

Capacitance (C)

  • Ability to store electric charge.
  • C = Q/V
  • Ratio of charge stored to potential difference.
  • SI unit: Farad (F).

🎙️ Capacitance is a measure of a conductor's ability to store electric charge. It's defined as the ratio of the charge stored on the conductor to the potential difference across it. A larger capacitance means more charge can be stored for a given voltage.

Parallel Plate Capacitor

  • Two conducting plates separated by dielectric.
  • C = ε₀A/d (for vacuum/air)
  • A is plate area, d is separation.
  • ε₀ is permittivity of free space.

🎙️ The parallel plate capacitor is the most common type. Its capacitance depends directly on the area of the plates and inversely on the distance between them. This formula is fundamental for designing and analyzing capacitors.

Factors Affecting Capacitance

  • Area of plates (A): C ∝ A
  • Distance between plates (d): C ∝ 1/d
  • Dielectric medium (ε_r): C ∝ ε_r
  • C = κ ε₀A/d where κ is dielectric constant.

🎙️ Understanding these factors allows us to design capacitors for specific applications. Increasing the plate area or introducing a dielectric material increases capacitance, while increasing the separation between plates decreases it.

Series Combination of Capacitors

  • Capacitors connected end-to-end.
  • Same charge (Q) on each capacitor.
  • Voltage divides: V = V₁+V₂+...
  • 1/C_eq = 1/C₁ + 1/C₂ + ...

🎙️ When capacitors are connected in series, the charge on each capacitor is the same. The total potential difference is the sum of individual potential differences. The equivalent capacitance is always less than the smallest individual capacitance.

Parallel Combination of Capacitors

  • Capacitors connected across same two points.
  • Same potential difference (V) across each.
  • Charge divides: Q = Q₁+Q₂+...
  • C_eq = C₁ + C₂ + ...

🎙️ In a parallel combination, the potential difference across each capacitor is the same. The total charge stored is the sum of charges on individual capacitors. The equivalent capacitance is greater than the largest individual capacitance.

Energy Stored in a Capacitor

  • Work done to charge the capacitor.
  • U = ½QV = ½CV² = Q²/2C
  • Stored in the electric field between plates.
  • Energy density: u = ½ε₀E²

🎙️ When a capacitor is charged, work is done against the electrostatic forces, and this energy is stored in the electric field between its plates. Remember the three equivalent formulas for stored energy; choose the one most convenient for your given values.

Quick Check: Capacitors & Energy

  • If a dielectric is inserted into a charged, isolated capacitor, what happens to V?
  • Voltage decreases (C increases, Q constant).
  • What is the energy stored if a capacitor is charged to 2V vs 1V?
  • 4 times more (U ∝ V²).

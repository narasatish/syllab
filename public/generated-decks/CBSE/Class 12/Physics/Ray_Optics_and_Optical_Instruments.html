Light: Rays and Beams

  • Ray: Path along which light energy travels.
  • Beam: Collection of light rays.
  • Assumes light travels in straight lines in a uniform medium.
  • Foundation for geometrical optics.

🎙️ In ray optics, we simplify light as straight lines called rays. A bundle of these rays forms a beam. This approximation allows us to use geometry to predict how light behaves when it interacts with surfaces like mirrors and lenses, forming the basis of what we call geometrical optics.

Laws of Reflection

  • Incident ray, reflected ray, and normal lie in the same plane.
  • Angle of incidence (i) equals angle of reflection (r).
  • Valid for all types of reflecting surfaces (plane, spherical).
  • Forms images in mirrors.

🎙️ The laws of reflection are fundamental. The first law ensures that reflection occurs in a 2D plane. The second law, angle i equals angle r, is crucial for ray diagrams and calculating image positions. Remember, these laws apply universally, whether it's a smooth mirror or a rough surface.

Spherical Mirrors: Concave vs. Convex

  • Concave: Reflecting surface curves inward (converging mirror).
  • Convex: Reflecting surface curves outward (diverging mirror).
  • Key terms: Pole (P), Centre of Curvature (C), Focus (F).
  • Focal length (f) = R/2 for small apertures.

🎙️ Spherical mirrors are sections of a hollow sphere. Concave mirrors converge parallel rays to a focus, while convex mirrors diverge them, making them appear to come from a virtual focus. Understanding their terminology—Pole, Focus, and Centre of Curvature—is vital for drawing ray diagrams and applying the mirror formula.

Image Formation by Concave Mirror (Steps)

  • Step 1: Ray parallel to principal axis passes through F after reflection.
  • Step 2: Ray passing through F becomes parallel to principal axis.
  • Step 3: Ray passing through C reflects back along the same path.
  • Step 4: Ray incident at P reflects symmetrically.

🎙️ To locate an image formed by a concave mirror, we typically use any two of these four principal rays. Their intersection point (or their apparent intersection) gives the image location. Mastering these ray diagrams helps visualize the nature, position, and size of the image without complex calculations initially.

Mirror Formula and Magnification

  • Mirror Formula: 1/f = 1/v + 1/u.
  • f = focal length, v = image distance, u = object distance.
  • Linear Magnification (m): m = h'/h = -v/u.
  • Sign conventions are CRUCIAL for correct application.

🎙️ The mirror formula is a powerful tool to calculate image positions. Always remember to use the Cartesian sign convention strictly: Pole as origin, incident light direction as positive x-axis. Magnification tells us if the image is enlarged or diminished, and if it's inverted (negative m) or erect (positive m).

Worked Example: Concave Mirror

  • Q: An object is placed 15 cm from a concave mirror of focal length 10 cm. Find image position and magnification.
  • Given: u = -15 cm, f = -10 cm (concave mirror).
  • Using 1/f = 1/v + 1/u => 1/-10 = 1/v + 1/-15.
  • Solving: 1/v = 1/15 - 1/10 = (2-3)/30 = -1/30 => v = -30 cm.
  • Magnification m = -v/u = -(-30)/(-15) = -2.
  • Image is real, inverted, magnified (2x), at 30 cm in front of mirror.

🎙️ This example demonstrates the application of the mirror formula and magnification. Notice the careful use of sign conventions: object distance 'u' is negative as it's left of the pole, and focal length 'f' is negative for a concave mirror. A negative 'v' means the image is real and formed on the same side as the object, and negative 'm' confirms it's inverted.

Laws of Refraction: Snell's Law

  • Bending of light as it passes from one medium to another.
  • Incident ray, refracted ray, and normal lie in the same plane.
  • Snell's Law: n1 sin i = n2 sin r.
  • n1, n2 are refractive indices; i, r are angles of incidence/refraction.

🎙️ Refraction is the phenomenon where light changes direction when it crosses the boundary between two different media. Like reflection, the rays and normal are coplanar. Snell's Law is the quantitative description of this bending, relating the angles to the refractive indices of the media. This law is central to understanding lenses and prisms.

Refractive Index and Optical Density

  • Refractive Index (n): Ratio of speed of light in vacuum (c) to speed in medium (v). (n = c/v)
  • Relative Refractive Index: n21 = n2/n1.
  • Optically denser medium: Higher refractive index, light slows down.
  • Optically rarer medium: Lower refractive index, light speeds up.

🎙️ The refractive index quantifies how much a medium slows down light. A higher refractive index means a denser medium and more bending towards the normal when light enters it from a rarer medium. Understanding relative refractive index is key when light passes between two non-vacuum media.

Total Internal Reflection (TIR)

  • Occurs when light travels from a denser to a rarer medium.
  • Angle of incidence (i) must be greater than the critical angle (ic).
  • Critical angle: sin ic = n_rarer / n_denser.
  • No refraction occurs; all light is reflected back into the denser medium.

🎙️ TIR is a fascinating phenomenon where light is completely reflected back within a denser medium. It requires two conditions: light must travel from denser to rarer, and the angle of incidence must exceed the critical angle. This principle is responsible for the sparkle of diamonds and the working of optical fibers.

Applications of Total Internal Reflection

  • Optical Fibers: Transmit light signals over long distances.
  • Prisms: Used in binoculars, periscopes to deviate light by 90° or 180°.
  • Endoscopes: Medical instruments for internal body examination.
  • Sparkle of Diamonds: Due to high refractive index and small critical angle.

🎙️ TIR has numerous practical applications. Optical fibers are a prime example, revolutionizing communication by efficiently guiding light signals. Prisms utilizing TIR are superior to mirrors in some optical instruments because they offer perfect reflection without silvering. These applications highlight the practical importance of understanding TIR.

Refraction at Spherical Surfaces

  • General formula: n2/v - n1/u = (n2 - n1)/R.
  • n1: refractive index of medium where object is.
  • n2: refractive index of medium where image forms.
  • R: radius of curvature of the spherical surface.

🎙️ This formula describes refraction at a single spherical surface, which is the building block for understanding lenses. Again, strict adherence to sign conventions for u, v, and R is essential. Positive R for convex towards the incident light, negative for concave towards incident light.

Lens Maker's Formula

  • Relates focal length (f) to refractive index (n) and radii of curvature (R1, R2).
  • 1/f = (n - 1) [1/R1 - 1/R2].
  • Assumes lens is thin and surrounded by air (n_air=1).
  • Crucial for lens design and manufacturing.

🎙️ The Lens Maker's Formula is fundamental for anyone designing lenses. It shows how the focal length of a lens depends on the material it's made from (refractive index) and the curvature of its two surfaces. Remember to apply the correct sign conventions for R1 and R2 based on the incident light direction.

Thin Lens Formula and Magnification

  • Lens Formula: 1/f = 1/v - 1/u.
  • f = focal length, v = image distance, u = object distance.
  • Linear Magnification (m): m = h'/h = v/u.
  • Sign conventions apply similarly to mirrors.

🎙️ The thin lens formula is used to calculate image positions for lenses, similar to the mirror formula. The key difference is the minus sign between 1/v and 1/u. Magnification for lenses also uses v/u, but without the negative sign present in the mirror formula. Pay close attention to sign conventions for f, u, v.

Power of a Lens and Combination of Lenses

  • Power (P): Reciprocal of focal length in meters (P = 1/f).
  • Unit: Dioptre (D). Converging lens: +ve Power, Diverging lens: -ve Power.
  • Combination of thin lenses: P_eq = P1 + P2 + P3 + ...
  • Equivalent focal length: 1/f_eq = 1/f1 + 1/f2 + 1/f3 + ...

🎙️ The power of a lens indicates its ability to converge or diverge light. A higher power means stronger bending. When multiple thin lenses are placed in contact, their powers simply add up, making it easy to find the equivalent focal length of the system. This principle is widely used in designing complex optical systems.

Worked Example: Converging Lens

  • Q: An object is placed 20 cm from a converging lens of focal length 15 cm. Find image position and magnification.
  • Given: u = -20 cm, f = +15 cm (converging lens).
  • Using 1/f = 1/v - 1/u => 1/15 = 1/v - 1/(-20).
  • Solving: 1/v = 1/15 - 1/20 = (4-3)/60 = 1/60 => v = +60 cm.
  • Magnification m = v/u = 60/(-20) = -3.
  • Image is real, inverted, magnified (3x), at 60 cm on the other side of lens.

🎙️ Here, we apply the thin lens formula. For a converging lens, focal length 'f' is positive. The object distance 'u' is negative as it's to the left of the optical center. A positive 'v' indicates a real image formed on the opposite side of the lens, and a negative 'm' confirms it's inverted.

Dispersion by a Prism

  • Splitting of white light into its constituent colors.
  • Different colors (wavelengths) have different speeds in the medium.
  • Violet light deviates the most, Red light deviates the least.
  • Angle of deviation (δ) depends on refractive index and prism angle (A).

🎙️ Dispersion is the beautiful phenomenon of light splitting into its rainbow components when passing through a prism. This occurs because the refractive index of the prism material is slightly different for different colors, causing each color to bend by a different amount. Violet light, having a shorter wavelength, deviates more than red light.

The Human Eye and Its Defects

  • Natural optical instrument, forms real, inverted images on retina.
  • Accommodation: Ciliary muscles change lens focal length to focus.
  • Myopia (Nearsightedness): Corrected by concave lens.
  • Hypermetropia (Farsightedness): Corrected by convex lens.

Simple Microscope (Magnifying Glass)

  • A single converging lens of short focal length.
  • Forms a virtual, erect, magnified image.
  • Object placed between optical center and focal point (F).
  • Angular magnification (M) = 1 + D/f (image at near point) or D/f (image at infinity).

🎙️ A simple microscope, or magnifying glass, is the most basic optical instrument for viewing magnified images. By placing an object within its focal length, it produces an enlarged, virtual image. The magnification depends on the focal length of the lens and the least distance of distinct vision (D).

Compound Microscope

  • Uses two converging lenses: Objective lens and Eyepiece.
  • Objective forms a real, inverted, magnified image (intermediate image).
  • Eyepiece acts as a simple microscope to further magnify the intermediate image.
  • Higher magnification than a simple microscope.

🎙️ The compound microscope achieves much higher magnification than a simple one by using two stages of magnification. The objective lens creates an enlarged real image, which then serves as the object for the eyepiece, which further magnifies it virtually. This design is crucial for observing very small objects.

Telescopes: Refracting and Reflecting

  • Used to view distant objects (planets, stars).
  • Refracting Telescope: Uses lenses (objective and eyepiece).
  • Reflecting Telescope: Uses mirrors (primary concave mirror) and eyepiece.
  • Reflecting telescopes avoid chromatic aberration and spherical aberration better.

🎙️ Telescopes allow us to explore the cosmos. Refracting telescopes use lenses, but large lenses can suffer from chromatic aberration and be heavy. Reflecting telescopes, using mirrors, overcome these issues and are preferred for very large apertures, offering superior image quality for astronomical observations.

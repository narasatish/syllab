  • Think of a ball bouncing off a wall
  • This phenomenon is essential for mirrors

🎙️ When light hits a smooth surface, it doesn't pass through; it bounces back. This phenomenon is called reflection, and it's what allows us to see our own image in a mirror.

The Rules of Reflection

  • Incident ray, reflected ray, and normal lie in the same plane
  • Angle of incidence (i) equals angle of reflection (r)
  • These laws apply to all reflecting surfaces

🎙️ These two laws are fundamental. The first law ensures that reflection happens in a predictable 2D plane, and the second law, i=r, dictates the exact direction of the reflected ray.

Curved Mirrors: Concave & Convex

  • Spherical mirrors are part of a hollow sphere
  • Concave mirror: reflecting surface curved inward
  • Convex mirror: reflecting surface curved outward

🎙️ Unlike flat plane mirrors, spherical mirrors can converge or diverge light, creating a wider range of image types. Think of the back of a spoon (convex) and the inside of a spoon (concave).

Key Terms for Mirrors

  • Center of Curvature (C): Center of the sphere it's part of
  • Principal Axis: Line passing through P and C
  • Focus (F): Point where parallel rays converge or appear to diverge from

Drawing Image Paths (Ray Tracing Rules)

  • Ray parallel to principal axis passes through F (or appears to)
  • Ray passing through F becomes parallel to principal axis
  • Ray passing through C reflects back along the same path
  • Ray incident at P reflects symmetrically about the principal axis

🎙️ These four rules are your toolkit for drawing accurate ray diagrams. You only need two of them to locate an image, but knowing all four helps verify your results.

🎙️ When light moves from air to water, or water to glass, it slows down or speeds up, causing it to change direction. This bending is called refraction, and it's why the spoon looks bent.

Snell's Law: Quantifying Bending

  • Incident ray, refracted ray, and normal lie in the same plane
  • Ratio of sine of angle of incidence to sine of angle of refraction is constant
  • Expressed as n1 sin i = n2 sin r (where n is refractive index)

🎙️ The first law is similar to reflection. The second law, Snell's Law, is the mathematical backbone of refraction, introducing the concept of the refractive index, which quantifies how much a medium bends light.

Real vs. Apparent Depth

  • Objects in a denser medium (like water) appear shallower
  • Refraction causes light rays to bend away from normal when exiting water
  • Real depth / Apparent depth = n (refractive index of denser medium)

🎙️ This explains why a swimming pool looks shallower than it is, or why a fish appears closer to the surface. It's a direct consequence of light bending as it leaves the water.

Total Internal Reflection: Trapping Light

  • Occurs when light travels from a denser to a rarer medium
  • Angle of incidence must exceed the critical angle
  • Light reflects entirely back into the denser medium, no refraction occurs

🎙️ Imagine light trying to escape from water into air. If it hits the surface at too steep an angle, it gets trapped inside! This is TIR, a powerful phenomenon.

  • Relates focal length (f) to refractive index (n) and radii of curvature (R1, R2)

Power of a Lens: Focusing Strength

  • Ability of a lens to converge or diverge light rays
  • Measured in Dioptres (D), where P = 1/f (f in meters)
  • Convex lens has positive power, concave lens has negative power

🎙️ The power of a lens tells you how strongly it bends light. A higher power means a shorter focal length and stronger bending. This is what your optometrist measures for your spectacles.

Avoid These Pitfalls!

  • Sign Conventions: Incorrectly applying signs for u, v, f, R in formulas
  • Ray Diagrams: Not drawing arrows on rays or incorrect positioning of F/C
  • Formulas: Using wrong formula or incorrect units (e.g., cm vs m for power)
  • TIR Conditions: Forgetting light must go from denser to rarer medium

🎙️ Many students lose marks due to these common errors. Pay close attention to sign conventions, practice drawing accurate ray diagrams, and double-check your units in numerical problems.

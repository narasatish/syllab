Early Models of the Atom

  • Ancient Greek philosophy: Democritus's 'atomos'.
  • John Dalton's Atomic Theory (early 19th century).
  • Ideas evolved with new experimental evidence.

🎙️ For centuries, the concept of an atom was purely philosophical. Dalton's work provided the first scientific basis, but it was still a very simple, indivisible particle model. This laid the groundwork for further discoveries.

J.J. Thomson's Plum Pudding Model (1897)

  • Atom as a sphere of uniformly distributed positive charge.
  • Electrons (negatively charged) embedded like plums.
  • Overall electrically neutral atom.

🎙️ After discovering the electron, Thomson proposed this model. Imagine a spherical cake of positive charge with negatively charged raisins scattered throughout. It was the first model to incorporate subatomic particles.

Limitations of Thomson's Model

  • Could not explain the results of Rutherford's alpha-particle scattering experiment.
  • Failed to account for the origin of spectral lines.
  • Lacked strong experimental evidence for its structure.

🎙️ Despite its initial acceptance, Thomson's model quickly faced challenges. The biggest blow came from Rutherford's experimental findings, which showed a very different distribution of mass and charge within the atom.

Rutherford's Alpha-Particle Scattering Experiment: Setup

  • Source of high-energy alpha particles (radioactive material).
  • Thin gold foil as the target (approx. 100 nm thick).
  • Circular zinc sulphide screen to detect scattered alpha particles.

🎙️ Rutherford, along with Geiger and Marsden, conducted this landmark experiment. They used alpha particles, which are positively charged and relatively heavy, to probe the atomic structure by firing them at a thin gold foil.

Key Observations: Alpha Scattering

  • Most alpha particles passed straight through undeflected.
  • A small fraction were deflected by small angles (< 90°).
  • Very few (about 1 in 8000) were deflected by large angles (> 90°), some even bounced back.

🎙️ These observations were revolutionary. The fact that most particles passed through suggested a lot of empty space, while the rare large deflections implied a very dense, positively charged core.

Rutherford's Conclusions: The Nuclear Model

  • Most of the atom is empty space.
  • The entire positive charge and most of the mass are concentrated in a tiny central region called the nucleus.
  • Electrons revolve around the nucleus in orbits.

🎙️ Rutherford's 'planetary model' proposed a very small, dense, positively charged nucleus at the center, with electrons orbiting it. This explained the large deflections by direct collisions with the nucleus.

Features of Rutherford's Atomic Model

  • Central, positively charged nucleus.
  • Electrons revolve around the nucleus in various orbits.
  • Electrostatic force provides the centripetal force for orbits.
  • Atom is electrically neutral overall.

🎙️ This model was a significant leap forward, accurately describing the atom's mass and charge distribution. It laid the foundation for all subsequent atomic models.

Drawbacks of Rutherford's Model

  • Atomic Stability: Accelerated electrons should radiate energy and spiral into the nucleus.
  • Atomic Spectra: Could not explain the discrete line spectra observed for elements.
  • Did not explain the distribution of electrons around the nucleus.

🎙️ Despite its successes, Rutherford's model had critical flaws. Classical electromagnetism predicted that orbiting electrons should continuously lose energy, making atoms unstable, which contradicts reality. It also failed to explain the unique 'fingerprint' of light emitted by atoms.

Niels Bohr's Postulates for Hydrogen Atom (1913)

  • Electrons revolve in certain stable, non-radiating orbits.
  • Only those orbits are allowed where angular momentum (L) is quantized: L = n(h/2π).
  • Energy is absorbed or emitted only when electrons jump between allowed orbits (E = hν).

🎙️ Bohr introduced revolutionary postulates, combining classical physics with quantum ideas. These postulates directly addressed the stability and spectral issues of Rutherford's model, specifically for the hydrogen atom.

Derivations from Bohr's Model

  • Radius of n-th orbit: r_n = (n²h²ε₀) / (πme²) = 0.529 n² Å.
  • Velocity of electron in n-th orbit: v_n = (e²) / (2ε₀nh) ∝ 1/n.
  • Energy of electron in n-th orbit: E_n = - (me⁴) / (8ε₀²h²n²) = -13.6/n² eV.

🎙️ By applying Bohr's postulates, we can derive exact expressions for the radius, velocity, and energy of an electron in any allowed orbit of a hydrogen-like atom. These are crucial formulas for numerical problems.

Quantized Energy Levels of Hydrogen

  • E_n = -13.6/n² eV, where n is the principal quantum number.
  • Ground state (n=1) has the lowest energy: E₁ = -13.6 eV.
  • Higher states (n=2, 3, ...) are excited states with less negative (higher) energy.
  • Ionization energy is the energy required to remove an electron (n=∞, E=0).

🎙️ The negative sign indicates that the electron is bound to the nucleus. As 'n' increases, the energy becomes less negative, meaning the electron is less tightly bound. Understanding this energy ladder is key to understanding atomic spectra.

Hydrogen Spectrum: Series and Wavelengths

  • Lyman series: Transitions to n_f=1 (Ultraviolet region).
  • Balmer series: Transitions to n_f=2 (Visible region).
  • Paschen series: Transitions to n_f=3 (Infrared region).
  • Brackett (n_f=4) and Pfund (n_f=5) series are also in the infrared.

🎙️ When an electron jumps from a higher energy level to a lower one, it emits a photon of specific energy, creating a line in the spectrum. Each series corresponds to transitions ending at a particular principal energy level, resulting in distinct regions of the electromagnetic spectrum.

Worked Example: Bohr's Model Calculation

  • Question: Calculate the radius of the second Bohr orbit of the hydrogen atom.
  • Given: r₁ = 0.529 Å (first Bohr radius).
  • Solution: r_n = n²r₁. For n=2, r₂ = (2)² * 0.529 Å = 4 * 0.529 Å = 2.116 Å.

🎙️ This example demonstrates how to use the relationship between orbital radius and the principal quantum number. Remember that the radius increases quadratically with 'n'.

Common Mistakes: Units and Signs

  • Confusing energy units: eV vs. Joules (1 eV = 1.6 x 10⁻¹⁹ J).
  • Incorrect sign for energy: Bound state energies are always negative.
  • Forgetting 'n' or using 'n' incorrectly in formulas (e.g., r_n ∝ n², E_n ∝ 1/n²).

🎙️ These are common pitfalls in exams. Always pay close attention to units and the physical meaning of negative energy. Double-check your formulas for the correct power of 'n'.

Excitation and Ionization Energies

  • Excitation energy: The minimum energy required to excite an electron from a lower to a higher energy level.
  • Ionization energy: The minimum energy required to remove an electron completely from the atom (i.e., move it to n=∞).
  • For hydrogen, ionization energy from ground state is 13.6 eV.

🎙️ These are important conceptual definitions. Excitation energy corresponds to specific energy differences between allowed levels, while ionization energy is the energy to free the electron from the atom's influence entirely.

De Broglie's Hypothesis and Bohr's Quantization

  • De Broglie proposed that electrons exhibit wave-particle duality (λ = h/mv).
  • He explained Bohr's second postulate by suggesting that stable orbits are those where the electron wave forms a standing wave.
  • Condition for standing wave: Circumference (2πr) = nλ, which leads to L = n(h/2π).

🎙️ De Broglie provided a beautiful physical justification for Bohr's seemingly arbitrary quantization condition. This concept of electron waves forming standing patterns in orbits is a cornerstone of quantum mechanics.

Introduction to X-rays

  • X-rays are electromagnetic waves of very short wavelength (0.1 Å to 100 Å).
  • Produced when fast-moving electrons strike a metal target (e.g., in a Coolidge tube).
  • Consist of continuous X-rays (bremsstrahlung) and characteristic X-rays.

🎙️ While not directly about atomic structure, X-rays are closely related as their production and characteristic spectra involve electron transitions within atoms. They have numerous applications, especially in medicine and industry.

Moseley's Law: Atomic Number and X-rays

  • Moseley studied characteristic X-ray spectra of various elements.
  • He found that the square root of the frequency (√ν) of characteristic X-rays is proportional to the atomic number (Z): √ν = a(Z-b).
  • This established atomic number (Z) as a more fundamental property of an element than atomic mass.

🎙️ Moseley's work was groundbreaking. His law provided a direct way to determine the atomic number of an element and correctly ordered elements in the periodic table, correcting previous discrepancies based on atomic mass.

LASER: Light Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation

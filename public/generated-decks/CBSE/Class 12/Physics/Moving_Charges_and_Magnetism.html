Lorentz Force: The Magnetic Component

  • A charge 'q' moving with velocity 'v' in magnetic field 'B' experiences a force.
  • The force is given by F = q(v × B).
  • This is the magnetic Lorentz force, a vector cross product.

🎙️ The magnetic Lorentz force equation, F = q(v × B), is crucial. Remember, the cross product means the force is perpendicular to both the velocity vector and the magnetic field vector. This has significant implications for the particle's motion.

Direction of Lorentz Force: Right-Hand Rule

  • For positive charge: Point fingers in 'v', curl towards 'B', thumb gives 'F'.
  • For negative charge: Use right-hand rule, then reverse the force direction.
  • Force is always perpendicular to both v and B.

🎙️ Mastering the right-hand rule is essential for determining the direction of the magnetic force. Practice this rule with different scenarios to avoid common mistakes in exams. Remember, for negative charges, the force direction is opposite to that predicted by the rule.

Worked Example 1: Force on a Proton

  • A proton (q = +1.6x10⁻¹⁹ C) moves at 2x10⁶ m/s along x-axis.
  • Magnetic field B = 0.5 T along y-axis.
  • Calculate the magnitude and direction of the force.

🎙️ Let's apply the formula. v is along i-hat, B is along j-hat. v x B will be along k-hat. So, F = (1.6x10⁻¹⁹ C)(2x10⁶ m/s)(0.5 T) along z-axis. The magnitude is 1.6x10⁻¹³ N, directed along the positive z-axis.

Force on a Current-Carrying Conductor

  • If a conductor of length 'L' carrying current 'I' is placed in a magnetic field 'B'.
  • The force experienced is F = I(L × B).
  • Here 'L' is a vector in the direction of current flow.

🎙️ This formula F = I(L × B) is directly derived from the Lorentz force on individual charges. The magnitude is BILsinθ, where θ is the angle between the current direction and the magnetic field. This force is crucial for the operation of electric motors.

Worked Example 2: Force on a Wire Segment

  • A wire 0.2 m long carries 5 A current along x-axis.
  • Magnetic field B = (0.4 T)k̂.
  • Find the magnetic force on the wire.

🎙️ Here, L = 0.2 i-hat and B = 0.4 k-hat. The cross product i-hat x k-hat is -j-hat. So, F = 5 A * 0.2 m * 0.4 T * (-j-hat). The force is -0.4 N j-hat, meaning 0.4 N in the negative y-direction.

Motion in a Magnetic Field: Circular Path

  • If v is perpendicular to B, the force F is always perpendicular to v.
  • This force acts as a centripetal force, causing circular motion.
  • Radius r = mv / (qB), and angular frequency ω = qB / m.

🎙️ When a charged particle enters a uniform magnetic field perpendicular to its velocity, it executes uniform circular motion. The magnetic force does no work because it's always perpendicular to displacement, so kinetic energy remains constant.

Motion in a Magnetic Field: Helical Path & Cyclotron

  • If v has a component parallel to B, the particle moves in a helix.
  • The parallel component remains unchanged, perpendicular component causes circular motion.
  • This principle is used in devices like the Cyclotron for accelerating charged particles.

🎙️ The helical path is a combination of linear motion along the field and circular motion perpendicular to it. The Cyclotron leverages this to accelerate charged particles to very high energies by repeatedly passing them through an electric field while a magnetic field keeps them in a spiral path.

Biot-Savart Law: Magnetic Field from a Current Element

  • Analogous to Coulomb's law for electric fields, but for magnetic fields.
  • It describes the magnetic field dB produced by a small current element Idl.
  • dB = (μ₀/4π) * (Idl × r̂) / r².

🎙️ The Biot-Savart Law is fundamental for calculating magnetic fields due to current distributions. Remember μ₀ is the permeability of free space. The direction of dB is given by the right-hand thumb rule or the cross product.

Biot-Savart Law Applications: Straight Wire & Circular Loop

  • For a long straight wire: B = (μ₀I) / (2πr) at distance 'r'.
  • For a circular loop at its center: B = (μ₀NI) / (2R) for 'N' turns and radius 'R'.
  • These are key formulas derived from the Biot-Savart Law.

Ampere's Circuital Law: The Loop Integral

  • Analogous to Gauss's Law in electrostatics, but for magnetic fields.
  • States that the line integral of B around any closed loop is μ₀ times the net current enclosed by the loop.
  • ∮ B ⋅ dl = μ₀I_enclosed.

🎙️ Ampere's Law simplifies magnetic field calculations for highly symmetric current distributions. The choice of the Amperian loop is critical – it should be a path where B is constant or perpendicular to dl. This law is fundamental for understanding magnetism.

Ampere's Law Applications: Solenoid

  • A solenoid is a long coil of wire with many turns.
  • Inside a long solenoid, the magnetic field is uniform and strong.
  • B = μ₀nI, where 'n' is turns per unit length.

🎙️ The magnetic field outside a long solenoid is nearly zero, making it a very useful device for creating controlled magnetic fields. The formula B = μ₀nI is a direct application of Ampere's Law and is crucial for understanding electromagnetism.

Ampere's Law Applications: Toroid

  • A toroid is a hollow circular ring with a large number of turns wound on it.
  • The magnetic field is confined entirely within the toroid's core.
  • B = (μ₀NI) / (2πr), where 'N' is total turns and 'r' is average radius.

🎙️ Similar to a solenoid, a toroid also confines the magnetic field, but in a closed loop. The field inside the toroid varies slightly with the radius, unlike the uniform field in a long solenoid. The field outside and inside the empty space of the toroid is zero.

Force between Two Parallel Current-Carrying Conductors

  • Two parallel wires carrying currents exert forces on each other.
  • Parallel currents attract, anti-parallel currents repel.
  • Force per unit length F/L = (μ₀I₁I₂) / (2πd).

🎙️ This force arises because each wire creates a magnetic field, and the other wire experiences a force in that field. This interaction is used to define the SI unit of current, the Ampere. Two parallel wires 1m apart, carrying 1A current, exert a force of 2x10⁻⁷ N/m on each other.

Torque on a Current Loop in a Magnetic Field

  • A current loop in a uniform magnetic field experiences a torque.
  • This torque tends to align the loop's magnetic dipole moment with the field.
  • τ = NIABsinθ = MBsinθ, where M = NIA is the magnetic dipole moment.

🎙️ This principle is fundamental to the operation of electric motors and galvanometers. The torque depends on the number of turns, current, area of the loop, magnetic field strength, and the angle between the magnetic moment and the field.

Moving Coil Galvanometer: Principle & Conversion

  • It detects and measures small electric currents.
  • Principle: Torque on a current-carrying coil in a magnetic field.
  • Can be converted to an Ammeter (low resistance shunt in parallel) or Voltmeter (high resistance in series).

🎙️ The moving coil galvanometer is a sensitive instrument. Its deflection is directly proportional to the current passing through it. Understanding its conversion into an ammeter or voltmeter by adding suitable resistances is a common exam question.

Worked Example 7: Galvanometer Conversion

  • A galvanometer has resistance 50 Ω and full-scale deflection current 10 mA.
  • How to convert it into an ammeter of range 1 A?
  • Calculate the shunt resistance required.

🎙️ Here, Rg=50 Ω, Ig=0.01 A, I=1 A. The formula for shunt resistance S = IgRg / (I - Ig). S = (0.01 * 50) / (1 - 0.01) = 0.5 / 0.99 ≈ 0.505 Ω. This low resistance is connected in parallel to the galvanometer.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Confusing right-hand rules for force and field direction.
  • Incorrectly applying vector cross products (v x B vs B x v).
  • Forgetting units and unit conversions (cm to m, mA to A).
  • Not identifying symmetry for Ampere's Law applications.

🎙️ Many students lose marks due to these common errors. Always double-check your directions using the right-hand rule, ensure units are consistent, and clearly identify the current enclosed when using Ampere's Law. Practice is key to avoiding these pitfalls.

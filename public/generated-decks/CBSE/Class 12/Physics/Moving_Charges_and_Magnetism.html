Magnetic Force on a Moving Charge

  • Charges at rest experience no magnetic force.
  • Moving charges create and experience magnetic fields.
  • The Lorentz force describes this interaction.
  • It's a vector cross product, determining direction.

🎙️ When a charge is stationary, it only interacts with electric fields. But the moment it starts moving, a new force comes into play – the magnetic force. This force is fundamental to understanding how motors, generators, and even particle accelerators work.

The Lorentz Force Law (F = q(v x B))

  • Force (F) is directly proportional to charge (q), velocity (v), and magnetic field (B).
  • Direction given by the right-hand rule (v to B).
  • Force is always perpendicular to both v and B.
  • Unit of Magnetic Field (B) is Tesla (T) or Weber/meter².

🎙️ The Lorentz force equation, F = q(v x B), is central to this chapter. Remember, the cross product means the force is always perpendicular to both the velocity vector and the magnetic field vector. This perpendicularity has profound implications for the motion of charges.

Right-Hand Rule for Force Direction

  • Point fingers in direction of velocity (v).
  • Curl fingers towards magnetic field (B).
  • Thumb points in direction of magnetic force (F).
  • For negative charges, force is opposite to thumb direction.

🎙️ Mastering the Right-Hand Rule is critical for solving problems. Practice it with various scenarios. Remember the crucial detail for negative charges: the force acts in the opposite direction to what the thumb indicates for a positive charge.

Special Cases: Force on Moving Charge

  • If v || B (angle 0° or 180°), F = 0 (sin 0° = 0).
  • If v ⊥ B (angle 90°), F = qvB (sin 90° = 1).
  • Maximum force occurs when velocity is perpendicular to the field.
  • No work is done by magnetic force as F ⊥ v.

🎙️ These special cases simplify calculations and reveal key insights. When a charge moves parallel or anti-parallel to the magnetic field, it experiences no magnetic force. The magnetic force does no work because it's always perpendicular to the displacement, thus it only changes direction, not speed.

Circular Motion in Uniform Magnetic Field

  • If v ⊥ B, charge moves in a circular path.
  • Magnetic force provides the necessary centripetal force (qvB = mv²/r).
  • Radius of path: r = mv / qB.
  • Period of revolution: T = 2πm / qB (independent of v and r).

🎙️ This is a classic scenario. When the velocity is perpendicular to a uniform magnetic field, the charge enters a perfect circular orbit. Notice that the period of revolution is independent of the particle's speed and the radius of its path – a key principle in devices like cyclotrons.

Helical Motion and Velocity Selector

  • If v has components parallel and perpendicular to B, motion is helical.
  • Perpendicular component causes circular path, parallel component causes linear motion.
  • Velocity Selector: Crossed E and B fields select particles with specific speed (v = E/B).
  • Only particles where F_e = F_m pass undeflected.

🎙️ Helical motion is a combination of circular and linear motion. The velocity selector is an ingenious device that uses both electric and magnetic fields to filter particles by their velocity, a concept used in mass spectrometers.

The Cyclotron: Accelerating Particles

  • Device to accelerate charged particles to high energies.
  • Uses perpendicular electric field (for acceleration) and magnetic field (for circular path).
  • Frequency of applied AC voltage matches cyclotron frequency (f = qB / 2πm).
  • Particles spiral outwards, gaining energy with each pass.

🎙️ The cyclotron is a brilliant application of the principles we've discussed. It accelerates charged particles to very high speeds using a combination of oscillating electric fields and a uniform magnetic field. Its resonant frequency is key to its operation.

Force on a Current-Carrying Conductor

  • Current is flow of charges, so a current-carrying wire in B field experiences force.
  • Force F = I (L x B), where L is length vector along current.
  • Direction given by Right-Hand Rule (I to B).
  • Unit of L is meter, I is Ampere.

🎙️ Since current is essentially moving charges, a wire carrying current in a magnetic field will experience a force. This is the principle behind electric motors. The direction of the force is again given by the right-hand rule, aligning your fingers with the current direction.

Worked Example: Force on a Wire

  • A 0.5 m wire carrying 2A current is placed in a 0.1T B-field at 30°.
  • F = I L B sinθ = (2A)(0.5m)(0.1T)sin(30°).
  • F = 0.1 * 0.5 = 0.05 N.
  • Common mistake: Forgetting sinθ or using wrong angle.

🎙️ Let's apply the formula. Remember to correctly identify the angle between the current direction and the magnetic field. A common mistake is using the angle with the normal, instead of the angle between the vectors themselves. Always double-check your angles.

Force Between Two Parallel Current Wires

  • Each wire produces a magnetic field, and the other wire experiences a force in that field.
  • Attractive force if currents are in the same direction.
  • Repulsive force if currents are in opposite directions.
  • Force per unit length: F/L = μ₀I₁I₂ / 2πr.

🎙️ This interaction is fascinating: currents running in the same direction attract each other, while currents in opposite directions repel. This formula for force per unit length is crucial and forms the basis for defining the standard unit of current, the Ampere.

Defining the Ampere

  • One Ampere is the constant current which, if maintained in two straight parallel conductors of infinite length, of negligible circular cross-section, and placed one meter apart in vacuum, would produce between these conductors a force equal to 2 x 10⁻⁷ Newton per meter of length.
  • This definition is based purely on magnetic force.
  • It's a fundamental SI unit.

🎙️ The definition of the Ampere is not arbitrary; it's precisely defined using the magnetic force between two parallel current-carrying wires. Understanding this definition helps appreciate the fundamental nature of electromagnetism and its connection to our SI unit system.

Torque on a Current Loop in B-Field

  • A current loop placed in a uniform magnetic field experiences a torque.
  • Torque τ = NIAB sinθ, where N=turns, I=current, A=area, B=field, θ=angle between normal to loop and B.
  • The loop tends to align its normal with the magnetic field.
  • This is the principle behind electric motors and galvanometers.

🎙️ A current loop acts like a tiny magnet. When placed in an external magnetic field, it experiences a torque that tries to align it with the field. This torque is the driving force in many electromechanical devices, from simple motors to sensitive measuring instruments.

Magnetic Dipole Moment (M)

  • For a current loop, the magnetic dipole moment M = NIA.
  • It's a vector quantity, direction perpendicular to the loop (Right-Hand Thumb Rule for current).
  • Torque can be written as τ = M x B.
  • SI unit of magnetic dipole moment is Ampere-meter² (Am²).

🎙️ Just as an electric dipole has an electric dipole moment, a current loop has a magnetic dipole moment. This concept simplifies the understanding of how loops and coils interact with magnetic fields. The direction of M is crucial and follows the right-hand thumb rule.

Moving Coil Galvanometer: Principle

  • Detects and measures small electric currents.
  • Principle: Current-carrying coil in a radial magnetic field experiences torque.
  • Torque causes deflection proportional to current.
  • Radial field ensures sinθ = 1, so τ = NIAB.

🎙️ The Moving Coil Galvanometer is a very sensitive instrument. Its design ensures that the magnetic field is always radial, meaning the angle between the normal to the coil and the field is always 90 degrees, maximizing the torque and making it linearly proportional to the current.

Sensitivity of Moving Coil Galvanometer

  • Current Sensitivity (I_s): Deflection per unit current (φ/I = NAB/k).
  • Voltage Sensitivity (V_s): Deflection per unit voltage (φ/V = NAB/kR).
  • Increasing N, A, B increases sensitivity. Decreasing k (torsional constant) increases sensitivity.
  • Increasing current sensitivity doesn't always increase voltage sensitivity.

🎙️ Sensitivity is a key performance parameter for a galvanometer. Understand the factors that affect both current and voltage sensitivity. It's important to note that increasing current sensitivity by increasing resistance (R) would actually decrease voltage sensitivity, so there's a trade-off.

Biot-Savart Law: Magnetic Field

  • Analogous to Coulomb's law for electric fields.
  • Gives magnetic field (dB) due to a small current element (Idl).
  • dB = (μ₀/4π) (Idl sinθ / r²).
  • Direction of dB is perpendicular to both Idl and r (Right-Hand Rule).

🎙️ The Biot-Savart Law is the fundamental law for calculating the magnetic field produced by a current distribution. It's a vector law, and the direction of the magnetic field dB is crucial, determined by the cross product of Idl and r, which again points out of or into the plane.

B-Field on Axis of Circular Current Loop

  • Field at center: B = μ₀I / 2R.
  • Field on axis (distance x from center): B = μ₀IR² / 2(R² + x²)^(3/2).
  • Direction is along the axis, given by Right-Hand Thumb Rule.
  • Field lines form closed loops.

🎙️ Calculating the magnetic field due to a circular loop is a common application of Biot-Savart Law. Remember the special case for the center of the loop. The field lines always form closed loops, a characteristic difference from electric field lines.

Ampere's Circuital Law

  • ∮ B ⋅ dl = μ₀I_enc.
  • The line integral of B around any closed loop equals μ₀ times the net current enclosed by the loop.
  • Useful for symmetrical current distributions (like straight wire, solenoid, toroid).
  • Magnetic field lines are always closed loops.

🎙️ Ampere's Circuital Law is extremely powerful for calculating magnetic fields in situations with high symmetry, much like Gauss's Law for electric fields. The key is choosing an appropriate Amperian loop that makes the integral easy to evaluate.

Applications of Ampere's Law: Solenoid & Toroid

  • Solenoid: Long coil of wire, produces uniform B field inside (B = μ₀nI).
  • Field outside a long solenoid is nearly zero.
  • Toroid: Doughnut-shaped solenoid, B field confined within its core (B = μ₀NI / 2πr).
  • Field outside a toroid is zero.

🎙️ Solenoids and toroids are crucial components in many electrical devices. Ampere's Law allows us to easily calculate the magnetic fields they produce. Remember the key difference: a solenoid has an open end, while a toroid is a closed loop, leading to different external field characteristics.

Worked Example: Solenoid Field

  • A solenoid has 1000 turns/meter and carries 5A current.
  • Calculate B-field inside: B = μ₀nI.
  • B = (4π x 10⁻⁷ T m/A) * (1000 turns/m) * (5A).
  • B = 2π x 10⁻³ T ≈ 6.28 x 10⁻³ T.

🎙️ This example shows a direct application of the solenoid formula. Remember μ₀ is the permeability of free space. Pay attention to units and ensure 'n' is turns per unit length, not total turns.

Common Mistakes & Exam Tips

  • Confusing Right-Hand Rule (direction of force/field) vs. Left-Hand Rule (motor effect).
  • Incorrectly identifying angle θ in F = qvBsinθ or τ = NIABsinθ.
  • Using Biot-Savart where Ampere's Law is simpler (and vice-versa).
  • Forgetting vector nature: direction matters!

🎙️ Be vigilant about these common pitfalls. The right-hand rule is versatile, but sometimes the left-hand rule is taught for motor effect. Stick to one consistent method. Always draw diagrams to visualize vector directions and angles. Practice makes perfect!

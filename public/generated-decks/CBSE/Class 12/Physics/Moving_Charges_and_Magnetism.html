Oersted's Discovery: Current's Magnetic Effect

  • An electric current produces a magnetic field around it.
  • First experimental evidence connecting electricity and magnetism.
  • A compass needle deflects when placed near a current-carrying wire.

🎙️ In 1820, Hans Christian Oersted made a groundbreaking discovery. He observed that a compass needle deflected when placed near a wire carrying electric current. This simple experiment revealed that electric currents are sources of magnetic fields, fundamentally linking two previously separate branches of physics.

Magnetic Field (B): The Invisible Influence

  • Region around a magnet or current where its magnetic effect is felt.
  • Vector quantity, denoted by B.
  • SI unit: Tesla (T) or Weber per square meter (Wb/m²).

🎙️ Just as an electric charge creates an electric field, a moving charge or a current creates a magnetic field. This field is a vector quantity, meaning it has both magnitude and direction. The strength of the magnetic field is measured in Tesla, a very important unit to remember.

Biot-Savart Law: Calculating Magnetic Fields

  • Fundamental law to find magnetic field due to a current element.
  • Analogous to Coulomb's law for electric fields.
  • dB ∝ (I dl sinθ) / r².

🎙️ The Biot-Savart Law is a cornerstone for calculating magnetic fields produced by current distributions. It allows us to determine the magnetic field dB at a point due to a small current element 'Idl'. Remember, it's a vector law, and the direction is given by the right-hand thumb rule.

Biot-Savart Application: Straight Wire

  • Magnetic field around an infinitely long straight current-carrying wire.
  • B = (μ₀ I) / (2π r).
  • Direction by Right-Hand Thumb Rule.

🎙️ One of the first applications of Biot-Savart Law is calculating the magnetic field due to a long straight wire. The formula B = μ₀I / (2πr) is vital. The field lines are concentric circles around the wire, and their direction can be found using the Right-Hand Thumb Rule – thumb in current direction, fingers show field direction.

1Take current element Idl
2Integrate from -∞ to +∞
3B = μ₀I / 2πr

Biot-Savart Application: Circular Loop (Center)

  • Magnetic field at the center of a circular current-carrying loop.
  • B = (μ₀ I) / (2R).
  • For N turns, B = (N μ₀ I) / (2R).

🎙️ Another important application is finding the magnetic field at the center of a circular loop. Here, every current element is equidistant from the center and perpendicular to the radius vector. The total field is the sum of contributions from all elements, leading to B = μ₀I / (2R).

Biot-Savart Application: Circular Loop (Axis)

  • Magnetic field on the axis of a circular current-carrying loop.
  • Field components perpendicular to the axis cancel out.
  • Only axial components sum up.

🎙️ Calculating the field on the axis of a circular loop is a bit more involved as we need to consider vector components. Due to symmetry, the components perpendicular to the axis cancel out, and only the components along the axis add up. This results in a more complex formula you should be familiar with for derivations.

Ampere's Circuital Law: A Shortcut

  • Integral of magnetic field B around any closed loop is μ₀ times total current enclosed.
  • ∮ B ⋅ dl = μ₀ I_enclosed.
  • Useful for highly symmetric current distributions.

🎙️ Ampere's Circuital Law (ACL) is analogous to Gauss's Law in electrostatics. It provides a simpler way to calculate magnetic fields for current distributions with high symmetry, like long wires, solenoids, and toroids. The key is choosing an appropriate 'Amperian loop'.

ACL Application: Infinitely Long Straight Wire

  • Using an Amperian circular loop concentric with the wire.
  • B is constant in magnitude and tangential.
  • ∮ B ⋅ dl = B (2πr) = μ₀ I.

🎙️ Let's apply ACL to the infinitely long straight wire. By choosing a circular Amperian loop concentric with the wire, the magnetic field B is constant in magnitude and always tangential to the loop. This simplifies the integral significantly, quickly yielding the same result as Biot-Savart Law.

1Choose circular Amperian loop
2B is constant, parallel to dl
3B = μ₀I / 2πr

ACL Application: Solenoid

  • Long coil of wire with many turns.
  • Magnetic field inside is uniform and strong.
  • B = μ₀ n I, where n is turns per unit length.

🎙️ A solenoid is a practical device that produces a strong and uniform magnetic field inside it when current flows. Using an appropriate rectangular Amperian loop, we can derive the field inside: B = μ₀ n I, where 'n' is the number of turns per unit length. Outside, the field is approximately zero.

ACL Application: Toroid

  • A solenoid bent into a closed ring shape.
  • Magnetic field is completely confined within the toroid.
  • B = (μ₀ N I) / (2π r).

🎙️ A toroid is essentially a solenoid bent into a donut shape. The advantage is that the magnetic field is entirely confined within the toroid's core, unlike a solenoid where some field lines escape. The formula B = (μ₀ N I) / (2π r) is derived using an Amperian circle inside the toroid.

Lorentz Force: Force on a Moving Charge

  • Force experienced by a charge moving in a magnetic field.
  • F = q (v × B).
  • Direction given by Right-Hand Rule or vector cross product.

🎙️ When a charge 'q' moves with velocity 'v' in a magnetic field 'B', it experiences a force called the Lorentz force. Remember, this force is always perpendicular to both the velocity and the magnetic field. The direction is crucial and found using the Right-Hand Rule for cross products.

Motion of Charge in Uniform Magnetic Field

  • If v ⊥ B, charge moves in a circular path.
  • If v || B, charge moves undeflected (straight line).
  • If v makes an angle θ with B, charge moves in a helical path.

🎙️ The nature of a charged particle's motion in a uniform magnetic field depends on the angle between its velocity and the field. If perpendicular, it's a circle; if parallel, it's a straight line. For any other angle, the motion is a helix, a combination of linear and circular motion.

Worked Example: Lorentz Force

  • An electron (q = -1.6x10⁻¹⁹ C) moves at 2x10⁶ m/s in +x direction.
  • Uniform B-field of 0.5 T in +y direction.
  • Find the magnetic force on the electron.

🎙️ Let's solve this problem. Here, v = 2x10⁶ î m/s and B = 0.5 ĵ T. Using F = q(v × B), we get F = (-1.6x10⁻¹⁹)(2x10⁶ î × 0.5 ĵ) = (-1.6x10⁻¹⁹)(1 k) = -1.6x10⁻¹⁹ k N. The force is in the -z direction, as electron charge is negative.

Force on a Current-Carrying Conductor

  • A current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field experiences a force.
  • F = I (L × B).
  • Direction by Right-Hand Rule or Fleming's Left-Hand Rule.

🎙️ Since current is a flow of charges, a current-carrying wire in a magnetic field will experience a force. This force is given by F = I (L × B), where L is the length vector of the conductor in the direction of current. Remember Fleming's Left-Hand Rule for direction: Thumb-Force, Forefinger-Field, Middle finger-Current.

Force Between Two Parallel Current-Carrying Conductors

  • Parallel currents attract, anti-parallel currents repel.
  • Force per unit length, F/L = (μ₀ I₁ I₂) / (2π d).
  • Defines the Ampere: 1A is current that produces 2x10⁻⁷ N/m force.

🎙️ This is a very important concept. Two parallel wires carrying current exert forces on each other. If currents are in the same direction, they attract; if opposite, they repel. This interaction is fundamental and is used to define the SI unit of current, the Ampere.

🧲🧲
Attraction/Repulsion

Worked Example: Force Between Wires

  • Two long parallel wires, 10 cm apart, carry currents 5 A and 10 A.
  • Currents are in the same direction.
  • Calculate the force per unit length.

🎙️ Using the formula F/L = (μ₀ I₁ I₂) / (2π d), with I₁=5A, I₂=10A, d=0.1m, and μ₀=4πx10⁻⁷ Tm/A. We get F/L = (4πx10⁻⁷ * 5 * 10) / (2π * 0.1) = 5x10⁻⁵ N/m. Since currents are in the same direction, the force is attractive.

Torque on a Current Loop in a Magnetic Field

  • A current loop in a uniform B-field experiences a torque.
  • τ = M × B, where M is the magnetic dipole moment.
  • M = N I A (N=turns, I=current, A=area).

🎙️ When a current loop is placed in a magnetic field, it behaves like a magnetic dipole and experiences a torque, tending to align its magnetic dipole moment with the external field. The torque is given by τ = M × B. This principle is fundamental to electric motors and galvanometers.

Moving Coil Galvanometer: Detecting Current

  • Detects and measures small electric currents.
  • Principle: Torque on a current-carrying coil in a magnetic field.
  • Current sensitivity: θ/I. Voltage sensitivity: θ/V.

🎙️ The moving coil galvanometer is a highly sensitive instrument. Its deflection is directly proportional to the current flowing through it. Understanding its construction, especially the radial magnetic field and phosphor bronze suspension, is key for exam questions.

Conversion: Galvanometer to Ammeter

  • Ammeter measures larger currents, connected in series.
  • Requires a low resistance 'shunt' parallel to galvanometer.
  • Shunt diverts excess current, protecting galvanometer.

🎙️ An ammeter must have very low resistance to avoid affecting the circuit current. To convert a galvanometer, a small resistance called a 'shunt' is connected in parallel. This allows most of the current to bypass the galvanometer, extending its range.

Conversion: Galvanometer to Voltmeter

  • Voltmeter measures potential difference, connected in parallel.
  • Requires a high resistance in series with galvanometer.
  • High resistance limits current through galvanometer.

🎙️ A voltmeter must have very high resistance to draw negligible current from the circuit it's measuring. To convert a galvanometer, a high resistance is connected in series. This limits the current flowing through the galvanometer and allows it to measure voltage across a component.

Common Mistakes & Exam Tips

  • Direction errors: Master Right-Hand Rules and Fleming's Rules.
  • Vector vs. Scalar: Biot-Savart (vector), ACL (integral).
  • Units: Always include correct SI units (Tesla, Ampere, Newton).

🎙️ Many students lose marks due to incorrect directions or unit errors. Practice applying the various rules for direction carefully. Differentiate between vector and scalar quantities. Also, remember to clearly state all formulas and derivations, showing each step for full marks.

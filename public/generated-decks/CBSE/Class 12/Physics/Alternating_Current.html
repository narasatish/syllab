Direct Current (DC) vs. Alternating Current (AC)

  • DC: Constant voltage/current, unidirectional flow.
  • AC: Voltage/current changes polarity periodically.
  • AC can be easily stepped up/down; transmitted long distances.
  • DC is safer for low-voltage applications.

🎙️ The fundamental difference lies in the direction of current flow. DC flows in one direction, like from a battery, while AC reverses direction periodically, which is what comes from our wall sockets.

How is Alternating Current Generated?

  • Based on Faraday's Law of Electromagnetic Induction.
  • A coil rotates in a uniform magnetic field.
  • Magnetic flux linked with the coil changes.
  • Induced EMF and current are sinusoidal.

🎙️ AC generators, or alternators, exploit Faraday's law. As a coil spins in a magnetic field, the changing magnetic flux induces an alternating electromotive force, creating AC.

Key Terms for Alternating Current

  • Peak Value (V₀, I₀): Maximum value of voltage/current.
  • RMS Value (Vᵣₘₛ, Iᵣₘₛ): Effective value, equivalent to DC power.
  • Average Value (Vₐᵥ, Iₐᵥ): Mean value over half a cycle.
  • Frequency (f): Number of cycles per second (Hz).

🎙️ Understanding these terms is vital. The peak value is the highest point, while the RMS value is what's typically quoted for household voltage, as it relates to the power delivered.

Calculating Average Value of AC

  • Mean value of AC over a full cycle is zero.
  • Calculated over a half cycle (0 to π).
  • For sinusoidal AC: Iₐᵥ = (2/π) I₀ ≈ 0.637 I₀.
  • For sinusoidal AC: Vₐᵥ = (2/π) V₀ ≈ 0.637 V₀.

🎙️ Since AC is symmetric, its average over a full cycle is zero. Therefore, we calculate the average over a positive or negative half-cycle. Remember the formula for exams!

Root Mean Square (RMS) Value of AC

  • Effective value of AC.
  • Produces same heat in a resistor as DC of that value.
  • For sinusoidal AC: Iᵣₘₛ = I₀/√2 ≈ 0.707 I₀.
  • For sinusoidal AC: Vᵣₘₛ = V₀/√2 ≈ 0.707 V₀.

🎙️ The RMS value is arguably the most important. It represents the 'effective' value of AC, especially when considering power dissipation. Household voltage, like 220V, is an RMS value.

1Square the current/voltage
2Take mean (average) of squared value
3Take square root of the mean
4Result: I₀/√2 or V₀/√2

Worked Example: Peak & RMS Values

  • An AC voltage is given by V = 282 sin(314t).
  • Find: (a) Peak voltage, (b) RMS voltage, (c) Frequency.
  • (a) Peak Voltage V₀ = 282 V.
  • (b) RMS Voltage Vᵣₘₛ = V₀/√2 = 282/√2 ≈ 200 V.
  • (c) ω = 314 rad/s. 2πf = 314 => f = 314/(2π) ≈ 50 Hz.

🎙️ Let's apply what we've learned. From the standard equation V = V₀ sin(ωt), we can directly identify the peak voltage. Then, use the RMS formula. For frequency, remember ω = 2πf.

AC Circuit with a Pure Resistor

  • Voltage and current are in phase.
  • Ohm's Law (V = IR) holds true at every instant.
  • Power dissipated is P = V_rms * I_rms = I_rms² * R.
  • No phase difference means power is always positive.

🎙️ When AC flows through a pure resistor, the voltage across it and the current through it rise and fall together. There is no time delay or phase difference between them.

AC Circuit with a Pure Inductor

  • Inductor opposes change in current (Lenz's Law).
  • Voltage leads current by π/2 (90°).
  • Inductive Reactance (X_L) = ωL = 2πfL.
  • X_L is the opposition offered by inductor to AC flow.

🎙️ An inductor introduces a phase shift. Due to self-induction, the voltage across an inductor reaches its peak earlier than the current through it. Inductive reactance increases with frequency.

AC Circuit with a Pure Capacitor

  • Capacitor stores charge and opposes change in voltage.
  • Current leads voltage by π/2 (90°).
  • Capacitive Reactance (X_C) = 1/(ωC) = 1/(2πfC).
  • X_C is the opposition offered by capacitor to AC flow.

🎙️ For a capacitor, the situation is reversed from an inductor. The current through the capacitor reaches its peak before the voltage across it. Capacitive reactance decreases with frequency.

Series LCR Circuit: Impedance

  • Combination of Resistor, Inductor, and Capacitor in series.
  • Total opposition to AC is called Impedance (Z).
  • Z = √(R² + (X_L - X_C)²).
  • Phase angle (φ) = tan⁻¹((X_L - X_C)/R).

🎙️ In a series LCR circuit, the individual oppositions from R, L, and C combine vectorially to give the total impedance. This is not a simple sum because of the phase differences.

Resonance in Series LCR Circuit

  • Occurs when X_L = X_C.
  • Inductive and capacitive reactances cancel each other.
  • Impedance Z becomes minimum, Z = R.
  • Resonant frequency f₀ = 1/(2π√(LC)).

🎙️ Resonance is a critical phenomenon where the circuit becomes purely resistive. At this specific frequency, the current in the circuit reaches its maximum value, leading to many practical applications.

Characteristics of Series Resonance

  • Impedance is minimum (Z = R).
  • Current is maximum (I = V/R).
  • Power factor is unity (cos φ = 1).
  • Circuit behaves purely resistively.

🎙️ At resonance, the circuit is highly efficient, and the current is limited only by resistance. This principle is used in radio tuning circuits to select a specific frequency.

Power in AC Circuits: Real, Apparent, Reactive

  • Real Power (P): Average power dissipated (P = V_rms I_rms cosφ).
  • Apparent Power (S): Total power supplied (S = V_rms I_rms).
  • Reactive Power (Q): Power oscillating between source and reactances (Q = V_rms I_rms sinφ).
  • Power Factor (cosφ): Ratio of real power to apparent power.

🎙️ Unlike DC, power in AC circuits is more complex due to phase differences. Real power is what's actually consumed, while apparent power is what the source supplies. Reactive power is exchanged and not consumed.

Importance of Power Factor

  • High power factor (closer to 1) means more efficient power usage.
  • Low power factor means more reactive power, increasing current.
  • Increased current leads to higher transmission losses (I²R).
  • Industries aim for high power factor to reduce electricity bills.

🎙️ A high power factor is desirable for energy efficiency. A low power factor means extra current flows for the same useful power, leading to wasted energy in transmission lines and higher costs.

LC Oscillations: Energy Exchange

  • Capacitor charged and connected to an inductor.
  • Energy oscillates between electric field (capacitor) and magnetic field (inductor).
  • No resistance: oscillations continue indefinitely.
  • Analogous to mechanical spring-mass system.

🎙️ LC circuits can store and exchange energy between the capacitor's electric field and the inductor's magnetic field. This forms the basis of oscillating circuits used in radio transmitters and receivers.

Transformer: Stepping Up and Down AC

  • Device that changes AC voltage levels.
  • Works on mutual induction principle.
  • Step-up transformer: increases voltage, decreases current.
  • Step-down transformer: decreases voltage, increases current.

🎙️ Transformers are crucial for efficient long-distance power transmission. They allow us to step up voltage for transmission to minimize losses and then step it down for safe use in homes and industries.

Recap & Key Takeaways

  • AC generated by rotating coil; characterized by V₀, I₀, V_rms, I_rms.
  • R, L, C behave differently in AC circuits (phase shifts).
  • Impedance (Z) is total opposition in LCR circuits.
  • Resonance: X_L = X_C, Z=R, max current, unity power factor.
  • Power factor dictates efficiency; transformers step up/down AC.

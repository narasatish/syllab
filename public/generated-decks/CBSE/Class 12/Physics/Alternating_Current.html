AC vs. DC: The Fundamental Difference

  • DC (Direct Current): Flows in one direction only.
  • AC (Alternating Current): Periodically reverses direction.
  • Analogy: Pushing a swing vs. pushing it back and forth.

🎙️ Imagine pushing a swing. If you always push it forward, that's like DC. If you push it forward, then pull it back, then push it forward again, that's AC. This back-and-forth motion is what makes AC so versatile for power transmission.

Graphical Representation of AC

  • AC voltage and current vary sinusoidally with time.
  • Represented by sine or cosine waves.
  • Magnitude changes continuously, direction reverses periodically.

🎙️ The most common form of AC is a sinusoidal wave. This means its value smoothly increases from zero to a maximum, drops to zero, then reverses direction to a minimum, and finally returns to zero. This cycle repeats over and over.

Key Terminology in AC

  • Peak Value (V₀, I₀): Maximum voltage/current.
  • Frequency (f): Cycles per second (Hz).
  • Time Period (T): Time for one complete cycle (T=1/f).

🎙️ Understanding these terms is crucial. The peak value tells us the maximum 'strength' of the AC. Frequency tells us how fast the current changes direction – in India, it's 50 Hz, meaning it reverses 100 times per second!

Equations for AC Voltage and Current

  • Instantaneous Voltage: V = V₀ sin(ωt)
  • Instantaneous Current: I = I₀ sin(ωt + φ)
  • ω = 2πf = 2π/T is the angular frequency.

🎙️ These equations mathematically describe the sinusoidal variation of AC. V₀ and I₀ are the peak values. Omega (ω) is the angular frequency, which relates to how quickly the phase changes. Phi (φ) represents any phase difference between voltage and current.

Phase and Phase Difference

  • Phase: The state of oscillation at any instant.
  • Phase Difference (φ): The difference in phase between two AC quantities.
  • Analogy: Two friends running a race, one starts later.

🎙️ Imagine two friends running a race. If one starts a bit later, they are 'out of phase'. In AC circuits, voltage and current can be 'out of phase', meaning their peaks and zeros don't occur at the exact same time. This phase difference is crucial for circuit analysis.

AC Circuit with a Pure Resistor (R)

  • Voltage and current are always in phase (φ = 0).
  • Ohm's Law still applies: I = V/R.
  • Resistor offers resistance to both AC and DC.

🎙️ When an AC voltage is applied across a pure resistor, the current flowing through it will rise and fall exactly in step with the voltage. They reach their peaks and zeros at the same time, hence they are 'in phase'. This is the simplest AC circuit.

Worked Example 1: Purely Resistive Circuit

  • Problem: An AC voltage V = 200 sin(100πt) is applied across a 50 Ω resistor.
  • Find the peak current and instantaneous current.
  • Steps: 1. Identify V₀ and R. 2. Use I₀ = V₀/R. 3. Write I = I₀ sin(ωt).

🎙️ Let's solve this. From V = 200 sin(100πt), we have V₀ = 200 V. Given R = 50 Ω. So, I₀ = 200/50 = 4 A. Since it's a resistor, voltage and current are in phase, so I = 4 sin(100πt) A. Simple, right?

AC Circuit with a Pure Inductor (L)

  • Inductor resists change in current, causing a phase shift.
  • Voltage leads current by π/2 (90°).
  • Analogy: A heavy wheel resisting sudden changes in rotation.

🎙️ An inductor, essentially a coil of wire, has inertia for current. It 'fights' any change in current. This property causes the voltage across it to reach its peak before the current does. Hence, voltage 'leads' the current by 90 degrees.

Inductive Reactance (X_L)

  • Opposition offered by an inductor to AC current.
  • X_L = ωL = 2πfL (measured in Ohms).
  • Higher frequency or inductance means higher X_L.

🎙️ Just like resistance, inductive reactance measures the inductor's opposition to current flow, but it's frequency-dependent. The faster the current tries to change (higher frequency), the more the inductor opposes it, leading to higher X_L.

Worked Example 2: Purely Inductive Circuit

  • Problem: An inductor (L = 0.5 H) is connected to V = 100 sin(314t).
  • Find inductive reactance and peak current.
  • Steps: 1. Identify ω and L. 2. Calculate X_L = ωL. 3. Find I₀ = V₀/X_L.

🎙️ Here, ω = 314 rad/s and L = 0.5 H. So, X_L = 314 * 0.5 = 157 Ω. V₀ = 100 V. Thus, I₀ = V₀/X_L = 100/157 ≈ 0.637 A. Remember, current will lag voltage by π/2 in this circuit.

AC Circuit with a Pure Capacitor (C)

  • Capacitor stores charge, causing current to lead voltage.
  • Current leads voltage by π/2 (90°).
  • Analogy: Filling a bucket with water; flow starts before water level rises.

🎙️ A capacitor works by storing charge. To charge it, current must flow first. So, the current reaches its peak before the voltage across the capacitor reaches its peak. Hence, current 'leads' the voltage by 90 degrees.

Capacitive Reactance (X_C)

  • Opposition offered by a capacitor to AC current.
  • X_C = 1/(ωC) = 1/(2πfC) (measured in Ohms).
  • Higher frequency or capacitance means lower X_C.

🎙️ Unlike inductive reactance, capacitive reactance decreases with increasing frequency. A capacitor allows AC to pass more easily at higher frequencies because it has less time to charge fully. For DC, a capacitor acts as an open circuit (infinite reactance).

⛔
Capacitive Barrier

Worked Example 3: Purely Capacitive Circuit

  • Problem: A 10 μF capacitor is connected to V = 50 sin(200πt).
  • Find capacitive reactance and peak current.
  • Steps: 1. Identify ω and C. 2. Calculate X_C = 1/(ωC). 3. Find I₀ = V₀/X_C.

🎙️ Here, ω = 200π rad/s and C = 10 μF = 10 x 10⁻⁶ F. So, X_C = 1/(200π * 10 x 10⁻⁶) ≈ 159.15 Ω. V₀ = 50 V. Thus, I₀ = V₀/X_C = 50/159.15 ≈ 0.314 A. Current will lead voltage by π/2.

RMS Value of AC: Why We Need It

  • Average value of AC over a cycle is zero (not useful).
  • RMS (Root Mean Square) value represents the effective AC.
  • It's the DC current that produces the same heating effect.

🎙️ Since AC changes direction, its simple average over a full cycle is zero. This doesn't tell us about its power or heating capability. The RMS value is a way to quantify AC's effectiveness, relating it to an equivalent DC that would produce the same heat.

Calculating RMS Values

  • For sinusoidal AC: V_rms = V₀/√2 and I_rms = I₀/√2.
  • V_rms ≈ 0.707 V₀, I_rms ≈ 0.707 I₀.
  • Most AC voltmeters/ammeters measure RMS values.

🎙️ The factor of 1/√2 comes from the mathematical derivation of the RMS value for a sine wave. This conversion is crucial. When you hear '220 V AC' for your household supply, it refers to the RMS voltage, not the peak voltage.

Impedance (Z) of LCR Circuit

  • Total effective opposition offered by the LCR circuit to AC.
  • Z = √(R² + (X_L - X_C)²).
  • Measured in Ohms (Ω), analogous to resistance.

🎙️ Impedance is the generalized resistance for an AC circuit containing R, L, and C. It's not a simple sum because X_L and X_C oppose each other. The formula comes from the Pythagorean theorem applied to the phasor diagram of voltages.

Phase Angle (φ) in LCR Circuit

  • Phase difference between the applied voltage and total current.
  • tan φ = (X_L - X_C) / R.
  • If X_L > X_C, circuit is inductive (V leads I). If X_C > X_L, capacitive (I leads V).

🎙️ The phase angle tells us whether the overall circuit behaves more inductively or capacitively, or if it's purely resistive. This angle is crucial for calculating power in AC circuits, which we'll see next.

Worked Example 5: LCR Circuit

  • Problem: R=30Ω, L=0.4H, C=100μF in series with V=100sin(100t).
  • Find Z and φ. (ω=100 rad/s)
  • Steps: 1. Calculate X_L and X_C. 2. Calculate Z. 3. Calculate φ.

🎙️ X_L = 100 * 0.4 = 40 Ω. X_C = 1 / (100 * 100 * 10⁻⁶) = 100 Ω. Z = √(30² + (40-100)²) = √(900 + (-60)²) = √(900+3600) = √4500 ≈ 67.08 Ω. tan φ = (40-100)/30 = -60/30 = -2. So φ ≈ -63.4°. The current leads the voltage.

Power in AC Circuits

  • Instantaneous power P = VI.
  • Average power P_avg = V_rms I_rms cos φ.
  • Power factor (cos φ): measures how effectively power is utilized.

🎙️ Unlike DC, where P=VI, in AC circuits, we must account for the phase difference. Only the component of current in phase with voltage contributes to average power. The power factor (cos φ) tells us how much of the apparent power is actually consumed.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Confusing peak values with RMS values.
  • Incorrectly applying phase relations (V leads I vs. I leads V).
  • Adding reactances and resistance directly (not vectorially).
  • Forgetting units (Ohms for X_L, X_C, Z).

🎙️ These are common pitfalls students encounter. Always double-check if you're using peak or RMS values. Remember the `CIVIL` mnemonic (Capacitor: I leads V; Inductor: V leads I). And never forget to use the correct units for all quantities.

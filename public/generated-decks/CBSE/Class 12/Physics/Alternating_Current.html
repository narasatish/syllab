What is Alternating Current (AC)?

  • Current direction reverses periodically.
  • Magnitude varies sinusoidally with time.
  • Contrast with Direct Current (DC).
  • Most power grids use AC.

🎙️ Alternating Current, or AC, is what powers our homes and industries. Unlike DC, where current flows in one direction, AC continuously changes its direction and magnitude, typically in a sinusoidal pattern. This periodic reversal is key to its unique properties.

Generation of Alternating Current

  • Based on Electromagnetic Induction.
  • Rotating coil in a magnetic field.
  • Induced EMF is sinusoidal.
  • Principle of AC Generator.

🎙️ AC is primarily generated using AC generators, which work on the principle of electromagnetic induction. As a coil rotates in a uniform magnetic field, the magnetic flux linked with it changes, inducing a sinusoidal electromotive force, or EMF, and thus current.

Representing AC Voltage and Current

  • Instantaneous values: v = V₀ sin(ωt), i = I₀ sin(ωt + φ).
  • V₀, I₀ are peak values (amplitude).
  • ω is angular frequency.
  • φ is phase difference.

🎙️ AC voltage and current are represented by sinusoidal functions of time. V₀ and I₀ denote the maximum or peak values, while ω is related to the frequency. The phase angle φ tells us if the voltage and current are in sync or out of sync.

Peak Value vs. RMS Value

  • RMS (Root Mean Square) is effective value.
  • I_rms = I₀ / √2, V_rms = V₀ / √2.
  • DC equivalent for heating effect.
  • Most AC meters read RMS values.

🎙️ The RMS value is a very important concept in AC circuits. It represents the equivalent DC current or voltage that would produce the same heating effect in a resistor. This is why when you hear '220V AC', it refers to the RMS voltage, not the peak.

Understanding Phase and Phase Difference

  • Phase describes position in cycle.
  • Phase difference (φ) between V and I.
  • In-phase: φ = 0.
  • Out-of-phase: φ ≠ 0.

🎙️ Phase tells us the state of oscillation at any given time. The phase difference between voltage and current is crucial in AC circuits, especially when dealing with inductors and capacitors, as it determines how power is consumed.

AC Circuit with a Pure Resistor

  • Voltage and current are in phase (φ = 0).
  • v = V₀ sin(ωt), i = I₀ sin(ωt).
  • Ohm's Law: V₀ = I₀R or V_rms = I_rms R.
  • Resistor consumes power.

🎙️ In a purely resistive AC circuit, the voltage across the resistor and the current flowing through it are always in phase. This means they reach their peaks and zeros at the same time, behaving much like in a DC circuit regarding their immediate relationship.

Power in a Purely Resistive AC Circuit

  • Instantaneous power p = vi.
  • Average power P_avg = V_rms I_rms.
  • P_avg = I_rms² R = V_rms² / R.
  • All power dissipated as heat.

🎙️ In a purely resistive circuit, the average power dissipated is simply the product of the RMS voltage and RMS current. This power is entirely converted into heat, making resistors the primary components for heating applications in AC circuits.

AC Circuit with a Pure Inductor

  • Inductive reactance X_L = ωL.
  • Current lags voltage by π/2 (90°).
  • v = V₀ sin(ωt), i = I₀ sin(ωt - π/2).
  • Inductor stores energy in magnetic field.

🎙️ When an AC voltage is applied across a pure inductor, the current lags the voltage by 90 degrees. This opposition to current flow is called inductive reactance, X_L, and it increases with frequency and inductance. Inductors store energy in their magnetic fields.

AC Circuit with a Pure Capacitor

  • Capacitive reactance X_C = 1 / (ωC).
  • Current leads voltage by π/2 (90°).
  • v = V₀ sin(ωt), i = I₀ sin(ωt + π/2).
  • Capacitor stores energy in electric field.

🎙️ In a purely capacitive AC circuit, the current leads the voltage by 90 degrees. The opposition to current flow here is capacitive reactance, X_C, which decreases with increasing frequency and capacitance. Capacitors store energy in their electric fields.

Series LCR Circuit

  • Resistor, Inductor, Capacitor in series.
  • Voltage drops across each component.
  • Total voltage is phasor sum of V_R, V_L, V_C.
  • Impedance Z is total opposition.

🎙️ A series LCR circuit combines all three basic AC components. The total voltage across the circuit is not a simple algebraic sum but a phasor sum due to the phase differences between the voltage drops across R, L, and C. The total opposition is called impedance.

Impedance (Z) of LCR Circuit

  • Z = √(R² + (X_L - X_C)²).
  • Generalizes Ohm's Law: V = IZ.
  • Unit is Ohm (Ω).
  • Determines current for given voltage.

🎙️ Impedance, Z, is the effective total opposition to current flow in an LCR circuit. It's calculated using the resistance and the net reactance (X_L - X_C). Impedance is like resistance for AC circuits and helps determine the current for a given applied voltage.

Resonance in Series LCR Circuit

  • Condition: X_L = X_C.
  • Resonant frequency f_r = 1 / (2π√(LC)).
  • Impedance Z is minimum (Z = R).
  • Current is maximum at resonance.

🎙️ Resonance occurs when the inductive and capacitive reactances cancel each other out. At this specific resonant frequency, the impedance of the LCR circuit becomes minimum, equal to just the resistance R, leading to a maximum current for a given voltage.

Quality Factor (Q-factor) of LCR Circuit

  • Measures sharpness of resonance.
  • Q = (ω_r L) / R = 1 / (ω_r C R).
  • High Q means sharper resonance.
  • Important for tuning circuits.

🎙️ The Q-factor quantifies how sharp or selective the resonance curve is. A high Q-factor indicates a very sharp resonance, meaning the circuit is very selective to a narrow band of frequencies. This is crucial in radio receivers for tuning specific stations.

Power in a General AC Circuit (LCR)

  • Average Power P_avg = V_rms I_rms cosφ.
  • cosφ is the power factor.
  • φ is phase difference between V and I.
  • Power factor determines useful power.

🎙️ In a general AC circuit, the average power dissipated depends not just on V_rms and I_rms, but also on the power factor, cosφ. The power factor indicates how much of the total apparent power is actually consumed or dissipated. A power factor of 1 means maximum power transfer.

Worked Example: Resistive Circuit

  • Q: An AC source 220V, 50Hz is connected to 44Ω resistor. Find RMS current.
  • Sol: V_rms = 220V, R = 44Ω.
  • I_rms = V_rms / R = 220 / 44 = 5A.
  • Answer: 5A.

🎙️ Let's apply Ohm's law to a simple resistive AC circuit. Remember that when the voltage is given as '220V AC', it typically refers to the RMS value. So, we can directly use Ohm's law with RMS values to find the RMS current.

Worked Example: LCR Impedance

  • Q: R=30Ω, X_L=50Ω, X_C=10Ω. Find Z and phase angle.
  • Sol: X_L - X_C = 50 - 10 = 40Ω.
  • Z = √(30² + 40²) = √(900 + 1600) = √2500 = 50Ω.
  • tanφ = (X_L - X_C) / R = 40 / 30 = 4/3. φ = tan⁻¹(4/3) ≈ 53.1°.

🎙️ This example demonstrates how to calculate the impedance and the phase angle for a series LCR circuit. Always remember to find the net reactance (X_L - X_C) first before applying the impedance formula. The phase angle tells us if the circuit is predominantly inductive or capacitive.

Common Mistakes in AC Circuits

  • Confusing Peak and RMS values.
  • Adding voltages/currents algebraically (not phasors).
  • Incorrect phase relations for L and C.
  • Not understanding power factor.

🎙️ Students often make mistakes by treating AC quantities like DC, especially regarding peak vs. RMS values and simply adding voltages. Always use phasor addition for voltages in series LCR circuits and remember the specific phase relationships for inductors and capacitors.

Applications of Alternating Current

  • Power transmission over long distances.
  • Transformers for stepping up/down voltage.
  • AC motors and generators.
  • Radio and communication circuits (tuning).

🎙️ AC is fundamental to our modern world. Its ability to be easily stepped up or down using transformers makes long-distance power transmission efficient, minimizing energy loss. It's also vital for various electrical machines and electronic communication systems.

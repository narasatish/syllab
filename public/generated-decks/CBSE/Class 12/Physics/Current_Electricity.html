Electric Current: The Flow of Charge

  • Definition: Rate of flow of electric charge (I = Q/t)
  • Conventional Direction: Opposite to electron flow
  • SI Unit: Ampere (A), where 1 A = 1 C/s

🎙️ Electric current is simply the rate at which charge flows through a conductor. Remember, by convention, current direction is taken as the direction of positive charge flow, opposite to electron flow. One Ampere means one Coulomb of charge flows per second.

Drift Velocity: Ordered Motion

  • Average velocity of free electrons in a conductor
  • Acquired under the influence of an electric field
  • Very small magnitude (typically mm/s)

🎙️ Even though electrons move randomly at high speeds, when an electric field is applied, they acquire a small average velocity in a specific direction. This is drift velocity, and it's responsible for the current. It's typically of the order of mm/s.

Current Density: Current per Unit Area

  • Current flowing per unit cross-sectional area (J = I/A)
  • Vector quantity: Direction same as current flow
  • SI Unit: Ampere per square metre (A/m²)

🎙️ Current density gives us a more localized picture of current flow. It's a vector, with its direction being the direction of current flow. This concept is particularly useful when dealing with non-uniform current distributions.

Ohm's Law: V = IR

  • Potential difference (V) across a conductor ∝ current (I)
  • Holds for metallic conductors at constant temperature
  • V = IR, where R is resistance

🎙️ Ohm's Law is a cornerstone of current electricity. It states that the voltage across a conductor is directly proportional to the current flowing through it, provided physical conditions like temperature remain constant. The proportionality constant is resistance.

Resistance: Opposition to Flow

  • Property of a material to oppose electric current flow
  • R = V/I; depends on material, length, area
  • SI Unit: Ohm (Ω)

🎙️ Resistance is a material's inherent property to oppose the flow of electric current. It depends on the material, its length, and its cross-sectional area. Think of it as friction for electrons.

Resistivity and Conductivity: Intrinsic Properties

  • Resistivity (ρ): Resistance of a material of unit length and unit cross-section (R = ρL/A)
  • Conductivity (σ): Reciprocal of resistivity (σ = 1/ρ)
  • SI Units: ρ in Ω·m, σ in S/m (Siemens per metre)

🎙️ While resistance depends on dimensions, resistivity (ρ) is an intrinsic property of the material itself, independent of its geometry. Conductivity (σ) is simply how well a material conducts electricity, being the inverse of resistivity.

Temperature Dependence of Resistance

  • Metals: Resistance increases with temperature (positive α)
  • Semiconductors: Resistance decreases with temperature (negative α)
  • R_T = R_0 (1 + αΔT)

🎙️ This is a crucial distinction. In metals, increased temperature means more atomic vibrations, hindering electron flow, thus increasing resistance. In semiconductors, higher temperature frees more charge carriers, thus decreasing resistance.

Worked Example 1: Calculating Resistance

  • Problem: A wire of length 2m, area 1x10⁻⁶ m², and resistivity 1.7x10⁻⁸ Ωm. Find its resistance.
  • Solution: R = ρL/A = (1.7x10⁻⁸ Ωm * 2m) / (1x10⁻⁶ m²) = 0.034 Ω
  • Common mistake: Units conversion errors.

🎙️ Let's apply our knowledge. If a wire has resistance R, length L, and cross-sectional area A, its resistivity ρ = (R * A) / L. Always ensure consistent units. A common mistake is forgetting to convert units like cm² to m².

Series Combination of Resistors

  • Resistors connected end-to-end, forming a single path
  • Same current flows through each resistor
  • Equivalent Resistance: R_eq = R1 + R2 + R3 + ...

🎙️ When resistors are connected in series, the total resistance is simply the sum of individual resistances. This arrangement increases the overall resistance of the circuit. Remember, current is the same through each resistor in series.

Parallel Combination of Resistors

  • Resistors connected between two common points
  • Same potential difference across each resistor
  • Equivalent Resistance: 1/R_eq = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + 1/R3 + ...

🎙️ In a parallel combination, the voltage across each resistor is the same, but the current divides. The equivalent resistance is less than the smallest individual resistance, effectively increasing the current capacity of the circuit.

Worked Example 2: Resistor Combinations

  • Problem: Two 6Ω resistors in parallel, then in series with a 3Ω resistor. Find R_eq.
  • Solution: R_parallel = (6*6)/(6+6) = 3Ω. R_eq = 3Ω + 3Ω = 6Ω.

EMF & Internal Resistance: The Cell's Heart

  • Electromotive Force (EMF, ε): Maximum potential difference of a source when no current is drawn
  • Internal Resistance (r): Opposition to current flow within the cell itself
  • Causes a voltage drop when current flows

🎙️ EMF is the work done by the cell per unit charge to drive it around the circuit, even when no current is drawn. Internal resistance is the resistance offered by the electrolyte and electrodes within the cell itself, leading to a voltage drop.

Terminal Voltage: What You Get

  • Actual potential difference across the cell terminals when current is flowing
  • During discharge: V = ε - Ir
  • During charging: V = ε + Ir

🎙️ Terminal voltage is the actual voltage available to the external circuit. When the cell is discharging, some voltage is lost across its internal resistance, so V is less than EMF. During charging, V is greater than EMF.

Cells in Series: Boosting Voltage

  • Positive terminal of one cell connected to negative of next
  • Total EMF: ε_eq = ε1 + ε2 + ...
  • Total Internal Resistance: r_eq = r1 + r2 + ...

🎙️ Connecting cells in series increases the total EMF available, which is useful for applications requiring higher voltages. The internal resistances also add up, so the overall internal resistance of the combination increases.

Cells in Parallel: Boosting Current Capacity

  • All positive terminals connected together, and all negative terminals together
  • For identical cells: ε_eq = ε (individual EMF)
  • For identical cells: 1/r_eq = 1/r1 + 1/r2 + ... or r_eq = r/n

🎙️ For identical cells, connecting them in parallel keeps the EMF the same but reduces the effective internal resistance, allowing for a larger current output. This is useful for applications requiring higher current for longer durations.

Worked Example 3: Cells & Terminal Voltage

  • Problem: Cell with ε=1.5V, r=0.5Ω. Current drawn is 0.2A. Find terminal voltage.
  • Solution: V = ε - Ir = 1.5V - (0.2A * 0.5Ω) = 1.5V - 0.1V = 1.4V
  • Common mistake: Forgetting to subtract or add Ir based on discharge/charge.

Kirchhoff's Laws: Circuit Analysis Tools

  • Junction Rule (KCL): Sum of currents entering a junction equals sum of currents leaving (Charge conservation)
  • Loop Rule (KVL): Algebraic sum of potential changes around any closed loop is zero (Energy conservation)
  • Essential for complex circuits

🎙️ Kirchhoff's Laws are indispensable for analyzing complex circuits where Ohm's law alone isn't sufficient. KCL is based on charge conservation, and KVL is based on energy conservation. Mastering these is key for circuit solving.

Wheatstone Bridge: Measuring Unknown Resistance

  • Consists of four resistors forming a bridge circuit
  • Used for precise measurement of unknown resistance
  • Balanced condition: P/Q = R/S (no current through galvanometer)

🎙️ The Wheatstone bridge is a very sensitive circuit for measuring unknown resistances. When the bridge is balanced, no current flows through the galvanometer arm, and a simple ratio of resistances holds true.

Metre Bridge: Practical Wheatstone Bridge

  • Laboratory application of the Wheatstone bridge principle
  • Uses a 1-meter long uniform resistance wire
  • Determines an unknown resistance by finding a null point

🎙️ The metre bridge is a laboratory instrument that uses the principle of the Wheatstone bridge. By finding the null point on the wire, where the galvanometer shows no deflection, we can easily determine an unknown resistance.

Potentiometer: Precise Voltage Measurement

  • Measures potential difference accurately without drawing current
  • Applications: Comparing EMFs of two cells
  • Applications: Determining internal resistance of a cell

🎙️ The potentiometer is a highly accurate device because it measures potential difference when no current flows through the galvanometer, making it an ideal voltmeter. It's also used to compare EMFs and find internal resistance of cells.

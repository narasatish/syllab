What is Electric Charge?

  • Intrinsic property of matter.
  • Causes electromagnetic interactions.
  • Two types: positive and negative.

🎙️ Electric charge is a fundamental property, just like mass. It's what makes things attract or repel electrically. We categorize charges into positive and negative, a convention established by Benjamin Franklin.

Key Properties of Electric Charges

  • Quantization: Charge exists in discrete packets (q = ne).
  • Conservation: Total charge in an isolated system is constant.
  • Additivity: Charges add up algebraically like scalars.

🎙️ Three essential properties govern electric charges. Quantization means charge comes in discrete packets. Conservation ensures charge is neither created nor destroyed. Additivity means charges simply add up like scalars.

Conductors vs. Insulators

  • Conductors: Allow electric charges to flow easily (e.g., metals).
  • Insulators: Resist the flow of electric charges (e.g., rubber).
  • Semiconductors: Exhibit intermediate conductivity.

🎙️ The ability of materials to conduct electricity varies greatly. Conductors like metals have free electrons, while insulators like plastic hold their electrons tightly. This difference is key to many applications.

Methods of Charging Objects

  • By Friction: Rubbing two different objects together.
  • By Conduction: Direct contact between a charged and uncharged object.
  • By Induction: Charging an object without direct contact.

🎙️ There are three primary ways to charge an object. Rubbing objects together causes charging by friction. Direct contact transfers charge via conduction. And induction allows charging from a distance, without physical touch.

Coulomb's Law: Force Between Charges

  • Quantifies electrostatic force between point charges.
  • F ∝ q₁q₂ / r².
  • Force is attractive for unlike charges, repulsive for like charges.

🎙️ Coulomb's Law is fundamental. It quantifies the force between two point charges, directly proportional to the product of their magnitudes and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them. Remember the constant 'k' or '1/4πε₀'.

📏
Measuring Force

Coulomb's Law: Vector Form

  • Expresses both magnitude and direction of force.
  • F₂₁ = k (q₁q₂ / r²₂) r̂₂₁.
  • Force acts along the line joining the two charges.

🎙️ While the scalar form gives the magnitude, the vector form of Coulomb's Law tells us the direction of the force. For like charges, it's repulsive; for unlike charges, it's attractive. The unit vector r̂ ensures correct direction.

Permittivity and Relative Permittivity

  • Permittivity (ε): Property of a medium affecting electric force.
  • Permittivity of free space (ε₀): 8.85 x 10⁻¹² F/m.
  • Relative permittivity (εᵣ or K): ε / ε₀ (dimensionless).

🎙️ The medium between charges affects the force. Permittivity measures how a medium permits an electric field. Relative permittivity, also called the dielectric constant, tells us how much weaker the force becomes in a medium compared to vacuum.

Worked Example: Coulomb's Law

  • Two point charges +3µC and +5µC are separated by 10 cm.
  • Calculate the magnitude of the electrostatic force between them.
  • Use F = k |q₁q₂| / r²; k = 9 × 10⁹ Nm²/C².

🎙️ Let's apply Coulomb's Law. Convert units to SI (microcoulombs to coulombs, cm to meters). Substitute values into the formula F = (9 x 10⁹ Nm²/C²) * (3 x 10⁻⁶ C) * (5 x 10⁻⁶ C) / (0.1 m)². The force will be repulsive.

Superposition Principle of Forces

  • Net force on any charge is the vector sum of individual forces.
  • Each force is calculated independently using Coulomb's Law.
  • Applicable for systems with multiple point charges.

🎙️ When multiple charges are present, the total force on any one charge is the vector sum of the forces exerted by all other individual charges. This principle simplifies complex charge configurations, allowing us to calculate the resultant force.

Electric Field: Concept and Definition

  • Region around a charge where its influence is felt.
  • Defined as force per unit positive test charge (E = F/q₀).
  • Vector quantity, SI unit: N/C or V/m.

🎙️ An electric charge creates an electric field in the space around it. This field is the mechanism through which charges exert forces on each other. It's defined as the force experienced by a unit positive test charge placed at that point.

Electric Dipole and Dipole Moment

  • Pair of equal and opposite charges (+q and -q).
  • Separated by a small distance, 2a.
  • Electric dipole moment (p) = q (2a), a vector quantity.

🎙️ An electric dipole is a fundamental concept, consisting of two charges of equal magnitude but opposite sign, separated by a small distance. The electric dipole moment, a vector quantity, characterizes the strength and orientation of the dipole.

E-Field on Axial Line of Dipole

  • Calculated along the line joining the two charges.
  • E_axial = 2kp / r³ (for r >> a).
  • Direction is same as the dipole moment vector.

🎙️ Calculating the electric field on the axial line of a dipole involves vector addition of fields from both charges. For distances much larger than the dipole separation, the field falls off as 1/r³, faster than a point charge.

⬆️
Along the Axis
18 / 25

E-Field on Equatorial Line of Dipole

  • Calculated on the perpendicular bisector of the dipole.
  • E_equatorial = -kp / r³ (for r >> a).
  • Direction is opposite to the dipole moment vector.

🎙️ The field on the equatorial line is also important. Here, the components perpendicular to the dipole axis cancel out, and the resultant field is antiparallel to the dipole moment. Again, for large distances, it falls as 1/r³.

Torque on a Dipole in Uniform Electric Field

  • A dipole experiences a torque (τ = pE sinθ).
  • Vector form: τ = p × E.
  • No net force on the dipole in a uniform field.

🎙️ When an electric dipole is placed in a uniform electric field, it experiences a torque that tends to align it with the field. There is no net force, but the turning effect causes rotation. This concept is vital for understanding dielectric materials.

Electric Flux: Definition and Concept

  • Measure of electric field lines passing through a surface.
  • Φ = E ⋅ A = EA cosθ (for uniform E and plane area A).
  • Scalar quantity, SI unit: N m²/C or V m.

🎙️ Electric flux quantifies the 'flow' of the electric field through a given surface. It's a measure of how many field lines cross the surface. It depends on the field strength, the area, and the orientation of the surface relative to the field.

Gauss's Law: Powerful Tool

  • Relates total electric flux through a closed surface to enclosed charge.
  • Φ = q_enclosed / ε₀.
  • Simplifies calculation of electric fields for symmetric charge distributions.

🎙️ Gauss's Law is incredibly powerful! It states that the total electric flux through any closed surface is proportional to the total electric charge enclosed within that surface. This law is particularly useful for finding electric fields of highly symmetric charge distributions.

Common Mistakes & Exam Tips

  • Vector vs. Scalar: Distinguish between vector (Force, Field, Dipole Moment) and scalar (Charge, Flux) quantities.
  • Units & Constants: Always use SI units and know values of ε₀, k.
  • Diagrams: Draw clear diagrams for all vector-related problems.

🎙️ Students often confuse scalar and vector quantities. Pay attention to units and constants – a small error can lead to a wrong answer. Always draw clear diagrams, especially for problems involving multiple charges or fields; it helps visualize directions and components.

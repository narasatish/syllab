Hertz's Observations (1887)

  • Spark discharge across electrodes enhanced by UV light.
  • Electrons emitted from metal surface under UV radiation.
  • Early hint of light's interaction with matter.

🎙️ While experimenting with electromagnetic waves, Hertz noticed that sparks across a detector loop became more vigorous when UV light illuminated the spark gap. This was an accidental but significant observation, suggesting that light could somehow influence the flow of electricity.

Hallwachs' and Lenard's Investigations

  • Hallwachs (1888): Negatively charged zinc plate loses charge under UV.
  • Lenard (1902): Discovered that emitted particles are electrons.
  • Photoelectrons' energy depends on light frequency, not intensity.

🎙️ Hallwachs confirmed that UV light causes a negatively charged zinc plate to discharge, while a positively charged plate does not. Lenard further demonstrated that the emitted particles were indeed electrons, which he called photoelectrons, and noted their surprising energy dependence.

Photoelectric Effect: Experimental Setup

  • Evacuated quartz tube with emitter (C) and collector (A).
  • Monochromatic light falls on C, electrons emitted.
  • Ammeter measures photocurrent, voltmeter measures potential difference.

🎙️ The setup typically involves an evacuated glass or quartz tube containing two electrodes: an emitter plate (cathode) and a collector plate (anode). Monochromatic light strikes the emitter, causing electrons to be ejected and collected, forming a measurable current.

Effect of Light Intensity on Photocurrent

  • Photocurrent is directly proportional to light intensity.
  • More intense light means more photoelectrons emitted per second.
  • Kinetic energy of photoelectrons is independent of intensity.

🎙️ When the intensity of light increases, the number of photoelectrons emitted per second also increases proportionally, leading to a larger photocurrent. However, surprisingly, the maximum kinetic energy of these emitted electrons remains unchanged, which contradicted classical wave theory.

Effect of Light Frequency and Threshold Frequency

  • Below a certain 'threshold frequency', no emission occurs.
  • Even with high intensity, no photoelectrons below threshold.
  • Above threshold, maximum KE of photoelectrons increases linearly with frequency.

🎙️ A critical observation is the existence of a threshold frequency, unique for each metal. If the incident light's frequency is below this value, no electrons are emitted, regardless of how intense the light is. Above this frequency, the kinetic energy of emitted electrons increases with frequency.

Effect of Potential and Stopping Potential

  • Photocurrent increases with positive collector potential.
  • At zero potential, some current still flows (energetic electrons).
  • Negative 'stopping potential' (V₀) stops current completely, KE_max = eV₀.

🎙️ As the positive potential on the collector plate increases, the photocurrent rises until saturation. When the collector potential is made negative, it repels the electrons. The minimum negative potential at which the photocurrent becomes zero is called the stopping potential, directly related to the maximum kinetic energy of the photoelectrons.

Summary: Laws of Photoelectric Emission

  • Instantaneous process (no time lag).
  • Threshold frequency exists for each metal.
  • Photocurrent proportional to intensity (above threshold).
  • Max KE of photoelectrons depends on frequency, not intensity.

🎙️ These four laws summarise the experimental observations of the photoelectric effect. They highlight the instantaneous nature of emission, the existence of a threshold frequency, the direct proportionality of current to intensity, and the dependence of kinetic energy on frequency.

Why Classical Wave Theory Failed

  • Wave theory predicts emission at any frequency (given enough intensity).
  • Predicts KE increases with intensity, not frequency.
  • Predicts time delay for energy accumulation.

🎙️ Classical wave theory predicted that light energy is spread continuously over the wavefront. This would mean that electrons could accumulate energy over time and be emitted at any frequency, given sufficient intensity, and with higher kinetic energy for more intense light. All these predictions contradict experimental observations.

Einstein's Quantum Explanation (1905)

  • Adopted Planck's quantum hypothesis.
  • Light consists of discrete energy packets called photons.
  • Energy of a photon: E = hν (h = Planck's constant, ν = frequency).

🎙️ Einstein provided a revolutionary explanation by extending Planck's quantum hypothesis. He proposed that light is not a continuous wave but rather a stream of discrete energy packets, or quanta, which he called photons. Each photon carries energy proportional to its frequency.

The Photoelectric Equation

  • One photon interacts with one electron.
  • Energy used for work function (φ₀) and kinetic energy (KE_max).
  • hν = φ₀ + KE_max, where φ₀ = hν₀.
  • KE_max = hν - hν₀ = eV₀.

🎙️ According to Einstein, when a photon strikes a metal, it transfers all its energy to a single electron. A portion of this energy, called the work function (φ₀), is used to liberate the electron from the metal surface, and the remaining energy is converted into the electron's maximum kinetic energy.

Worked Example: Photoelectric Effect

  • Problem: Light of frequency 7.2 x 10¹⁴ Hz is incident on a metal surface. The stopping potential is 2.5 V. Calculate the work function of the metal.
  • Given: ν = 7.2 x 10¹⁴ Hz, V₀ = 2.5 V, h = 6.63 x 10⁻³⁴ Js, e = 1.6 x 10⁻¹⁹ C.
  • Solution: KE_max = eV₀ = 1.6 x 10⁻¹⁹ C * 2.5 V = 4.0 x 10⁻¹⁹ J.
  • From Einstein's equation: φ₀ = hν - KE_max = (6.63 x 10⁻³⁴ * 7.2 x 10¹⁴) - 4.0 x 10⁻¹⁹ = 4.77 x 10⁻¹⁹ J - 4.0 x 10⁻¹⁹ J = 0.77 x 10⁻¹⁹ J.

🎙️ Let's apply Einstein's equation to a numerical problem. Remember that the maximum kinetic energy of the emitted electrons is directly related to the stopping potential. By substituting the given values, we can calculate the work function of the metal.

Common Mistakes in Photoelectric Effect

  • Confusing intensity with frequency: Intensity affects number, frequency affects energy.
  • Ignoring threshold frequency: No emission below ν₀, regardless of intensity.
  • Incorrect units: Ensure energy is in Joules or electron-volts consistently.

🎙️ Students often confuse the roles of intensity and frequency. Remember, intensity determines the *number* of photons and thus the photocurrent, while frequency determines the *energy* of individual photons and thus the kinetic energy of photoelectrons. Always check for the threshold frequency condition.

Properties of Photons

  • Energy E = hν = hc/λ.
  • Momentum p = h/λ = E/c.
  • Photons are electrically neutral and unaffected by E & B fields.
  • Travel at speed of light 'c' in vacuum.

🎙️ Photons are the fundamental particles of light, possessing both energy and momentum. They are always in motion at the speed of light and are unique in that they have zero rest mass and are electrically neutral, meaning they do not interact with electric or magnetic fields.

De Broglie's Hypothesis: Matter Waves

  • Louis de Broglie (1924): Proposed symmetry in nature.
  • If light can behave as particles, then matter (electrons, protons) should behave as waves.
  • Associated a wavelength with moving particles.

🎙️ Inspired by the dual nature of light, Louis de Broglie hypothesized that if radiation has a dual nature, then matter particles should also exhibit wave-like properties. This revolutionary idea suggested that every moving particle has a wave associated with it, known as a matter wave or de Broglie wave.

De Broglie Wavelength Equation

  • De Broglie wavelength λ = h/p, where p is momentum.
  • For a particle of mass m, velocity v: p = mv, so λ = h/mv.
  • For a charged particle accelerated through potential V: KE = eV, p = sqrt(2mKE), so λ = h/sqrt(2meV).

🎙️ The de Broglie wavelength is inversely proportional to the momentum of the particle. For macroscopic objects, this wavelength is extremely small and unobservable, but for microscopic particles like electrons, it becomes significant and measurable, leading to observable wave phenomena.

Worked Example: De Broglie Wavelength

  • Problem: Calculate the de Broglie wavelength of an electron accelerated through a potential difference of 100 V.
  • Given: V = 100 V, m_e = 9.1 x 10⁻³¹ kg, e = 1.6 x 10⁻¹⁹ C, h = 6.63 x 10⁻³⁴ Js.
  • Solution: λ = h / sqrt(2meV) = 6.63 x 10⁻³⁴ / sqrt(2 * 9.1 x 10⁻³¹ * 1.6 x 10⁻¹⁹ * 100).
  • λ ≈ 1.227 x 10⁻¹⁰ m = 0.1227 nm.

🎙️ Let's calculate the de Broglie wavelength for an electron. Notice how a relatively small accelerating voltage results in a wavelength comparable to atomic dimensions, which is why electron diffraction effects are observable, unlike for everyday objects.

Davisson-Germer Experiment (1927)

  • Experimental verification of de Broglie's hypothesis.
  • Electron beam diffracted by a nickel crystal.
  • Diffraction pattern similar to X-rays, confirming wave nature of electrons.

🎙️ The Davisson-Germer experiment provided direct experimental evidence for the wave nature of electrons. They observed that electrons scattered from a nickel crystal produced a diffraction pattern, a phenomenon characteristic of waves, thereby confirming de Broglie's prediction.

Applications of Dual Nature

  • Electron Microscope: Uses electron waves for high resolution imaging.
  • Photocells: Convert light energy to electrical energy (e.g., solar cells).
  • Night Vision Devices: Utilize photoelectric effect to amplify dim light.

🎙️ The understanding of the dual nature of radiation and matter has led to significant technological advancements. Electron microscopes, for instance, exploit the wave nature of electrons to achieve much higher magnifications than optical microscopes, revolutionizing biology and materials science.

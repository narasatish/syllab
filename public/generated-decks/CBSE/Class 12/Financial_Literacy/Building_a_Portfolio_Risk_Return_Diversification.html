Calculating Absolute Return (Example)

  • Formula: (Ending Value - Beginning Value) / Beginning Value * 100
  • Example: Invested ₹10,000, value grew to ₹12,000.
  • Calculation: (₹12,000 - ₹10,000) / ₹10,000 * 100
  • Absolute Return: 20%

🎙️ Absolute return tells you the total percentage change in your investment's value over its entire holding period. It's a straightforward measure of your overall gain or loss.

1Identify values
2Subtract initial from final
3Divide by initial
4Multiply by 100 for percentage

Calculating Annualized Return (Simplified Example)

  • Formula (simple): Absolute Return / Number of Years
  • Example: 20% absolute return over 2 years.
  • Calculation: 20% / 2 years
  • Annualized Return: 10% per year

🎙️ Annualized return helps us compare investments held for different durations. It normalizes the return to a per-year basis, making comparisons much easier and more meaningful.

Comparing Returns: Which is Better?

  • Investment A: 15% return in 1 year.
  • Investment B: 25% return in 3 years.
  • Annualized A: 15%. Annualized B: 25%/3 ≈ 8.33%.
  • Investment A offers a higher annualized return.

🎙️ When comparing investments, especially over different timeframes, always look at the annualized return. This gives you a clear picture of which investment performed better on a yearly basis.

Risk: The Uncertainty of Outcomes

  • Analogy: Crossing a busy street – you might reach safely, or you might not.
  • Definition: The possibility of losing money or earning less than expected.
  • Inherent in all investments; some are riskier than others.
  • Measured by volatility (how much an investment's value fluctuates).

🎙️ Risk is simply the chance that your actual return will differ from your expected return. It's the 'what if' factor in investing, and it's present in every financial decision you make.

Types of Investment Risk (Briefly)

  • Systematic Risk: Affects the entire market (e.g., economic recession, political instability).
  • Unsystematic Risk: Specific to a company or industry (e.g., a company's product failure, labour strike).
  • Systematic risk cannot be diversified away.
  • Unsystematic risk can be reduced through diversification.

🎙️ Understanding these two broad categories of risk is crucial. Systematic risk is like a rising tide lifting or lowering all boats, while unsystematic risk affects only specific boats.

Measuring Risk: Volatility & Standard Deviation

  • Volatility: How much an investment's price fluctuates over time.
  • High volatility = higher risk (greater ups and downs).
  • Standard Deviation: A statistical measure of volatility.
  • A higher standard deviation means higher risk.

🎙️ While the calculation of standard deviation is complex, conceptually, it helps us quantify risk. A stock that swings wildly in price has a higher standard deviation and is considered riskier.

How Diversification Works: Reducing Unsystematic Risk

  • If you invest only in one company's stock, its failure impacts you entirely.
  • If you invest in 10 different companies, one failure has less impact.
  • It cancels out the specific risks of individual assets.
  • It does NOT eliminate systematic (market) risk.

🎙️ Diversification is most effective against unsystematic risk. By holding a variety of assets that react differently to economic events, you smooth out the overall portfolio performance.

Benefits of Diversification

  • Provides stability during market downturns.
  • Enhances the probability of achieving long-term goals.
  • Smooths out portfolio performance over time.

🎙️ The primary benefit is risk reduction. A diversified portfolio is more resilient to shocks specific to a particular company or sector, making your investment journey less bumpy.

Building Your Portfolio: Practical Steps

  • Step 1: Define your financial goals (e.g., higher education, car).
  • Step 2: Assess your risk tolerance (conservative, moderate, aggressive).
  • Step 3: Determine asset allocation (mix of stocks, bonds, etc.).
  • Step 4: Diversify within each asset class and across sectors.

🎙️ Building a portfolio is a structured process. It starts with understanding yourself and your objectives, then strategically allocating your assets to achieve those goals while managing risk.

Worked Example: Riya's Diversified Portfolio

  • Riya invests ₹10,000: ₹5,000 in FD (5% return), ₹5,000 in stocks (15% return).
  • Total Return: (₹5,000 * 0.05) + (₹5,000 * 0.15) = ₹250 + ₹750 = ₹1,000.
  • Overall Portfolio Return: ₹1,000 / ₹10,000 = 10%.
  • Benefit: If stocks fell, FD provided stability. If FD was low, stocks boosted overall.

🎙️ This example shows how Riya, by diversifying, achieved a balanced return. Even if one part underperformed, the other could compensate, leading to a more stable overall gain.

What are Derivatives?

  • A financial contract whose value is 'derived' from an underlying asset.
  • Underlying assets can be stocks, commodities, currencies, interest rates, etc.
  • They don't have independent value; they depend on the price movements of the underlying asset.

🎙️ Think of a derivative as a shadow. The shadow's shape and size depend on the object casting it. Similarly, a derivative's value depends entirely on its underlying asset. If the asset's price changes, so does the derivative's value.

Purpose of Derivatives: Why Use Them?

  • Hedging: To reduce or 'hedge' against potential price risks.
  • Speculation: To profit from anticipated future price movements.
  • Arbitrage: To profit from price differences in different markets.

🎙️ Derivatives serve multiple purposes. Businesses often use them to protect themselves from adverse price changes, like a farmer selling his crop in advance. Savvy investors might use them to bet on future prices, while others exploit tiny price discrepancies across markets.

Main Types of Derivatives

  • Futures: Obligation to buy/sell at a future date.
  • Options: Right, but not obligation, to buy/sell.

🎙️ While there are several types, for Class 12 CBSE, our primary focus will be on Futures and Options. Forwards are similar to futures but are private agreements, and swaps involve exchanging cash flows.

Futures Contracts: The Obligation

  • An agreement to buy or sell an underlying asset.
  • At a predetermined price.
  • On a specified future date.
  • Both parties are *obligated* to fulfill the contract.

🎙️ A futures contract is a firm commitment. Once you enter into it, you are bound to either buy or sell the asset as agreed, regardless of what the market price is on the expiry date. This obligation is a key differentiator from options.

Key Features of Futures Contracts

  • Standardized: Fixed quantity, quality, and delivery dates.
  • Exchange-Traded: Traded on organized exchanges (e.g., NSE, BSE).
  • Margin Requirement: Initial deposit to cover potential losses.
  • Mark-to-Market: Daily settlement of gains/losses.

🎙️ Standardization ensures liquidity and transparency. Being exchange-traded means there's a clearing house guaranteeing performance. Margin is crucial; it's a security deposit. Mark-to-market means your account is adjusted daily based on price movements.

Futures Example: Farmer & Flour Mill

  • A farmer expects to harvest wheat in 3 months.
  • A flour mill needs wheat in 3 months.
  • They agree today on a price of ₹2,000 per quintal for 100 quintals.
  • This locks in the price for both, reducing uncertainty.

🎙️ Imagine a farmer worried about wheat prices falling, and a mill worried about prices rising. A futures contract allows them to agree on a price today for a future transaction, providing price certainty for both. This is a classic example of hedging.

Worked Example: Nifty Futures (Long Position)

  • You buy 1 lot (50 units) of Nifty Futures at ₹18,000, expiring in 1 month.
  • You expect Nifty to rise. Initial margin paid.
  • After 1 month, Nifty is at ₹18,200.
  • You fulfill your obligation to buy at ₹18,000 and immediately sell at ₹18,200.

🎙️ Here, you took a 'long' position, meaning you bet on the price increasing. Since your prediction was correct, you make a profit. Remember, you were obligated to buy at ₹18,000, even if the market price was different.

1Buy Futures @ ₹18,000
2Nifty Rises to ₹18,200
3Profit = (18,200 - 18,000) * 50
4Total Profit = ₹10,000

Worked Example: Nifty Futures (Short Position)

  • You sell 1 lot (50 units) of Nifty Futures at ₹18,000, expiring in 1 month.
  • You expect Nifty to fall. Initial margin paid.
  • After 1 month, Nifty is at ₹17,800.
  • You fulfill your obligation to sell at ₹18,000 and immediately buy at ₹17,800.

🎙️ In a 'short' position, you bet on the price decreasing. Since Nifty fell, you can buy it cheaper in the spot market and deliver it against your futures contract, making a profit. If Nifty had risen, you would have incurred a loss.

1Sell Futures @ ₹18,000
2Nifty Falls to ₹17,800
3Profit = (18,000 - 17,800) * 50
4Total Profit = ₹10,000

Advantages of Futures Contracts

  • Price Discovery: Help in determining future spot prices.
  • Risk Management: Effective hedging tool for producers/consumers.
  • Liquidity: Exchange-traded contracts offer high liquidity.
  • Leverage: Control large value of assets with small margin.

🎙️ Futures are powerful tools. They provide a clear indication of future price expectations and are excellent for managing price risk. The leverage aspect means you can control a significant asset value with a relatively small upfront investment, amplifying both gains and losses.

Disadvantages of Futures Contracts

  • High Risk: Leverage can lead to significant losses.
  • Margin Calls: If losses accumulate, more margin may be required.
  • Complexity: Requires good understanding of market dynamics.
  • Obligation: You *must* buy/sell, even if unfavorable.

🎙️ While advantageous, futures come with substantial risks. The same leverage that magnifies gains can also magnify losses, potentially exceeding your initial margin. Margin calls can be demanding, and the obligation to transact can be costly if the market moves against you.

Options Contracts: The Right, Not the Obligation

  • A contract giving the buyer the *right*, but not the obligation, to buy or sell an underlying asset.
  • At a predetermined price (strike price).
  • On or before a specified future date (expiry date).
  • The buyer pays a 'premium' for this right.

🎙️ This is the fundamental difference from futures. As an option buyer, you have the flexibility to choose whether to exercise your right or let the option expire worthless. This flexibility comes at a cost, known as the premium.

Two Types of Options: Call & Put

  • Call Option: Gives the buyer the right to BUY the underlying asset.
  • Put Option: Gives the buyer the right to SELL the underlying asset.

🎙️ These are the two basic types of options. A call option is used when you expect the price to rise, giving you the right to buy at a lower, pre-agreed price. A put option is used when you expect the price to fall, giving you the right to sell at a higher, pre-agreed price.

Call Option Explained

  • Buyer has the right to buy the underlying asset.
  • Seller (writer) has the obligation to sell if exercised.
  • Buyers profit if the underlying price rises above the strike price + premium.
  • Sellers profit from the premium if the option expires unexercised.

🎙️ Think of 'Call' as calling the asset to you, i.e., buying it. A call option buyer is bullish. Their maximum loss is the premium paid. The call option seller is bearish or neutral and hopes the price stays below the strike so they keep the premium.

Put Option Explained

  • Buyer has the right to sell the underlying asset.
  • Seller (writer) has the obligation to buy if exercised.
  • Buyers profit if the underlying price falls below the strike price - premium.
  • Sellers profit from the premium if the option expires unexercised.

🎙️ Think of 'Put' as putting the asset away, i.e., selling it. A put option buyer is bearish. Again, their maximum loss is limited to the premium. The put option seller is bullish or neutral and earns the premium if the price stays above the strike.

Key Terms in Options Trading

  • Strike Price: The predetermined price at which the asset can be bought/sold.
  • Premium: The price paid by the option buyer to the seller for the right.
  • Expiry Date: The last date on which the option can be exercised.
  • Lot Size: The fixed number of units of the underlying asset in one contract.

🎙️ These terms are fundamental to understanding options. The strike price is your reference point. The premium is your upfront cost and maximum loss as a buyer. The expiry date defines the lifespan of your right, and lot size determines the total value of your contract.

Worked Example: Call Option Buyer

  • You buy a Call Option on Reliance stock with a strike price of ₹2,500.
  • Premium paid = ₹50 per share. Lot size = 250 shares.
  • Total premium paid = ₹50 * 250 = ₹12,500.
  • Scenario 1: Reliance closes at ₹2,600 on expiry. You exercise.
  • Scenario 2: Reliance closes at ₹2,400 on expiry. You let it lapse.

🎙️ In Scenario 1, you make a profit because the stock price went above your strike price plus premium. In Scenario 2, the stock price fell below your strike, so exercising would lead to a bigger loss. You simply let the option expire, limiting your loss to the premium.

1Spot Price ₹2,600: Buy @ ₹2,500, Sell @ ₹2,600. Profit = (₹100*250) - ₹12,500 = ₹12,500
2Spot Price ₹2,400: Don't Exercise. Loss = ₹12,500 (premium)

Worked Example: Put Option Buyer

  • You buy a Put Option on TCS stock with a strike price of ₹3,500.
  • Premium paid = ₹70 per share. Lot size = 150 shares.
  • Total premium paid = ₹70 * 150 = ₹10,500.
  • Scenario 1: TCS closes at ₹3,400 on expiry. You exercise.
  • Scenario 2: TCS closes at ₹3,600 on expiry. You let it lapse.

🎙️ Here, you profited when the stock price fell below your strike price, allowing you to sell at a higher price than the market. If the price had risen, you would not exercise, limiting your loss to the premium paid, demonstrating the limited risk for an option buyer.

1Spot Price ₹3,400: Sell @ ₹3,500, Buy @ ₹3,400. Profit = (₹100*150) - ₹10,500 = ₹4,500
2Spot Price ₹3,600: Don't Exercise. Loss = ₹10,500 (premium)

Moneyness of Options

  • In-the-Money (ITM): Option has intrinsic value if exercised immediately.
  • At-the-Money (ATM): Strike price equals the underlying asset's current price.
  • Out-of-the-Money (OTM): Option has no intrinsic value; exercising would be unprofitable.

🎙️ This concept describes the profitability of an option relative to its strike price and the current market price. An ITM option is 'in profit' before considering the premium, while an OTM option is 'out of profit' and likely to expire worthless if it stays that way.

Advantages of Options Contracts

  • Limited Risk (for buyer): Maximum loss is limited to the premium paid.
  • Flexibility: Buyer has the right, not the obligation, to act.
  • Leverage: Control a large asset value with a smaller premium.
  • Income Generation (for seller): Can earn premium by writing options.

🎙️ The limited risk for buyers is a major draw for options. This allows for strategic bets without the fear of unlimited losses. Sellers, however, face unlimited risk but earn income from the premium received, making it a strategy for those with a specific market view.

👍

Disadvantages of Options Contracts

  • Complexity: More intricate strategies and factors affect pricing.
  • Time Decay: Options lose value as they approach expiry.
  • High Risk (for seller): Unlimited loss potential for option writers.
  • Premium Cost: Options can be expensive, eating into potential profits.

🎙️ Options are not without their drawbacks. Their pricing is complex, influenced by factors like volatility and time. Time decay, or 'theta,' is a constant enemy of the option buyer. Sellers face substantial risk, and the premium itself can be a significant cost for buyers.

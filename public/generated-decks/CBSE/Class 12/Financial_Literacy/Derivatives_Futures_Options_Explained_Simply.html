What are Derivatives? The 'Derived' Value

  • Financial instruments whose value comes from an 'underlying' asset.
  • They don't have intrinsic value on their own.
  • Think of them as contracts based on something else.

🎙️ Think of derivatives like an insurance policy. An insurance policy's value comes from the asset it covers, like your car or health. Similarly, a derivative's value is *derived* from an underlying asset, such as a stock or commodity.

The 'Underlying' Asset: The Source

🎙️ Just like a tree grows from its roots, a derivative's value comes from its 'underlying' asset. This can be anything from shares of a company, government bonds, essential commodities like gold or oil, foreign currencies, or even interest rates.

Why Do We Use Derivatives?

  • Risk Management (Hedging): Protect against adverse price movements.
  • Speculation: Bet on future price direction for profit.
  • Arbitrage: Exploit price differences in different markets.

🎙️ Derivatives serve three main purposes: managing price risk (hedging), speculating on future price movements, and exploiting price differences in different markets (arbitrage). Understanding these uses is key to their importance.

Main Types of Derivatives

  • Forwards: Customized, OTC contracts.
  • Futures: Standardized, exchange-traded contracts.
  • Options: Gives the right, not obligation, to buy/sell.
  • Swaps: Agreements to exchange cash flows.

🎙️ While there are several types, we'll focus on the most common and important ones for your syllabus: Futures and Options. Forwards are similar to futures but are customized, while swaps involve exchanging cash flows.

Introducing Futures Contracts

  • A standardized agreement to buy or sell an asset.
  • At a predetermined price on a specified future date.
  • Traded on organized exchanges, ensuring transparency.

🎙️ A futures contract is a standardized agreement to buy or sell an asset at a predetermined price on a specified future date. It's like our mango deal, but with strict rules set by a regulated exchange, making it transparent and liquid.

Futures vs. Forwards: Key Differences

  • Futures are standardized; Forwards are customized.
  • Futures are exchange-traded; Forwards are OTC (Over-The-Counter).
  • Futures have daily settlement (Marking to Market); Forwards settle at maturity.

🎙️ Understanding these differences is crucial for exams. Futures offer greater liquidity and reduced default risk due to standardization and exchange trading, unlike customized, private forward contracts.

Key Terms in Futures Contracts

  • Spot Price: Current market price of the underlying asset.
  • Futures Price: Agreed price for future delivery/settlement.
  • Expiry Date: The date when the contract matures and must be settled.
  • Lot Size: The fixed quantity of the underlying asset in one contract.

🎙️ These terms are fundamental to understanding futures. The lot size ensures contracts are standardized, and the expiry date clearly defines the contract's lifespan. Remember, futures price is not necessarily the spot price.

How Futures Work: The Farmer's Hedge Revisited

  • Farmer expects to harvest 100 quintals of wheat in 3 months.
  • Current spot price: ₹2000/quintal. He fears price drop.
  • He sells a futures contract for 100 quintals at ₹2050/quintal, expiring in 3 months.
  • He has locked in his selling price, hedging against a fall.

🎙️ By selling a futures contract, the farmer ensures he will receive at least ₹2050 per quintal, regardless of the spot price at harvest. This protects him from price volatility, even if he misses out on higher prices.

Futures Positions: Long & Short

  • Long Position (Buyer): Agrees to *buy* the underlying asset.
  • Short Position (Seller): Agrees to *sell* the underlying asset.
  • Both parties have an obligation to fulfill the contract.

🎙️ It's crucial to remember that both the buyer (long) and seller (short) in a futures contract are obligated to perform. There is no 'option' to back out; the contract must be settled.

Marking to Market: Daily Settlement

  • Futures contracts are 'marked to market' daily.
  • Profits and losses are calculated and settled at the end of each trading day.
  • This process reduces the risk of default by either party.

🎙️ Unlike forwards, futures contracts are 'marked to market' daily. This means profits and losses are calculated and settled each day, preventing large defaults and ensuring transparency in the market. It's a continuous adjustment.

Introducing Options Contracts

  • Gives the buyer the *right*, but not the *obligation*, to buy or sell.
  • At a specific price (strike price) on or before a certain date (expiry).
  • The buyer pays a 'premium' for this right.

🎙️ Options are different from futures. An option gives the buyer the *right*, but not the *obligation*, to buy or sell an underlying asset at a specific price on or before a certain date. This flexibility comes at a cost, known as the premium.

Key Terms in Options Contracts

  • Strike Price: The predetermined price at which the underlying can be bought/sold.
  • Premium: The price paid by the option buyer to the seller for the right.
  • Expiry Date: The last date on which the option can be exercised.
  • Underlying Asset: The asset on which the option contract is based.

🎙️ These terms are crucial for understanding options. The premium is the cost of the option, and the strike price determines the profitability. Remember, the option expires, so timing is important.

Option Buyer vs. Seller

  • Buyer (Holder): Pays premium, has the right, limited loss (to premium).
  • Seller (Writer): Receives premium, has the obligation, potentially unlimited loss.

🎙️ This is a key distinction. The option buyer's risk is capped at the premium paid, while the seller, who receives the premium, faces potentially unlimited losses if the market moves unfavorably and the option is exercised.

Option States: ITM, ATM, OTM

  • In-the-Money (ITM): Option has intrinsic value (profitable to exercise).
  • At-the-Money (ATM): Strike price equals current spot price.
  • Out-of-the-Money (OTM): Option has no intrinsic value (not profitable to exercise).

🎙️ An option is 'In-the-Money' (ITM) if exercising it would be profitable. 'At-the-Money' (ATM) when the strike price is the same as the current price. 'Out-of-the-Money' (OTM) if it has no intrinsic value and would not be profitable to exercise.

How Call Options Work: An Example

  • You buy a Call Option for Reliance shares: Strike ₹2500, Premium ₹50, Expiry 1 month.
  • Scenario 1: Reliance shares rise to ₹2600. You exercise, buy at ₹2500, sell at ₹2600. Profit = (₹2600 - ₹2500) - ₹50 = ₹50.
  • Scenario 2: Reliance shares fall to ₹2400. You let the option expire. Loss = ₹50 (premium).

🎙️ This example shows the limited loss potential of an option buyer. You only exercise if it's profitable. Otherwise, you let it expire, and your maximum loss is simply the premium you paid.

How Put Options Work: An Example

  • You own Infosys shares at ₹1500. You buy a Put Option to protect: Strike ₹1450, Premium ₹30, Expiry 1 month.
  • Scenario 1: Infosys shares fall to ₹1400. You exercise, sell at ₹1450. Profit = (₹1450 - ₹1400) - ₹30 = ₹20 (protection gain).
  • Scenario 2: Infosys shares rise to ₹1550. You let the option expire. Loss = ₹30 (premium).

🎙️ Here, the put option acts as an insurance. It limits your downside risk by allowing you to sell at a minimum price. If the shares rise, you simply lose the premium but benefit from the share price increase.

Common Mistakes in Derivatives

  • Confusing 'obligation' (Futures) with 'right' (Options).
  • Ignoring the cost of premium (Options) or margin (Futures).
  • Not understanding ITM/ATM/OTM for both Call and Put options.
  • Overlooking the significance of expiry dates in calculations.

🎙️ Many students mix up the mandatory nature of futures with the optional nature of options. Also, forgetting about the costs involved (margin for futures, premium for options) and how different price scenarios affect options is a common pitfall. Always pay attention to the expiry date.

Recap: Key Takeaways

  • Derivatives derive their value from an underlying asset.
  • Futures contracts involve an *obligation* to buy/sell at a future date.
  • Options contracts give the *right*, but not the obligation, to buy/sell.
  • Both are used for hedging risk and for speculation in financial markets.

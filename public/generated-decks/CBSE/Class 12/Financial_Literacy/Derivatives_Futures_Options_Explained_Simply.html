What are Derivatives?

  • Financial contracts deriving value from an underlying asset.
  • Value changes as the underlying asset's price changes.
  • Underlying assets: stocks, commodities, currencies, interest rates.

🎙️ Think of a derivative as something whose value is 'derived' from something else. Just like the taste of orange juice is derived from oranges, a derivative contract's value comes from an underlying asset like a share or a barrel of oil.

Why Use Derivatives?

  • Risk Management (Hedging): Protect against adverse price movements.
  • Speculation: Profit from anticipated price changes.
  • Arbitrage: Profit from price differences in different markets.

🎙️ Derivatives serve multiple purposes. The most common are hedging, which is like an insurance policy, and speculation, where investors bet on future prices. Arbitrage is about exploiting temporary price inefficiencies.

Main Types of Derivatives

  • Futures: Obligation to buy/sell at a future date.
  • Options: Right, but not obligation, to buy/sell.
  • Forwards: Similar to futures, but customized.
  • Swaps: Exchange of cash flows.

🎙️ While there are several types, Futures and Options are the most commonly traded on exchanges and are central to our CBSE syllabus. We'll briefly touch upon Forwards and Swaps later, but our focus will be primarily on the first two.

Futures Contracts: The Basics

  • Agreement to buy or sell an asset at a predetermined price.
  • Transaction takes place on a specific future date.
  • Standardized contracts traded on an exchange.
  • Obligation for both parties.

🎙️ A futures contract is a firm commitment. Both the buyer and the seller are obligated to fulfill their part of the deal on the expiry date, regardless of the market price at that time. This standardization helps in easy trading.

Futures Example: Farmer & Baker

  • Farmer expects to harvest wheat in 3 months.
  • Baker needs wheat in 3 months.

🎙️ Imagine a farmer and a baker. The farmer wants to secure a price for his future harvest, and the baker wants to lock in his cost. A futures contract allows them to fix the price today, removing uncertainty for both.

Key Terms in Futures

  • Underlying Asset: The asset being traded (e.g., Nifty 50, Gold).
  • Expiry Date: The date the contract ceases to exist.
  • Lot Size: Fixed quantity of the underlying asset per contract.
  • Tick Size: Minimum price fluctuation.

🎙️ These terms are fundamental. The underlying asset dictates what you're trading. The expiry date is when the contract ends, and lot size determines the total value of one contract. Understanding these is critical for calculations.

Margin in Futures Trading

  • Initial Margin: Deposit required to open a futures position.
  • Maintenance Margin: Minimum margin level to be maintained.
  • Margin Call: If margin falls below maintenance level, more funds are required.

🎙️ Futures involve leverage, meaning you control a large value with a small deposit. This deposit is called margin. If your position moves against you, your margin might fall, triggering a margin call, where you must add more funds or your position will be closed.

Worked Example: Futures P/L

  • You buy 1 Nifty Futures contract at ₹18,000.
  • Lot Size: 50. Expiry: 1 month.
  • Scenario A: Nifty closes at ₹18,200.
  • Scenario B: Nifty closes at ₹17,900.

🎙️ Let's calculate. In Scenario A, profit per unit is ₹200 (18200-18000). Total profit: 200 * 50 = ₹10,000. In Scenario B, loss per unit is ₹100 (18000-17900). Total loss: 100 * 50 = ₹5,000. This shows how P/L is magnified.

Options Contracts: The Basics

  • Gives the holder the right, but not the obligation.
  • To buy or sell an underlying asset.
  • At a specified price (strike price) on or before an expiry date.
  • The buyer pays a premium to the seller.

🎙️ This is the key difference from futures. With options, you have the flexibility. You pay a small fee, called the premium, for this right. If the market moves favorably, you exercise your right; otherwise, you let it expire, losing only the premium.

⚖️
Right vs. Obligation

Call Option: Right to Buy

  • Gives the holder the right to buy the underlying asset.
  • At a fixed strike price, by the expiry date.
  • Buyers expect the price to rise.
  • Sellers expect the price to fall or stay same.

🎙️ Think of a Call option as a bet that the price of the underlying asset will go up. If it does, you can buy it at the lower strike price and sell it at the higher market price, making a profit. If it doesn't, you just lose the premium.

⬆️
Bullish Bet

Put Option: Right to Sell

  • Gives the holder the right to sell the underlying asset.
  • At a fixed strike price, by the expiry date.
  • Buyers expect the price to fall.
  • Sellers expect the price to rise or stay same.

🎙️ Conversely, a Put option is a bet that the price of the underlying asset will go down. If it falls, you can sell it at the higher strike price, even though its market value is lower. This is often used for hedging against price drops.

Options Example: Car Insurance

  • You own a car worth ₹5 Lakhs today.
  • You buy an insurance policy (option) for ₹5,000 premium.
  • Policy guarantees you ₹4.8 Lakhs if car value drops.

🎙️ This is a simple analogy. Your car insurance is like a put option. You pay a premium (₹5,000) for the right to sell your car for a minimum value (₹4.8 Lakhs) if its market value falls significantly. If the car's value increases, you don't use the 'option' but still paid the premium.

Key Terms in Options

  • Strike Price: The predetermined price at which the asset can be bought/sold.
  • Premium: The price paid by the option buyer to the seller.
  • Expiry Date: The last day the option can be exercised.
  • Lot Size: Fixed quantity of the underlying asset per contract.

🎙️ These terms are crucial for options. The premium is your cost for the flexibility. The strike price is your reference point for profit or loss. And remember the lot size, as it multiplies your per-unit profit or loss.

Call Options: ITM, ATM, OTM

  • In-the-Money (ITM): Spot Price > Strike Price (Profitable)
  • At-the-Money (ATM): Spot Price = Strike Price (Break-even potential)
  • Out-of-the-Money (OTM): Spot Price < Strike Price (Not profitable)

🎙️ For a Call option buyer, you want the spot price to be much higher than your strike price to be ITM and profitable. If it's OTM, the option will likely expire worthless, and you lose only the premium.

Worked Example: Call Option P/L

  • You buy 1 Nifty Call Option: Strike ₹18,000, Premium ₹100.
  • Lot Size: 50. Expiry: 1 month.
  • Scenario A: Nifty closes at ₹18,250.
  • Scenario B: Nifty closes at ₹17,900.

🎙️ In Scenario A, Nifty is 18250 > 18000. Intrinsic Value = 250. Net P/L per unit = 250 - 100 = 150. Total Profit = 150 * 50 = ₹7,500. In Scenario B, Nifty is 17900 < 18000. Option expires worthless. Total Loss = Premium * Lot Size = 100 * 50 = ₹5,000.

Worked Example: Put Option P/L

  • You buy 1 Nifty Put Option: Strike ₹18,000, Premium ₹100.
  • Lot Size: 50. Expiry: 1 month.
  • Scenario A: Nifty closes at ₹17,700.
  • Scenario B: Nifty closes at ₹18,100.

🎙️ In Scenario A, Nifty is 17700 < 18000. Intrinsic Value = 18000 - 17700 = 300. Net P/L per unit = 300 - 100 = 200. Total Profit = 200 * 50 = ₹10,000. In Scenario B, Nifty is 18100 > 18000. Option expires worthless. Total Loss = Premium * Lot Size = 100 * 50 = ₹5,000.

Futures vs. Options: Key Differences

  • Obligation: Futures (Yes), Options (No, only right).
  • Premium: Futures (No), Options (Yes, paid by buyer).
  • Risk: Futures (Unlimited P/L), Options (Limited loss for buyer, unlimited for seller).
  • Flexibility: Futures (Less), Options (More).

🎙️ This comparison is vital for your exams. The obligation and premium are the most fundamental distinctions. Understand how these differences translate into varying risk-reward profiles for traders.

Risks in Derivatives Trading

  • Leverage: Magnifies both profits and losses.
  • Volatility: Sudden price swings can lead to significant losses.
  • Complexity: Requires deep understanding; not for beginners.
  • Expiry: Time decay (especially for options) can erode value.

🎙️ While powerful, derivatives come with significant risks. Leverage is a double-edged sword. Options also suffer from time decay, meaning their value erodes as the expiry date approaches, even if the underlying price doesn't move much.

What is a Sequence?

  • An ordered list of numbers or objects.
  • Each item in the list is called a 'term'.
  • It follows a specific rule or pattern.

🎙️ Think of a sequence as a line-up where everyone has their specific spot. The key is that there's an order, and usually, a secret rule that tells us how to get from one person (or number) to the next. This rule is what makes predicting possible.

Types of Sequences: Finite vs. Infinite

  • Finite Sequence: Has a limited number of terms, it has an end.
  • Example: Days of the week (7 terms).
  • Infinite Sequence: Continues indefinitely, it has no end.
  • Example: Natural numbers (1, 2, 3, ...).

🎙️ Just like a story can have an end or be an ongoing series, sequences can be finite or infinite. Knowing whether a sequence has a last term helps us understand its nature and how we might work with it.

What are Progressions?

  • Progressions are special types of sequences.
  • The rule linking consecutive terms is always fixed and very clear.
  • We will focus on two main types: Arithmetic and Geometric Progressions.

🎙️ While all progressions are sequences, not all sequences are progressions. Progressions have a very strict, mathematical rule that is easy to define. This makes them predictable and powerful tools in math.

⚙️
Fixed Mechanism

Arithmetic Progression (AP)

  • A sequence where the difference between consecutive terms is constant.
  • This constant difference is called the 'common difference' (d).
  • Think of it as adding or subtracting the same number each time.

🎙️ An Arithmetic Progression, or AP, is like climbing a staircase where each step has the exact same height. The 'common difference' is that consistent height, which can be positive (climbing up) or negative (climbing down).

↔️
Constant Gap
10 / 25

AP: Understanding Common Difference (d)

  • To find 'd', subtract any term from its succeeding term.
  • d = a₂ - a₁ = a₃ - a₂ = a₄ - a₃, and so on.
  • The common difference 'd' can be positive, negative, or even zero.

🎙️ This formula helps us calculate 'd'. If the differences are not constant, then it's not an AP. Remember, 'd' can be positive if the sequence is increasing, negative if it's decreasing, or zero if all terms are the same.

1a₂ - a₁
2a₃ - a₂
3a₄ - a₃

Real-Life AP Example: Saving Money

  • Imagine you save ₹100 in the first month.
  • Then you decide to save ₹50 more each subsequent month.
  • Sequence: ₹100, ₹150, ₹200, ₹250, ...
  • Here, the common difference (d) is ₹50.

🎙️ Saving money is a great real-world example of an AP. If you consistently add a fixed amount to your savings each period, the total amount in your account will form an arithmetic progression. This helps in financial planning.

The nth Term of an AP

  • We can find any term in an AP without listing all of them.
  • If 'a' is the first term and 'd' is the common difference, then:
  • The nth term, denoted as aₙ = a + (n-1)d.

🎙️ This formula is incredibly powerful! It's like having a magic key that unlocks any term in the sequence, no matter how far down the line it is. You just need the first term, the common difference, and the position 'n' you're looking for.

📈
Formula for Growth
13 / 25

Deriving aₙ = a + (n-1)d

  • First term (a₁): a
  • Second term (a₂): a + d
  • Third term (a₃): a + 2d
  • Fourth term (a₄): a + 3d
  • Notice the pattern: 'd' is multiplied by (n-1) for the nth term.

🎙️ Let's see how this formula comes about. Each time we move to the next term, we add 'd'. So, for the 2nd term, we add 'd' once; for the 3rd term, twice, and so on. This shows that for the 'n'th term, 'd' is added 'n-1' times.

2a+d

14 / 25

Worked Example: Finding the 10th Term

  • Consider the AP: 2, 5, 8, 11, ...
  • Here, the first term (a) = 2.
  • The common difference (d) = 5 - 2 = 3.
  • To find the 10th term (a₁₀): a₁₀ = a + (10-1)d = 2 + (9) * 3 = 2 + 27 = 29.

🎙️ Let's put the formula into practice. With a given AP, we first identify 'a' and 'd'. Then, we substitute these values into our formula for the nth term. As you can see, finding the 10th term is straightforward with this method.

Arithmetic Mean

  • If three terms a, b, c are in an Arithmetic Progression,
  • Then 'b' is called the Arithmetic Mean of 'a' and 'c'.
  • The formula for the arithmetic mean is: b = (a + c) / 2.

🎙️ The concept of Arithmetic Mean is closely related to AP. It's essentially the middle term of any three consecutive terms in an AP. It represents the average value between two numbers, maintaining the common difference.

Geometric Progression (GP)

  • A sequence where the ratio between consecutive terms is constant.
  • This constant ratio is called the 'common ratio' (r).
  • Think of it as multiplying or dividing by the same number each time.

🎙️ Now, let's explore another fascinating type of progression: Geometric Progression, or GP. Instead of adding a fixed amount, here we multiply by a fixed amount. This leads to much faster growth or decay!

✖️
Constant Factor
18 / 25

GP: Understanding Common Ratio (r)

  • To find 'r', divide any term by its preceding term.
  • r = a₂ / a₁ = a₃ / a₂ = a₄ / a₃, and so on.
  • The common ratio 'r' can be positive or negative, but never zero.

🎙️ Similar to 'd' in AP, 'r' is the defining characteristic of a GP. It's found by division. If the ratios are not constant, it's not a GP. 'r' cannot be zero because that would make all subsequent terms zero, losing the progression.

1a₂ / a₁
2a₃ / a₂
3a₄ / a₃

Real-Life GP Example: Bacterial Growth

  • Imagine a single bacterium that doubles every hour.
  • Sequence: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, ...
  • Here, the common ratio (r) is 2.
  • This shows rapid, exponential growth.

🎙️ Bacterial growth is a classic example of a Geometric Progression. The population doesn't just add a few bacteria; it multiplies. This rapid increase illustrates the power of a common ratio greater than one.

The nth Term of a GP

  • Similar to AP, we have a formula for the nth term of a GP.
  • If 'a' is the first term and 'r' is the common ratio, then:
  • The nth term, denoted as aₙ = a * r^(n-1).

🎙️ Just like with APs, this formula is your shortcut to finding any term in a GP. Notice the exponent (n-1) applied to 'r'. This is what drives the characteristic exponential growth or decay of geometric progressions.

🚀
Power of Ratio

Deriving aₙ = a * r^(n-1)

  • First term (a₁): a
  • Second term (a₂): a * r
  • Third term (a₃): a * r²
  • Fourth term (a₄): a * r³
  • Notice the pattern: 'r' is raised to the power (n-1) for the nth term.

🎙️ Let's trace the pattern for the GP formula. Each time we move to the next term, we multiply by 'r'. So, for the 2nd term, 'r' is used once; for the 3rd term, twice, and so on. This confirms 'r' is raised to the power (n-1) for the 'n'th term.

Worked Example: Finding the 5th Term (GP)

  • Consider the GP: 3, 6, 12, ...
  • Here, the first term (a) = 3.
  • The common ratio (r) = 6 / 3 = 2.
  • To find the 5th term (a₅): a₅ = a * r^(5-1) = 3 * 2⁴ = 3 * 16 = 48.

🎙️ Let's work through an example for GP. First, identify 'a' and 'r'. Then, substitute these into the formula for the nth term. You can see how quickly the numbers grow in a GP, even with a small common ratio like 2.

Recap: AP vs. GP

  • Arithmetic Progression (AP): Involves a common difference (d) that is added or subtracted.
  • Geometric Progression (GP): Involves a common ratio (r) that is multiplied or divided.
  • Both are sequences with predictable patterns.
  • They help us understand growth, decay, and ordered lists.

🎙️ To summarize, the core difference lies in the operation: APs use addition/subtraction, while GPs use multiplication/division. Both are powerful tools for predicting future terms and understanding various phenomena in the world around us.

AP (Add/Subtract 'd')
GP (Multiply/Divide 'r')

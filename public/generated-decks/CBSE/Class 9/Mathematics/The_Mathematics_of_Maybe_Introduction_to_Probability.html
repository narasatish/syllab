Random Experiment: What Is It?

  • An action with unpredictable outcomes.
  • All possible outcomes are known beforehand.
  • The exact outcome cannot be predicted.

🎙️ Imagine rolling a standard die. You know it can land on 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, or 6. But you can't predict which number it will be *before* you roll it. This act of rolling the die is called a 'random experiment'.

Outcomes: What Can Happen?

  • Each possible result of a random experiment.
  • They are the building blocks of probability.
  • Every time you perform an experiment, an outcome occurs.

🎙️ When you toss a coin, what can happen? It can land on 'Heads' or 'Tails'. These individual results – Heads and Tails – are called the 'outcomes' of the coin toss experiment. Simple, right?

Sample Space: All Possibilities

  • The set of all possible outcomes of an experiment.
  • Represented by 'S' or 'Ω'.
  • It lists everything that *could* happen.

🎙️ For our coin toss, the outcomes were Heads and Tails. The 'sample space' is simply the collection of *all* these possible outcomes. So, for a coin toss, the sample space is {Heads, Tails}. For a die roll, it's {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}.

Event: A Specific Outcome or Group

  • One or more outcomes from the sample space.
  • We are interested in whether an event happens.
  • It's a subset of the sample space.

🎙️ An 'event' is what we are specifically looking for. For example, if you roll a die, 'getting an even number' is an event. This event includes the outcomes {2, 4, 6} from the sample space {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}. It's a specific target we're aiming for.

The Probability Formula: Favorable / Total

  • P(E) = (Number of Favorable Outcomes) / (Total Number of Outcomes)
  • This is the classical definition of probability.
  • It's a ratio, always between 0 and 1.

🎙️ This is the heart of probability! To find the probability of an event (P(E)), we count how many ways our desired event can happen (favorable outcomes) and divide it by the total number of things that *could* happen (total outcomes). It's like picking your favorite sweet from a jar – your sweet is favorable, all sweets are total.

Range of Probability: From 0 to 1

  • Probability is always a number between 0 and 1 (inclusive).
  • 0 means the event is impossible.
  • 1 means the event is certain.

🎙️ Think of probability as a scale. An impossible event, like getting a '7' on a standard die, has a probability of 0. A sure event, like the sun rising tomorrow, has a probability of 1. All other events fall somewhere in between, like a 0.5 for a coin toss.

Probability of a Sure Event: P(E) = 1

  • An event that will definitely happen.
  • Number of favorable outcomes equals total outcomes.
  • Example: The sun will rise in the east.

🎙️ If an event is guaranteed to happen, its probability is 1. For instance, what's the probability that you will eventually take a breath? It's 1! The number of times you breathe is always equal to the total number of times you could breathe in that moment, making it a certainty.

Probability of an Impossible Event: P(E) = 0

  • An event that can never happen.
  • Number of favorable outcomes is zero.
  • Example: A standard die landing on '7'.

🎙️ On the other hand, if an event absolutely cannot happen, its probability is 0. If you roll a standard six-sided die, what's the probability of getting a '7'? There are zero '7's on a die, so the number of favorable outcomes is 0. Thus, P(7) = 0/6 = 0.

Complementary Events: Happening vs. Not Happening

  • An event 'E' and its complement 'not E'.
  • 'not E' means the event E does not occur.
  • Together, they cover all possibilities.

🎙️ Every event has a 'complement'. If 'E' is the event that it rains, then 'not E' (or E-bar) is the event that it *does not* rain. These two events are mutually exclusive and together they cover all possible outcomes of the experiment.

↔️
Opposites Attract

The Complement Rule: P(E) + P(not E) = 1

  • The probability of an event happening PLUS the probability of it not happening is always 1.
  • P(not E) = 1 - P(E).
  • Useful for finding the probability of 'at least one' or 'not' something.

🎙️ This is a very useful property! If you know the probability of an event, you can easily find the probability of it *not* happening by subtracting from 1. For example, if there's a 60% chance of rain (0.6), then there's a 1 - 0.6 = 0.4 (40%) chance of no rain.

Worked Example 5: Not Getting a 6

  • Experiment: Rolling a fair six-sided die once.
  • Event E: Getting a '6'.
  • Event 'not E': Not getting a '6'. Calculate P(not 6).

🎙️ We know the probability of getting a '6' is 1/6. So, using the complement rule, the probability of *not* getting a '6' is 1 minus 1/6, which equals 5/6. This is much faster than counting all the outcomes that are not 6!

11. P(6) = 1/6
22. Using Complement Rule: P(not 6) = 1 - P(6)
33. P(not 6) = 1 - 1/6
44. P(not 6) = 5/6

Common Mistakes to Avoid!

  • Forgetting to simplify fractions.
  • Miscounting total or favorable outcomes.
  • Using percentages instead of fractions/decimals (unless asked).
  • Probability values outside 0 and 1.

🎙️ Students often make small errors that cost marks. Always simplify your probability fractions to their lowest terms. Double-check your counts for total and favorable outcomes. And remember, probability is a number between 0 and 1, so if you get a negative number or something greater than 1, you've made a mistake!

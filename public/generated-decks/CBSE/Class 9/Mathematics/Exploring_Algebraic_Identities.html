What are Algebraic Identities?

  • An equation that is true for ALL values of its variables.
  • Unlike equations, identities don't need to be 'solved'.
  • They are fundamental rules or formulas.

🎙️ An algebraic identity is like a balanced scale that always stays balanced, no matter what numbers you put on it. It's a statement of equality that is universally true for any real numbers substituted for its variables. This distinguishes them from conditional equations which are only true for specific values.

Identity 1: The Square of a Sum (a + b)²

  • Formula: (a + b)² = a² + 2ab + b²
  • Used for squaring a binomial sum.
  • This identity expands to three terms.

🎙️ Our first identity is the square of a sum: (a + b)². This formula states that when you square a binomial where two terms are added, the result is the square of the first term, plus twice the product of the two terms, plus the square of the second term. It's a very common and fundamental identity.

Sum Squared

Deriving (a + b)²

  • We know (a + b)² = (a + b) × (a + b)
  • Multiply each term in the first bracket by each term in the second.
  • Combine like terms to simplify.

🎙️ Let's see how this identity is derived. We can expand (a + b)² by multiplying (a + b) by itself. By applying the distributive property, we multiply each term in the first binomial by each term in the second. After combining the like terms 'ab' and 'ba', we arrive at the identity a² + 2ab + b².

1(a + b)(a + b)
2a(a + b) + b(a + b)
3a² + ab + ba + b²
4a² + 2ab + b²

Real-Life Use: Area of a Square

  • Imagine a square with side length (10 + 2) units.
  • Area = (10 + 2)² = 12² = 144 sq units.
  • Using identity: 10² + 2(10)(2) + 2² = 100 + 40 + 4 = 144.
  • Quickly calculate areas of squares with binomial sides.

🎙️ Identities are not just abstract formulas; they have practical applications. Consider finding the area of a square whose side is expressed as a sum, like (10 + 2) units. Using the identity (a + b)², we can quickly calculate the area by squaring each part and adding the middle term, making the calculation much faster than direct multiplication for larger numbers.

Worked Example: (x + 3)²

  • Identify 'a' and 'b': a = x, b = 3.
  • Apply the identity: (a + b)² = a² + 2ab + b².
  • Substitute values: (x)² + 2(x)(3) + (3)².
  • Simplify: x² + 6x + 9.

🎙️ Let's work through an example to solidify our understanding. Here, 'x' is our 'a' and '3' is our 'b'. We simply substitute these values into the identity (a + b)² = a² + 2ab + b² and then simplify the expression. This method is much more systematic than multiplying (x+3) by (x+3) directly.

Identity 2: The Square of a Difference (a - b)²

  • Formula: (a - b)² = a² - 2ab + b²
  • Used for squaring a binomial difference.
  • Notice the minus sign in the middle term.

🎙️ Next, we have the square of a difference, (a - b)². This identity is very similar to the previous one, but with a crucial difference: the middle term is negative. It expands to a² minus twice the product of 'a' and 'b', plus b². Pay close attention to the sign change.

➖➖
Difference Squared

Deriving (a - b)²

  • We know (a - b)² = (a - b) × (a - b)
  • Multiply each term in the first bracket by each term in the second.
  • Combine like terms to simplify.

🎙️ Just like the previous identity, we can derive (a - b)² by multiplying (a - b) by itself. When distributing, be careful with the negative signs. The product of two negative terms, (-b) * (-b), results in a positive b², while the cross-products give -ab and -ba, which combine to -2ab. This confirms the identity.

1(a - b)(a - b)
2a(a - b) - b(a - b)
3a² - ab - ba + b²
4a² - 2ab + b²

Real-Life Use: Remaining Area

  • A square plot of side 20m has a 2m border removed.
  • Remaining side = (20 - 2)m = 18m.
  • Area = (20 - 2)² = 18² = 324 sq m.
  • Using identity: 20² - 2(20)(2) + 2² = 400 - 80 + 4 = 324.

🎙️ Imagine a situation where you have a square area and a smaller square portion is removed from its side, effectively reducing the side length. For example, a 20m square where 2m is reduced from each side. The identity (a - b)² helps us quickly calculate the area of the remaining square without directly squaring the reduced side, showcasing its efficiency.

Worked Example: (2y - 5)²

  • Identify 'a' and 'b': a = 2y, b = 5.
  • Apply the identity: (a - b)² = a² - 2ab + b².
  • Substitute values: (2y)² - 2(2y)(5) + (5)².
  • Simplify: 4y² - 20y + 25.

🎙️ Let's practice with (2y - 5)². Here, 'a' is 2y and 'b' is 5. We carefully substitute these into the identity, remembering to square the entire term '2y' as (2y)², which becomes 4y². The middle term will be negative, and the last term will be the square of 5. This leads to the simplified expression 4y² - 20y + 25.

Identity 3: Product of Sum and Difference (a + b)(a - b)

  • Formula: (a + b)(a - b) = a² - b²
  • Known as 'Difference of Squares'.
  • Expands to only two terms.

🎙️ Our third identity is the product of a sum and a difference: (a + b)(a - b). This identity is particularly neat because it simplifies to just two terms: the square of 'a' minus the square of 'b'. It's often called the 'Difference of Squares' and is extremely useful for factorization and quick calculations.

✖️✖️
Sum x Difference

Deriving (a + b)(a - b)

  • Multiply each term in the first bracket by each term in the second.
  • Notice that the middle terms cancel each other out.
  • This leaves only the squared terms.

🎙️ Let's derive the 'Difference of Squares' identity. When we expand (a + b)(a - b) using the distributive property, we get a² - ab + ba - b². Notice that the terms -ab and +ab are additive inverses, meaning they cancel each other out. This elegant cancellation leaves us with the simple result of a² - b².

1a(a - b) + b(a - b)
2a² - ab + ba - b²
3a² - ab + ab - b²
4a² - b²

Real-Life Use: Quick Multiplication

  • Calculate 98 × 102 quickly.
  • Rewrite as (100 - 2) × (100 + 2).
  • Using identity: 100² - 2² = 10000 - 4 = 9996.
  • Avoids lengthy traditional multiplication.

🎙️ This identity is fantastic for mental math! Imagine you need to multiply 98 by 102. Instead of direct multiplication, you can rewrite it as (100 - 2)(100 + 2). This perfectly fits the (a + b)(a - b) pattern, allowing you to quickly compute 100² - 2² which is 10000 - 4, giving you 9996 in seconds. It's a powerful trick for simplifying arithmetic.

Worked Example: (3x + 4)(3x - 4)

  • Identify 'a' and 'b': a = 3x, b = 4.
  • Apply the identity: (a + b)(a - b) = a² - b².
  • Substitute values: (3x)² - (4)².
  • Simplify: 9x² - 16.

🎙️ Let's apply the difference of squares identity to (3x + 4)(3x - 4). Here, 'a' is 3x and 'b' is 4. Substituting these into the formula a² - b², we get (3x)² - (4)². Remember to square the entire term 3x, which results in 9x². The final simplified expression is 9x² - 16.

Identity 4: Product of Two Binomials (x + a)(x + b)

  • Formula: (x + a)(x + b) = x² + (a + b)x + ab
  • Useful when the first term is common in both binomials.
  • Expands to three terms.

🎙️ Our final identity for today is (x + a)(x + b). This is a more general form for multiplying two binomials where the first term in each is the same, 'x'. The identity expands to x² plus the sum of 'a' and 'b' times 'x', plus the product of 'a' and 'b'. It's a versatile identity for various expansions.

General Product

Deriving (x + a)(x + b)

  • Multiply each term in the first bracket by each term in the second.
  • Combine the like terms involving 'x'.
  • This reveals the pattern for the identity.

🎙️ To derive (x + a)(x + b), we again use the distributive property. Multiply 'x' by (x + b) and 'a' by (x + b). This gives x² + xb + ax + ab. Notice that 'xb' and 'ax' are like terms, so we can factor out 'x' to get (a + b)x. This neatly leads us to the identity x² + (a + b)x + ab.

1x(x + b) + a(x + b)
2x² + xb + ax + ab
3x² + (b + a)x + ab
4x² + (a + b)x + ab

Real-Life Use: Compound Area

  • Find area of a rectangle with sides (x + 3) and (x + 5).
  • Area = (x + 3)(x + 5).
  • Using identity: x² + (3 + 5)x + (3 × 5) = x² + 8x + 15.
  • Useful for designing layouts or calculating areas with variable dimensions.

🎙️ This identity can be applied when calculating the area of a rectangle whose sides are given as binomials, for example, (x + 3) and (x + 5). Instead of multiplying them out term by term, we can directly apply the identity. This gives us x² + (3 + 5)x + (3 × 5), simplifying to x² + 8x + 15, which is much quicker and more organized.

  • Fundamental for counting, measuring, and ordering.
  • They form the basis of all mathematics.

🎙️ Numbers are like the alphabet of mathematics. They allow us to quantify things, understand 'how many' or 'how much'. Without numbers, simple tasks like telling time or managing money would be impossible.

Rational Numbers (Q)

  • Numbers that can be written as p/q, where p, q are integers and q ≠ 0.
  • Represented by the symbol 'Q'.
  • Includes fractions, terminating decimals, and repeating decimals.

🎙️ Rational numbers are a big leap! They include all integers, plus fractions. Any number that can be expressed as a simple fraction, like half a pizza or a quarter of an hour, is a rational number. The key is that 'q' cannot be zero, as division by zero is undefined.

Irrational Numbers

  • Numbers that cannot be written as p/q.
  • Their decimal expansion is non-terminating and non-repeating.
  • Examples: √2, √3, π (Pi).

🎙️ Now we meet the 'irrational' numbers – they cannot be neatly expressed as a fraction. Their decimal forms go on forever without repeating a pattern. Think of Pi, which is crucial in circles, or square roots of non-perfect squares like √2.

Visualising Irrational Numbers (e.g., √2)

  • Construct a right-angled triangle with sides 1 unit each.
  • The hypotenuse will be √2 units long.
  • Transfer this length to the number line using a compass.

🎙️ For numbers like √2, we use geometry. By constructing a right-angled triangle with legs of 1 unit each, its hypotenuse will be exactly √2 units long according to Pythagoras theorem. We can then use a compass to mark this length on the number line.

Operations on Real Numbers: Add & Subtract

  • Sum or difference of two rational numbers is rational.
  • Sum or difference of two irrational numbers can be rational or irrational.
  • Sum or difference of a rational and an irrational number is irrational.

🎙️ When we add or subtract real numbers, the outcome depends on their type. Rational numbers behave predictably. However, mixing rational and irrational numbers always results in an irrational number. Two irrationals can sometimes simplify to a rational number, like (√2 - √2 = 0).

Operations on Real Numbers: Multiply & Divide

  • Product or quotient of two rational numbers is rational.
  • Product or quotient of two irrational numbers can be rational or irrational.
  • Product or quotient of a non-zero rational and an irrational number is irrational.

🎙️ Similar to addition and subtraction, multiplication and division of real numbers follow specific rules. A non-zero rational number multiplied or divided by an irrational number will always yield an irrational result. For example, 2 * √3 is irrational. But √2 * √2 = 2, which is rational.

  • Simplify: (√5 + √3)(√5 - √3)
  • Using (a+b)(a-b) = a² - b²

🎙️ Let's try an example. This expression looks complex, but it fits a common algebraic identity: (a+b)(a-b) = a² - b². By applying this, the square roots disappear, and we get a simple rational number as the answer. This is a powerful technique for simplifying radical expressions.

Laws of Exponents for Real Numbers

  • Exponents simplify writing repeated multiplication.
  • These laws help us manipulate and solve expressions with powers.
  • Applicable for real number bases and rational exponents.

🎙️ Exponents are a shorthand for repeated multiplication. Instead of writing 2 * 2 * 2 * 2 * 2, we write 2^5. Understanding the laws of exponents is crucial for simplifying complex expressions and solving equations involving powers. We'll explore these laws for real number bases.

📈
Growth with Exponents
18 / 25

Law 1: Product Rule

  • a^m * a^n = a^(m+n)
  • When multiplying powers with the same base, add the exponents.
  • Example: 2³ * 2² = 2^(3+2) = 2⁵ = 32

🎙️ The first law states that if you multiply two powers with the same base, you can simply add their exponents. Think of it as combining groups of identical factors. For instance, (2*2*2) * (2*2) is simply 2 multiplied by itself five times.

⬆️
Adding Powers
19 / 25

Law 2: Power Rule

  • (a^m)^n = a^(mn)
  • When raising a power to another power, multiply the exponents.
  • Example: (3²)³ = 3^(2*3) = 3⁶ = 729

🎙️ This law is about a power being raised to another power. Here, you multiply the exponents. Imagine (3²)³ means (3*3) multiplied by itself three times: (3*3) * (3*3) * (3*3), which is 3 multiplied six times.

💪
Power of a Power
20 / 25

Law 3: Quotient Rule

  • a^m / a^n = a^(m-n) (where a ≠ 0)
  • When dividing powers with the same base, subtract the exponents.
  • Example: 5⁴ / 5² = 5^(4-2) = 5² = 25

🎙️ For division with the same base, we subtract the exponents. This is because division cancels out common factors. For example, (5*5*5*5) / (5*5) leaves us with (5*5), or 5².

➗
Subtracting Powers
21 / 25

Law 4: Product with Same Exponent

  • a^m * b^m = (ab)^m
  • When multiplying powers with different bases but same exponent, multiply the bases and keep the exponent.
  • Example: 2³ * 3³ = (2*3)³ = 6³ = 216

🎙️ This law applies when you have different bases but the same exponent. You can multiply the bases first and then apply the common exponent. For instance, (2*2*2) * (3*3*3) is the same as (2*3) * (2*3) * (2*3), or (6*6*6).

🤝
Joining Bases

Rational Exponents (a^(p/q))

  • a^(p/q) = (q√a)^p = q√(a^p)
  • A rational exponent represents both a root and a power.
  • Example: 8^(2/3) = (³√8)² = 2² = 4

🎙️ Rational exponents bridge the gap between powers and roots. The denominator of the fraction indicates the root, and the numerator indicates the power. So, a^(p/q) means taking the q-th root of 'a' and then raising it to the power 'p'.

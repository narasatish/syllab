The **Horizontal Line**: The X-axis

  • It's a number line extending left and right
  • Positive numbers are to the right of zero
  • Negative numbers are to the left of zero

🎙️ Think of the X-axis as a straight road. Moving right takes you to positive numbers, and moving left takes you to negative numbers. It's our first direction in the plane.

The **Vertical Line**: The Y-axis

  • It's a number line extending up and down
  • Positive numbers are above zero
  • Negative numbers are below zero

🎙️ Now, imagine another road crossing the first one, going up and down. This is our Y-axis. Moving up is positive, and moving down is negative, completing our directional framework.

The **Origin**: Our Central Point (0,0)

  • Where the X-axis and Y-axis intersect
  • Its coordinates are always (0, 0)
  • It's the reference point for all other locations

🎙️ The point where both roads cross is called the Origin. It's like the starting point on a map, from where all other locations are measured. Every journey begins here.

**Quadrants**: Dividing the Plane into Four Regions

  • The axes divide the plane into four parts
  • Numbered counter-clockwise: I, II, III, IV
  • Each quadrant has unique sign combinations for (x, y)

🎙️ These two intersecting lines create four distinct areas, like four rooms in a house. We call these quadrants, and each one has its own special characteristics based on positive and negative values for x and y.

Locating Points: The **Ordered Pair (x, y)**

  • Always written as (x-coordinate, y-coordinate)
  • The x-coordinate tells us horizontal distance from Y-axis
  • The y-coordinate tells us vertical distance from X-axis

🎙️ To find any spot, we need two numbers: how far left or right (x) and how far up or down (y). This pair of numbers is called an ordered pair, and the order matters significantly!

**Distance** from the Y-axis (X-coordinate)

  • The x-coordinate represents the perpendicular distance of a point from the Y-axis
  • It's always a positive value for distance (absolute value)
  • Example: Point (3, 2) is 3 units away from the Y-axis

🎙️ The x-coordinate tells us how far away a point is from the vertical Y-axis. Even if the x-coordinate is negative, the distance itself is always positive, so we take its absolute value.

**Distance** from the X-axis (Y-coordinate)

  • The y-coordinate represents the perpendicular distance of a point from the X-axis
  • It's always a positive value for distance (absolute value)
  • Example: Point (3, 2) is 2 units away from the X-axis

🎙️ Similarly, the y-coordinate tells us how far a point is from the horizontal X-axis. Again, distance is always a positive value, so we consider the absolute value of the y-coordinate.

**Points on the X-axis**: Where Y is Zero

  • Any point on the X-axis has its y-coordinate as 0
  • Its general form is (x, 0)
  • Example: (5, 0) or (-3, 0)

🎙️ If a point is directly on the X-axis, it means it hasn't moved up or down from that axis. Therefore, its y-coordinate must be zero, as it has no vertical displacement.

**Points on the Y-axis**: Where X is Zero

  • Any point on the Y-axis has its x-coordinate as 0
  • Its general form is (0, y)
  • Example: (0, 4) or (0, -2)

🎙️ Conversely, if a point is directly on the Y-axis, it means it hasn't moved left or right from that axis. So, its x-coordinate must be zero, indicating no horizontal displacement.

**Worked Example**: Finding Distances from Axes

  • For point P(4, -5):
  • Distance from Y-axis is |4| = 4 units
  • Distance from X-axis is |-5| = 5 units

🎙️ Remember that distance is always a positive value, so we use the absolute value of the coordinate. This ensures we always get a positive length, regardless of the direction.

1Identify x-coordinate (4)
2Distance from Y-axis = |x| = 4
3Identify y-coordinate (-5)
4Distance from X-axis = |y| = 5

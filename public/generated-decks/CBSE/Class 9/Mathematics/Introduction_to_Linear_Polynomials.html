Introducing Polynomials

  • A special type of algebraic expression.
  • Variables must have non-negative integer powers.
  • No variables in the denominator or under a radical sign.

🎙️ Polynomials are specific types of algebraic expressions with strict rules. The powers of the variables must be whole numbers (0, 1, 2, ...), and you won't find variables in the denominator or inside square roots.

Well-formed Expressions

What is the Degree of a Polynomial?

  • The highest power of the variable in a polynomial.
  • For a single variable, it's the highest exponent.
  • Example: In 4x³ + 2x - 1, the degree is 3.

🎙️ The degree of a polynomial tells us its 'highest' power. It's simply the largest exponent found on any variable in the polynomial. This is a crucial concept for classifying polynomials.

⬆️
Highest Power

Classifying Polynomials by Degree

  • Degree 1: Linear Polynomial
  • Degree 2: Quadratic Polynomial
  • Degree 3: Cubic Polynomial

🎙️ We classify polynomials based on their degree. A polynomial with degree 1 is linear, degree 2 is quadratic, and degree 3 is cubic. This naming convention helps us understand their properties and graphs.

Deep Dive: Linear Polynomials

  • A polynomial with degree 1.
  • Standard form: ax + b, where a ≠ 0.
  • 'a' and 'b' are real numbers.
  • Graphs as a straight line.

🎙️ Our main focus today! A linear polynomial is characterized by its highest power being 1. It always takes the form 'ax + b', where 'a' cannot be zero. When plotted on a graph, it always forms a straight line.

📏
Straight Line

Linear Polynomials in Action: Distance

  • Distance (d) = Speed × Time (t).
  • So, d = 60t, which is a linear polynomial (degree 1).

🎙️ Think about a car moving at a steady speed. The relationship between the distance covered and the time taken is a perfect example of a linear polynomial. For instance, if speed is 60 km/h, distance is 60 times time.

Finding the Zero of a Linear Polynomial

  • The value of the variable that makes the polynomial equal to zero.
  • Also called the 'root' of the polynomial.
  • For ax + b, the zero is found by setting ax + b = 0.

🎙️ The 'zero' of a polynomial is a very important concept. It's the specific value of 'x' that makes the entire polynomial evaluate to zero. It's like finding the exact point where the expression balances out to nothing.

Solving for the Zero

  • Example: Find the zero of P(x) = 2x - 6.
  • Step 1: Set P(x) = 0 => 2x - 6 = 0.
  • Step 2: Isolate x => 2x = 6.
  • Step 3: Solve for x => x = 3.

Avoiding Common Mistakes

  • Confusing degree of variable with number of terms.
  • Incorrectly identifying non-polynomials (e.g., x + 1/x).
  • Errors in solving for the zero (e.g., sign errors).

🎙️ It's easy to make small errors, so let's be aware of common pitfalls. Remember, the degree is the highest power, not the number of terms. Also, ensure you follow algebraic rules carefully when finding a zero.

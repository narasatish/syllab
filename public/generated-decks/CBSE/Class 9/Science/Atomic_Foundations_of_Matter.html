Ancient Ideas about Matter

  • Indian philosopher Maharishi Kanad (500 BC) proposed 'paramanu' (indivisible particles).
  • Greek philosophers Democritus and Leucippus (400 BC) called them 'atomos'.
  • These were philosophical ideas, not based on experiments.

🎙️ Long, long ago, even before science as we know it, people wondered what matter was made of. In India, Maharishi Kanad spoke of 'paramanu', and in Greece, Democritus and Leucippus called them 'atomos', meaning 'uncuttable'. These were brilliant philosophical thoughts, but they weren't proven by experiments.

John Dalton's Atomic Theory - Introduction

  • First scientific theory about atoms (1808).
  • Based on experimental observations.
  • Explained the Laws of Chemical Combination.

🎙️ Now, let's fast forward to the early 19th century. A British scientist named John Dalton proposed the first scientific theory about atoms. His ideas were revolutionary because they were based on experiments and helped explain important laws of chemistry.

Postulate 1: Matter is made of Atoms

  • All matter is composed of very tiny particles called atoms.
  • Atoms are the fundamental building blocks.

🎙️ Dalton's first postulate states that absolutely everything around us, from a tiny dust particle to a giant mountain, is made up of extremely small particles called atoms. Think of them as the smallest LEGO bricks that build everything.

Postulate 2: Atoms are Indivisible

  • Atoms cannot be created or destroyed in a chemical reaction.
  • They are the smallest, uncuttable particles.

🎙️ According to Dalton, atoms are like tiny, hard spheres that cannot be broken down further. He believed you couldn't create new atoms or destroy existing ones during a chemical reaction. They just rearrange themselves.

Postulate 3: Identical Atoms of an Element

  • All atoms of a given element are identical in mass and chemical properties.
  • Example: All gold atoms are exactly alike.

🎙️ Imagine you have a pure gold bar. Dalton said that every single atom in that gold bar is exactly the same. They all have the same mass and behave in the same way chemically. This is a key idea for understanding elements.

Postulate 4: Different Atoms for Different Elements

  • Atoms of different elements have different masses and chemical properties.
  • Example: A gold atom is different from an iron atom.

🎙️ Now, if you compare a gold atom with an iron atom, they are completely different. They have different masses, different sizes, and react differently. This explains why gold and iron have such distinct properties.

Postulate 5: Atoms Combine in Fixed Ratios

  • Atoms combine in the ratio of small whole numbers to form compounds.
  • Example: Water (H₂O) always has 2 hydrogen atoms for 1 oxygen atom.

🎙️ When atoms come together to form compounds, they don't just mix randomly. They combine in very specific, simple whole-number ratios. For instance, water always has two hydrogen atoms for every one oxygen atom, never 1.5 or 2.3!

Postulate 6: Atoms Rearrange in Reactions

  • Atoms are neither created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction.
  • They only rearrange themselves to form new substances.

🎙️ This postulate is very important. During a chemical reaction, atoms don't disappear or pop out of nowhere. Instead, the existing atoms simply break their old connections and form new ones, creating entirely new substances with different properties.

Law of Conservation of Mass

  • Mass can neither be created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction.
  • Total mass of reactants equals total mass of products.
  • Proposed by Antoine Lavoisier.

🎙️ This fundamental law, discovered by Lavoisier, is perfectly explained by Dalton's theory. It means that if you start a reaction with a certain amount of mass, you will end up with the exact same amount of mass in the products, even if they look completely different.

Law of Constant Proportions

  • In a chemical substance, elements are always present in definite proportions by mass.
  • Proposed by Joseph Proust.

🎙️ Another law explained by Dalton's theory is the Law of Constant Proportions. It states that no matter where you get a sample of a pure chemical compound, the elements within it will always be in the same fixed ratio by mass. For example, pure water always has hydrogen and oxygen in a 1:8 mass ratio.

What is an Atom?

  • The smallest particle of an element.
  • May or may not exist independently.
  • Retains the chemical properties of the element.

🎙️ So, what exactly is an atom? It's the smallest unit of an element that still shows all the properties of that element. Some atoms, like noble gases, can exist on their own, while others, like oxygen or hydrogen, prefer to join up with other atoms.

How Small are Atoms?

  • Extremely tiny, much smaller than anything visible to the naked eye.
  • Atomic radius measured in nanometers (1 nm = 10⁻⁹ m).
  • Example: Hydrogen atom radius is 0.037 nm.

🎙️ Atoms are incredibly, unbelievably small. You cannot see them even with the most powerful optical microscope. To give you an idea, a single drop of water contains billions of billions of atoms! We measure their size in nanometers.

Modern Atomic Symbols: Berzelius

  • J.J. Berzelius proposed using one or two letters from the element's name.
  • First letter is always capital, second (if any) is lowercase.
  • This system is still used today.

🎙️ Thankfully, J.J. Berzelius came up with a much simpler and more efficient system. He suggested using the first letter, or the first two letters, of the element's English or Latin name. This system, with a capital first letter and a small second letter, became universally adopted.

Atomic Mass

  • The mass of a single atom of an element.
  • Too small to measure directly in grams.
  • Measured in atomic mass units (amu or u).
  • Defined relative to Carbon-12 atom.

🎙️ We know atoms have mass, but they are incredibly light. It's impractical to measure their mass in grams directly. So, scientists developed a relative scale using 'atomic mass units' or 'u'. One atomic mass unit is defined as exactly one-twelfth the mass of one carbon-12 atom.

How do Atoms Exist?

  • Most atoms do not exist independently.
  • They combine to form molecules and ions.
  • Noble gases are an exception, existing as single atoms.

🎙️ While atoms are fundamental, most of them are quite sociable! They don't like to be alone and prefer to join up with other atoms. They do this by forming either molecules or ions. Only a few special elements, like the noble gases, are happy existing as single, independent atoms.

What is a Molecule?

  • A group of two or more atoms chemically bonded together.
  • Can be atoms of the same element (e.g., O₂) or different elements (e.g., H₂O).
  • The smallest particle of a compound that can exist independently.

🎙️ A molecule is formed when two or more atoms link up chemically. These atoms can be identical, like two oxygen atoms forming an O₂ molecule, or different, like hydrogen and oxygen forming a water molecule, H₂O. Molecules are the smallest units of compounds that can exist on their own.

Atomicity

  • The number of atoms constituting a molecule.
  • Monatomic: 1 atom (e.g., He); Diatomic: 2 atoms (e.g., O₂).
  • Triatomic: 3 atoms (e.g., O₃, H₂O); Polyatomic: More than 3 atoms (e.g., P₄, S₈).

🎙️ Atomicity simply tells us how many atoms are present in one molecule of an element or compound. For example, Helium is monatomic because it exists as single atoms. Oxygen gas is diatomic, having two atoms. Ozone is triatomic, and phosphorus or sulfur can be polyatomic with many atoms.

What is an Ion?

  • An atom or group of atoms that has gained or lost electrons.
  • Carries a net electrical charge (positive or negative).
  • Cation: Positively charged ion (lost electrons, e.g., Na⁺); Anion: Negatively charged ion (gained electrons, e.g., Cl⁻).

🎙️ Sometimes, atoms can gain or lose electrons, which are tiny charged particles. When this happens, the atom develops an electrical charge and becomes an 'ion'. If it loses electrons, it becomes positively charged, a 'cation'. If it gains electrons, it becomes negatively charged, an 'anion'.

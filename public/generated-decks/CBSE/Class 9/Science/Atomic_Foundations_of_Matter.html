Dalton's Atomic Theory: The Beginning

  • Proposed by John Dalton in 1808.
  • Provided a scientific basis for the idea of atoms.
  • Explained the laws of chemical combination.

🎙️ Around 200 years ago, an English chemist named John Dalton put forward a revolutionary idea: the Atomic Theory. This theory was a major breakthrough as it provided a scientific explanation for why chemicals react the way they do, and it introduced the concept of atoms as the fundamental units of matter.

Dalton's Postulates: Atoms are Indivisible

  • Matter consists of indivisible particles called atoms.
  • Atoms can neither be created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction.

🎙️ Dalton's first two postulates are very important. He stated that all matter is made up of tiny, indivisible particles called atoms. Furthermore, he proposed that these atoms cannot be created or destroyed during any chemical reaction, meaning the total amount of matter remains constant.

Dalton's Postulates: Same vs. Different Atoms

  • Atoms of a given element are identical in mass and properties.
  • Atoms of different elements have different masses and properties.

🎙️ Dalton also explained how atoms of different elements behave. He suggested that all atoms of a specific element, like oxygen, are exactly alike in every way, including their mass. However, atoms of different elements, such as carbon and oxygen, are distinct and have different masses and characteristics.

Dalton's Postulates: Combining and Rearranging

  • Atoms combine in small whole number ratios to form compounds.
  • Atoms are only rearranged in a chemical reaction.

🎙️ His theory further stated that when atoms combine to form compounds, they do so in simple, fixed whole-number ratios. This means you won't find half an atom combining! Also, in any chemical reaction, atoms are not changed into other types of atoms; they are simply rearranged to form new substances.

Real-Life Example: Building Blocks

  • Think of LEGO bricks as atoms.
  • Each color/shape of LEGO represents a different element.
  • You can combine them in many ways, but the bricks themselves don't change.

🎙️ To understand Dalton's theory better, imagine LEGO bricks. Each type of brick, say a red 2x4 block or a blue 1x1 block, is like an atom of a different element. You can combine these bricks to build houses, cars, or spaceships – these are like compounds. But the individual LEGO bricks themselves remain unchanged, just rearranged.

Law of Conservation of Mass

  • States that mass can neither be created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction.
  • The total mass of reactants equals the total mass of products.
  • Formulated by Antoine Lavoisier.

🎙️ One of the fundamental laws explained by Dalton's theory is the Law of Conservation of Mass. This law, established by Lavoisier, states that during any chemical change, the total mass of the substances involved remains constant. No mass is gained, and no mass is lost.

Example: Conservation of Mass in Action

  • When you burn a candle, the wax seems to disappear.
  • However, the mass of the wax, oxygen, and products (CO2, water vapor) remains constant.
  • The atoms are just rearranged into new molecules.

🎙️ Consider burning a candle. It might look like the candle wax disappears, but if you could collect all the gases produced, like carbon dioxide and water vapor, and measure their mass along with any leftover soot, you would find that the total mass is exactly the same as the initial mass of the candle wax and the oxygen consumed. The atoms are simply reorganised.

Law of Constant Proportions

  • Also known as the Law of Definite Proportions.
  • States that a chemical compound always contains exactly the same proportion of elements by mass.
  • Given by Joseph Proust.

🎙️ Another cornerstone of chemistry is the Law of Constant Proportions, given by Proust. This law tells us that no matter how a pure chemical compound is prepared or where it comes from, the elements within it will always be present in the same fixed ratio by mass. This is a very powerful concept for understanding compounds.

🎯
Fixed Ratio

Example: Water (H₂O) Always the Same

  • Water (H₂O) always has hydrogen and oxygen in a 1:8 mass ratio.
  • Whether it's river water, tap water, or ice, the ratio is constant.
  • Two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom combine.

🎙️ Let's take water as an example. Every single molecule of water, whether it's from the Ganges river, a tap in your home, or even a cloud, is made up of two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom. This means the mass ratio of hydrogen to oxygen in water will always be 1:8, regardless of its source. This consistency is a direct consequence of the Law of Constant Proportions.

What is an Atom?

  • The smallest particle of an element.
  • May or may not exist independently.
  • Retains the chemical properties of that element.

🎙️ So, what exactly is an atom? An atom is the smallest particle of an element that still has all the chemical properties of that element. It's the basic unit of matter. While some atoms can exist independently, many prefer to combine with other atoms to form more stable structures.

How Small are Atoms?

  • Atoms are incredibly tiny, measured in nanometres (nm).
  • 1 nanometre = 10⁻⁹ metre (one billionth of a metre).
  • We cannot see them even with powerful optical microscopes.

🎙️ To give you an idea of how small atoms are, imagine stacking millions of atoms on top of each other just to see a speck. Their size is measured in nanometres. For example, a hydrogen atom is about 0.1 nanometre in radius. Even a powerful microscope can't show us individual atoms; we need special techniques like scanning tunneling microscopy.

Symbols of Elements: Early Days

  • Dalton used unique symbols for each element.
  • Represented the element and often one atom of it.
  • Examples: ☉ for Hydrogen, ⚫ for Carbon.

🎙️ When Dalton proposed his theory, he also suggested unique symbols for elements. These were pictorial representations, like a circle with a dot for hydrogen or a shaded circle for carbon. While creative, these symbols became cumbersome as more elements were discovered and were hard to draw and remember.

Symbols of Elements: Modern IUPAC System

  • Developed by Berzelius, refined by IUPAC.
  • Uses one or two letters of the English or Latin name.
  • First letter always capital, second letter always small.

🎙️ The system we use today was proposed by Berzelius and is standardized by the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry, or IUPAC. This system uses either the first letter of the element's name, or the first and a second letter, often from its Latin name. The first letter is always capitalized, and the second, if present, is always lowercase.

Atomic Mass: The Weight of an Atom

  • The mass of a single atom of an element.
  • Initially difficult to determine absolute mass.
  • Expressed relative to a standard atom (Carbon-12).

🎙️ Since atoms are so tiny, measuring their absolute mass is extremely difficult. So, scientists developed a system of relative atomic masses. They chose Carbon-12 as a reference point and assigned it a mass of exactly 12 atomic mass units (amu). The mass of other atoms is then compared to this standard.

Molecules: Atoms Joining Hands

  • A molecule is a group of two or more atoms.
  • Held together by chemical forces (bonds).
  • Can be of the same element or different elements.

🎙️ While some atoms can exist independently, many prefer to join together to form molecules. A molecule is essentially a stable group of two or more atoms that are chemically bonded. These atoms can be identical, like in an oxygen molecule (O₂), or different, like in a water molecule (H₂O).

Atomicity: How Many Atoms in a Molecule?

  • The number of atoms present in one molecule of an element or compound.
  • Examples: Monoatomic (He), Diatomic (O₂), Triatomic (O₃), Polyatomic (P₄).

🎙️ Atomicity refers to the number of atoms that make up a single molecule. For instance, noble gases like Helium exist as single atoms, so they are monoatomic. Oxygen gas exists as O₂, making it diatomic. Ozone (O₃) is triatomic, and phosphorus (P₄) is polyatomic, having more than three atoms.

Ions: Charged Atoms

  • An atom or group of atoms that has a net electrical charge.
  • Formed by gaining or losing electrons.
  • Cations are positive (+), Anions are negative (-).

🎙️ Sometimes, atoms can gain or lose electrons, which are negatively charged particles. When this happens, they become electrically charged particles called ions. If an atom loses electrons, it forms a positively charged ion called a cation. If it gains electrons, it forms a negatively charged ion called an anion.

Writing Chemical Formulae: The Language of Chemistry

🎙️ Chemical formulae are like a shorthand notation for compounds. They tell us which elements are present and in what proportion. We use the symbols of elements and subscripts to indicate the number of atoms. There's a systematic way to write these formulae, often using the concept of valency.

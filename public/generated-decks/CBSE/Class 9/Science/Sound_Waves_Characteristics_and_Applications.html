Sound: Like Ripples in a Pond

  • Drop a stone in a pond: ripples spread outwards.
  • Sound is similar: a disturbance that spreads.
  • It's a form of energy that causes the sensation of hearing.

🎙️ Just as a stone creates ripples that travel through water, sound is a disturbance that travels through a medium, carrying energy, not matter, from one place to another.

Sound Travels as a Wave

  • Sound is produced by vibrating objects.
  • These vibrations create disturbances in the medium.
  • The disturbance travels as a wave, transferring energy.

🎙️ When you strike a bell, it vibrates. These vibrations push and pull the air molecules around it, creating regions of compression and rarefaction that travel outwards. This is how sound energy moves.

Sound Needs a Medium to Travel

  • Sound cannot travel through a vacuum.
  • It requires a material medium (solid, liquid, or gas).
  • The particles of the medium vibrate to transmit sound.

🎙️ Imagine trying to talk on the Moon without a special suit – you couldn't hear anything! That's because space is a vacuum. Sound needs particles to vibrate and pass on the energy. It travels fastest in solids, then liquids, and slowest in gases.

Sound is a Longitudinal Wave

  • Particles vibrate parallel to wave direction.
  • Creates compressions (high pressure) and rarefactions (low pressure).
  • Think of a Slinky spring pushed and pulled.

🎙️ Unlike ocean waves where water moves up and down while the wave moves forward, in a longitudinal wave, the particles of the medium oscillate back and forth in the *same direction* as the wave is travelling.

Wavelength (λ): The Length of a Wave

  • Distance between two consecutive compressions or rarefactions.
  • Represented by the Greek letter 'lambda' (λ).
  • Measured in metres (m).

🎙️ Imagine a series of crests and troughs in a water wave. Wavelength is the distance from one crest to the next. For sound, it's the distance between two successive high-pressure regions or two successive low-pressure regions.

Measuring Wavelength

Amplitude (A): The Size of the Vibration

  • Maximum displacement of particles from their mean position.
  • Determines the loudness of the sound.
  • Larger amplitude means louder sound.

🎙️ Think of a swinging pendulum. How far it swings from the centre is its amplitude. For sound, it's how much the air pressure changes from its normal level. A bigger swing means more energy and a louder sound.

↕️
Height of Wave

Frequency (f): How Often it Vibrates

  • Number of oscillations (compressions/rarefactions) per second.
  • Determines the pitch of the sound.
  • Measured in Hertz (Hz).

🎙️ If a drum vibrates 100 times in one second, its frequency is 100 Hz. A high frequency sound means many vibrations per second, resulting in a high-pitched sound, like a whistle.

Time Period (T): Time for One Vibration

  • Time taken for one complete oscillation.
  • It is the reciprocal of frequency: T = 1/f.
  • Measured in seconds (s).

🎙️ If a wave has a frequency of 10 Hz, it completes 10 oscillations in one second. Therefore, one oscillation takes 1/10th of a second. Time period and frequency are inversely related.

⏰
Time for One Cycle
13 / 30

Speed of Sound (v): How Fast it Travels

  • Distance travelled by a sound wave per unit time.
  • Related to wavelength and frequency: v = λ × f.
  • Speed of sound is constant in a given medium.

🎙️ Just like any other speed, it's distance over time. For waves, this translates to wavelength multiplied by frequency. Remember, the speed of sound changes with the medium and temperature, but not with its own frequency or amplitude.

Pitch: The 'Shrillness' or 'Flatness'

  • Determined by frequency: High frequency = High pitch (shrill).
  • Low frequency = Low pitch (flat/deep).
  • Example: A baby's cry (high pitch) vs. a man's voice (low pitch).

🎙️ Think of a flute playing a high note versus a drum producing a deep thud. The flute produces sound waves with a higher frequency, hence a higher pitch. The drum produces lower frequency waves.

Loudness: The 'Intensity' of Sound

  • Determined by amplitude: Large amplitude = Loud sound.
  • Small amplitude = Soft sound.
  • Example: Whispering (low amplitude) vs. shouting (high amplitude).

🎙️ Loudness depends on the energy carried by the sound wave. A larger amplitude means more energy and thus a louder sound. We measure loudness in decibels (dB).

Quality (Timbre): The Uniqueness of Sound

Echo: The Reflected Sound

  • Perception of a distinct sound due to reflection.
  • Minimum time delay of 0.1 seconds needed for distinct echo.
  • Minimum distance from reflector ≈ 17.2 meters (at 22°C).

🎙️ For us to hear an echo, the reflected sound must reach our ears at least 0.1 seconds after the original sound. This time difference, combined with the speed of sound, determines the minimum distance to the reflecting surface.

Reverberation: Lingering Sound

  • Multiple reflections causing a sound to persist.
  • Occurs in large enclosed spaces like auditoriums.
  • Excessive reverberation can make speech unclear.

🎙️ If the reflections happen too quickly and continuously, the original sound and the reflected sounds merge, creating a prolonged sound effect called reverberation. This is why concert halls use sound-absorbing materials.

SONAR: Sound Navigation And Ranging

  • Uses ultrasonic waves to detect objects underwater.
  • Transmitter sends waves, receiver detects reflected waves.
  • Calculates distance, direction, and speed of underwater objects.

🎙️ SONAR is like using sound to 'see' underwater. Ships use it to find fish, map the ocean floor, and detect submarines. Bats use a similar principle, called echolocation, to navigate in the dark.

Ultrasound: High Frequency, High Impact

  • Sound waves with frequencies above 20 kHz (inaudible to humans).
  • Used for medical imaging (e.g., prenatal scans).
  • Also used for cleaning delicate objects and detecting flaws in metals.

🎙️ Ultrasound is extremely versatile. In medicine, it's safe for imaging unborn babies. Industrially, its high energy can dislodge dirt from intricate parts or reveal tiny cracks in metal structures without damaging them.

Common Mistakes in Sound Waves

  • Confusing pitch with loudness (frequency vs. amplitude).
  • Believing sound can travel in a vacuum.
  • Mixing up longitudinal and transverse waves.
  • Incorrectly applying the echo formula (distance vs. 2x distance).

🎙️ Students often mix up pitch and loudness. Remember, a high-pitched sound can be soft, and a low-pitched sound can be loud. Also, always remember that sound needs a medium; it's not like light.

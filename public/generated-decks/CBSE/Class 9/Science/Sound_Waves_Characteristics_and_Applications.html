What is Sound?

  • A form of energy that produces the sensation of hearing.
  • Produced by vibrations.
  • Requires a medium to travel.

🎙️ Sound is essentially a type of energy that our ears can detect, allowing us to hear. It's always created when something vibrates, meaning it moves back and forth rapidly. Think about a guitar string or a drum skin.

How Sound Travels: Wave Propagation

  • Vibrations create disturbances in the medium.
  • Particles transmit energy, not themselves.
  • Travels as a wave.

🎙️ When a source vibrates, it pushes the nearby particles of the medium. These particles then push their neighbours, and so on. The energy travels forward, but the particles themselves just oscillate around their fixed positions, like a domino effect.

Sound Needs a Medium

  • Sound cannot travel through a vacuum.
  • Requires a material medium: solid, liquid, or gas.

🎙️ Unlike light, sound needs something to travel through. It can't travel in empty space, like the vacuum of outer space. It needs a medium, which can be a solid, a liquid, or a gas, to transmit its vibrations.

Wavelength (λ)

  • Distance between two consecutive compressions or rarefactions.
  • Unit: metre (m).
  • Determines the 'length' of the wave.

🎙️ Imagine a wave as a series of crests and troughs. Wavelength is simply the distance from one crest to the next, or one trough to the next. It's like the length of one complete 'cycle' of the wave.

〰️
Wave Length

Amplitude (A)

  • Maximum displacement of particles from their mean position.
  • Related to the loudness of sound.
  • Larger amplitude = louder sound.

🎙️ Amplitude refers to how 'tall' a wave is, or how much the particles of the medium move from their resting position. A bigger amplitude means the particles are vibrating more vigorously, which results in a louder sound.

↕️
Wave Height

Frequency (f)

  • Number of oscillations per unit time.
  • Unit: Hertz (Hz).
  • Related to the pitch of sound (high/low).

🎙️ Frequency tells us how many complete waves pass a point in one second. If many waves pass quickly, the frequency is high. High frequency sounds have a high pitch, like a whistle, while low frequency sounds have a low pitch, like a drum.

Time Period (T)

  • Time taken for one complete oscillation.
  • Unit: second (s).
  • Inverse of frequency: T = 1/f.

🎙️ Time period is simply the time it takes for one full wave to complete its journey. It's directly related to frequency; if a wave has a high frequency, it means many waves pass quickly, so the time period for each wave is short.

Wave Velocity (v)

  • Speed at which the sound wave travels.
  • Depends on the medium.
  • Formula: Velocity (v) = Frequency (f) × Wavelength (λ).

🎙️ Wave velocity is how fast the sound energy moves through the medium. It's a crucial characteristic that links frequency and wavelength. This formula, v = f × λ, is fundamental for solving problems related to sound waves.

Differentiating Loudness and Pitch

  • Loudness depends on amplitude (energy).
  • Pitch depends on frequency (rate of vibration).
  • High amplitude = loud sound; High frequency = high pitch.

🎙️ These two terms are often confused. Remember, loudness is about how intense or powerful the sound feels, linked to its amplitude. Pitch is about how high or low a sound is, determined by its frequency.

Speed of Sound in Different Media

  • Sound travels fastest in solids, then liquids, slowest in gases.
  • Particles are closer in solids, allowing faster energy transfer.
  • Example: Sound travels faster through a railway track than air.

🎙️ The speed of sound varies greatly depending on the medium. In solids, particles are tightly packed, so vibrations are transmitted very efficiently. In gases, particles are far apart, making transmission slower.

Reflection of Sound: Echoes

  • Sound waves bounce off a surface, just like light.
  • An echo is the repetition of sound due to reflection.
  • Requires a minimum distance from the reflecting surface.

🎙️ When sound waves hit a hard surface, they don't just stop; they bounce back. This phenomenon is called reflection. An echo is a common example, where you hear your own sound again after it reflects off a distant wall or mountain.

Applications of Sound Reflection

🎙️ Reflection of sound isn't just for echoes; it has many practical uses. Megaphones use reflection to amplify and direct your voice, while stethoscopes use multiple reflections to guide faint body sounds directly to the doctor's ears.

Reverberation

  • Multiple reflections of sound within an enclosed space.
  • Can cause sound to persist for a longer time.
  • Often controlled in concert halls using sound-absorbing materials.

🎙️ Reverberation is like many tiny echoes happening very quickly in a room. If a room has hard, reflective surfaces, sound can bounce around for a while, making it difficult to understand speech. That's why concert halls use special materials to absorb sound.

Range of Human Hearing

  • Audible Range: 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz (20 kHz).
  • Infrasound: Below 20 Hz (e.g., elephants, earthquakes).
  • Ultrasound: Above 20,000 Hz (e.g., bats, dolphins, medical imaging).

🎙️ Our ears can only detect a specific range of frequencies. Sounds below 20 Hz are called infrasound, and those above 20,000 Hz are ultrasound. Many animals can hear sounds outside our range, which is fascinating.

Applications of Ultrasound

  • Medical imaging (sonography) to see inside the body.
  • Cleaning delicate objects (jewellery, electronic components).

🎙️ Ultrasound has incredible applications due to its high frequency and short wavelength. In medicine, it's used to create images of organs and babies. Industrially, it cleans intricate parts and finds hidden defects in materials.

SONAR: Sound Navigation And Ranging

  • Uses ultrasonic waves to measure distance, direction, and speed of underwater objects.
  • A transducer sends and receives ultrasound pulses.
  • Calculates distance using time taken for echo and speed of sound in water.

🎙️ SONAR is a powerful technology that works on the principle of ultrasound reflection. It's used by ships and submarines to detect objects underwater, map the seabed, and even locate schools of fish. It's like sending out a sound 'ping' and listening for the echo.

Worked Example: Wave Calculation

  • A sound wave has a frequency of 2 kHz and a wavelength of 35 cm.
  • Calculate its speed.
  • Solution: f = 2000 Hz, λ = 0.35 m. v = f × λ = 2000 × 0.35 = 700 m/s.

🎙️ Let's apply the formula we learned. Remember to convert units to SI units before calculating. Frequency in Hertz and wavelength in meters will give you speed in meters per second. This is a common type of problem.

Worked Example: Echo Distance

  • A person hears an echo from a cliff after 3 seconds.
  • If the speed of sound in air is 340 m/s, how far away is the cliff?
  • Solution: Total distance = v × t = 340 × 3 = 1020 m. Distance to cliff = 1020 / 2 = 510 m.

🎙️ For echo problems, remember that the sound travels to the reflecting surface and back. So, the total distance covered is twice the distance to the object. Always divide the total distance by two to find the actual distance to the source of reflection.

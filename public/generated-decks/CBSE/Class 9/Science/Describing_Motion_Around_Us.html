What is Motion?

  • An object is in motion if its position changes with time.
  • Motion is relative to an observer or a reference point.

🎙️ Simply put, if something changes where it is over time, it's in motion. But motion isn't absolute; it always depends on who is observing it and from where. We need a fixed point to judge if something is moving.

The Importance of a Reference Point

  • A reference point is a fixed place or object.
  • It helps us determine if another object is moving or at rest.
  • Think of it as your starting point for observation.

🎙️ Imagine you're on a train. Are you moving? Yes, compared to the ground outside. But are you moving compared to the person sitting next to you? No. The ground or the person next to you acts as a reference point. A tree or a building often serves as a good reference point.

Rest vs. Motion: A Relative Concept

  • An object is at rest if its position does not change relative to a reference point.
  • An object is in motion if its position changes relative to a reference point.

🎙️ So, rest and motion are not absolute. A person sitting in a moving bus is at rest with respect to other passengers but in motion with respect to a person standing outside the bus. This relativity is a fundamental concept in physics.

Uniform Motion

  • An object covers equal distances in equal intervals of time.
  • Its speed remains constant throughout the journey.
  • Example: A car moving on a straight, empty highway at a constant speed.

🎙️ In uniform motion, an object maintains a steady pace. If it travels 10 meters in the first second, it will travel another 10 meters in the next second, and so on. This means there's no change in its speed or direction.

Non-Uniform Motion

  • An object covers unequal distances in equal intervals of time.
  • Its speed changes during the journey.
  • Example: A car moving in heavy traffic, speeding up and slowing down.

🎙️ Most of the motion we observe in daily life is non-uniform. Think about walking in a crowded market or riding a bicycle uphill. Your speed is constantly changing, meaning you cover different distances in the same time intervals.

Uniform vs. Non-Uniform Motion: A Comparison

  • Uniform: Constant speed, straight path.

🎙️ Imagine a train on a clear track versus a car in a city. The train often exhibits uniform motion for long stretches, while the car in the city is constantly in non-uniform motion due to stops, turns, and varying traffic.

Describing Motion: Distance

  • Distance is the total path length covered by an object.
  • It is a scalar quantity, meaning it only has magnitude (a value).
  • Unit: metre (m).

🎙️ When you walk from your home to school, the total length of the path you take is the distance. It doesn't matter if you took detours or walked in circles; it's simply the total ground covered. We don't care about the direction here.

Describing Motion: Displacement

  • Displacement is the shortest straight-line distance between the initial and final positions.
  • It is a vector quantity, meaning it has both magnitude and direction.
  • Unit: metre (m).

🎙️ Displacement is more about 'how far are you from where you started?' and 'in what direction?'. If you walk 5m East and then 5m West, your distance is 10m, but your displacement is 0m because you're back at the start.

Distance vs. Displacement: Key Differences

  • Distance is scalar, Displacement is vector.
  • Distance can never be zero or negative, Displacement can be zero, positive, or negative.
  • Distance is path-dependent, Displacement is path-independent.

🎙️ These differences are crucial. Remember, distance tells you 'how much ground was covered', while displacement tells you 'how far and in what direction is the final position from the initial position'.

Describing Motion: Speed

  • Speed is the rate at which an object covers distance.
  • Formula: Speed = Distance / Time.
  • It is a scalar quantity. Unit: metre per second (m/s).

🎙️ Speed tells us how fast an object is moving. If a car covers a large distance in a short amount of time, its speed is high. It only cares about the magnitude of movement, not the direction.

Describing Motion: Velocity

  • Velocity is the rate of change of displacement.
  • Formula: Velocity = Displacement / Time.
  • It is a vector quantity, having both magnitude (speed) and direction. Unit: metre per second (m/s).

🎙️ Velocity is more specific than speed. It not only tells you how fast you're going but also in which direction. For example, '50 km/h North' is a velocity, while '50 km/h' is just a speed.

Average Speed

  • When an object travels at varying speeds, we calculate average speed.
  • Formula: Average Speed = Total Distance / Total Time.
  • Used for non-uniform motion over a duration.

🎙️ In real-world scenarios, objects rarely move at a constant speed. Average speed helps us describe the overall rate of motion during a journey, even if there were stops or changes in speed.

⏱️

Average Velocity

  • For non-uniform motion, average velocity is the total displacement divided by total time.
  • Formula: Average Velocity = Total Displacement / Total Time.
  • Can also be calculated as (Initial Velocity + Final Velocity) / 2 for uniform acceleration.

🎙️ Just like average speed, average velocity accounts for changes in motion. It's crucial because it considers the overall change in position and direction from start to finish, not just the path taken.

Worked Example: Calculating Average Speed

  • A car travels 100 km in 2 hours and then another 50 km in 1 hour.
  • Calculate its average speed.

🎙️ This is a straightforward application of the average speed formula. Always remember to add up all the distances and all the times separately before dividing them.

1Total Distance = 100 km + 50 km = 150 km
2Total Time = 2 hours + 1 hour = 3 hours
3Average Speed = 150 km / 3 hours = 50 km/h

Describing Motion: Acceleration

  • Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity.
  • Formula: Acceleration = (Final Velocity - Initial Velocity) / Time.
  • It is a vector quantity. Unit: metre per second squared (m/s²).

🎙️ Acceleration tells us how quickly an object's velocity is changing. This change can be an increase in speed, a decrease in speed (deceleration), or even a change in direction while maintaining constant speed.

Types of Acceleration

  • Positive: Velocity increases (speeding up).
  • Negative (Deceleration): Velocity decreases (slowing down).
  • Zero: Velocity is constant (uniform motion).

🎙️ When a car speeds up, it has positive acceleration. When it brakes, it experiences negative acceleration or deceleration. If it cruises at a constant speed in a straight line, its acceleration is zero.

Worked Example: Calculating Acceleration

  • A bus increases its speed from 36 km/h to 72 km/h in 10 seconds.
  • Calculate its acceleration.

🎙️ Remember to always convert units to a consistent system (SI units are preferred) before plugging them into formulas. Here, converting km/h to m/s is the first crucial step.

1Convert speeds to m/s: 36 km/h = 10 m/s; 72 km/h = 20 m/s
2Initial velocity (u) = 10 m/s, Final velocity (v) = 20 m/s, Time (t) = 10 s
3Acceleration (a) = (v - u) / t = (20 - 10) / 10 = 1 m/s²

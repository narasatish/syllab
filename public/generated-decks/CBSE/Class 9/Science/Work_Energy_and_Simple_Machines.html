Work: Definition & Conditions (W = F × s)

  • Work is done when a force causes displacement.
  • Force (F) must act on an object.
  • Object must be displaced (s) in force's direction.

🎙️ Scientifically, work is defined as the product of the force applied and the displacement in the direction of the force. For work to be done, two main conditions must be met: a force must act on an object, and the object must move some distance in the direction of the applied force.

Units of Work: The Joule (J)

  • The SI unit of work is the Joule (J).
  • Named after James Prescott Joule.
  • 1 Joule = 1 Newton × 1 meter.

🎙️ The standard international unit for work is the Joule, abbreviated as 'J'. It is named after the English physicist James Prescott Joule, who made significant contributions to the study of heat and its relation to mechanical work. One Joule is the work done when a force of 1 Newton moves an object by 1 meter.

Types of Work: Positive, Negative, Zero

  • Positive: Force and displacement in same direction.
  • Negative: Force and displacement in opposite directions.
  • Zero: No displacement or force perpendicular to displacement.

🎙️ Work can be positive, negative, or zero. Positive work occurs when the force helps the motion. Negative work happens when the force opposes motion, like friction. Zero work is when there's no movement, or the force acts perpendicular to the direction of motion.

Kinetic Energy: The Energy of Motion

  • Energy possessed by an object due to its motion.
  • Depends on mass (m) and velocity (v).
  • Formula: KE = ½ mv².

🎙️ Any object that is moving possesses kinetic energy. The amount of kinetic energy depends on two factors: its mass and its velocity. The faster an object moves and the more massive it is, the more kinetic energy it has. The formula KE = ½ mv² shows velocity has a squared effect.

Worked Example: A Bowled Cricket Ball

  • Mass of ball: 0.16 kg.
  • Velocity: 40 m/s.
  • Calculate its kinetic energy.

🎙️ Let's calculate the kinetic energy of a cricket ball. Given mass (m) = 0.16 kg and velocity (v) = 40 m/s. Using KE = ½ mv², we get KE = ½ × 0.16 kg × (40 m/s)² = ½ × 0.16 × 1600 = 0.08 × 1600 = 128 Joules. That's a lot of energy!

Potential Energy: Stored for Later

  • Energy stored in an object due to its position or state.
  • Gravitational Potential Energy (due to height).
  • Elastic Potential Energy (e.g., stretched spring).

🎙️ Potential energy is the energy stored in an object, waiting to be released. A common type is gravitational potential energy, which an object has due to its height above a reference point. A stretched rubber band or a compressed spring also stores elastic potential energy.

Calculating Gravitational Potential Energy

  • Depends on mass (m), gravity (g), and height (h).
  • Formula: PE = mgh.
  • 'g' is acceleration due to gravity (approx. 9.8 m/s²).

🎙️ For gravitational potential energy (PE), the formula is PE = mgh, where 'm' is the mass of the object, 'g' is the acceleration due to gravity (approximately 9.8 m/s² on Earth), and 'h' is its height above a chosen reference level. The higher the object, the more potential energy it stores.

Worked Example: Book on a Shelf

  • Mass of book: 2 kg.
  • Height of shelf: 1.5 m.
  • Calculate its potential energy (g=9.8 m/s²).

🎙️ Let's find the potential energy of a book. Given mass (m) = 2 kg, height (h) = 1.5 m, and g = 9.8 m/s². Using PE = mgh, we get PE = 2 kg × 9.8 m/s² × 1.5 m = 29.4 Joules. This energy could be converted to kinetic energy if the book falls.

Law of Conservation of Energy

  • Energy cannot be created or destroyed.
  • It only transforms from one form to another.
  • Total energy in an isolated system remains constant.

🎙️ This fundamental law states that energy can neither be created nor destroyed; it can only be transformed from one form to another. For example, in a swinging pendulum, potential energy at the highest point converts to kinetic energy at the lowest point, and back again, but the total mechanical energy remains constant (ignoring air resistance).

Calculating Power: Formula & Unit

  • Formula: Power (P) = Work (W) / time (t).
  • The SI unit of power is the Watt (W).
  • 1 Watt = 1 Joule per second (J/s).

🎙️ The formula for power (P) is P = Work (W) / time (t). The SI unit of power is the Watt (W), named after James Watt, known for his improvements to the steam engine. One Watt is equivalent to one Joule of work done per second. A larger unit, kilowatt-hour (kWh), is used commercially.

Worked Example: Boy Climbing Stairs

  • Boy's mass: 50 kg.
  • Climbs 40 steps, each 15 cm high.
  • Time taken: 10 seconds (g=9.8 m/s²).

🎙️ First, calculate total height: 40 steps × 0.15 m/step = 6 m. Work done (W) = mgh = 50 kg × 9.8 m/s² × 6 m = 2940 J. Now, Power (P) = W/t = 2940 J / 10 s = 294 Watts. This shows the power output of the boy.

Common Mistakes to Avoid!

  • Confusing 'effort' with 'scientific work'.
  • Mixing up Energy (capacity) with Power (rate).
  • Forgetting units in numerical problems.

🎙️ Students often confuse 'work' with 'effort'. Remember, no displacement means no scientific work. Also, don't mix up energy, which is the capacity to do work, with power, which is the rate of doing work. Always write units for your answers in numerical problems to avoid losing marks!

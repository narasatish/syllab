What is a Force?

  • A push or a pull on an object.
  • It can be contact or non-contact.
  • Measured in Newtons (N).

🎙️ Think about your daily activities. When you open a door, kick a ball, or even lift your school bag, you are applying a force. It's essentially an interaction that can cause an object to change its state.

➡️

Effects of Force: Changing Motion

  • A force can start motion in a stationary object.
  • It can stop a moving object.
  • It can change the speed of a moving object.
  • It can change the direction of a moving object.

🎙️ Imagine a football lying still on the ground. A kick (force) makes it move. If a ball is rolling, you can apply a force to stop it, speed it up, or even make it go in a different direction. These are all changes in its state of motion.

Effects of Force: Changing Shape or Size

  • Forces can deform objects.
  • This change can be temporary or permanent.
  • Example: Squeezing a sponge or clay.

🎙️ Forces don't just affect motion; they can also alter an object's physical form. When you squeeze a sponge, it changes shape temporarily. If you press on a lump of clay, its shape changes permanently. This is another important effect of force.

Balanced Forces

  • Equal and opposite forces acting on an object.
  • The net force is zero.
  • Objects remain at rest or continue constant motion.

🎙️ When forces acting on an object cancel each other out, we call them balanced forces. If an object is already stationary, it will stay stationary. If it's moving at a constant speed in a straight line, it will continue to do so.

🤝🤝
Equal pull

Unbalanced Forces

  • Forces acting on an object are not equal and opposite.
  • The net force is not zero.
  • Causes a change in an object's state of motion.

🎙️ For an object to accelerate, decelerate, or change direction, an unbalanced force must be acting on it. This means there's a net force in one direction, causing a change in the object's velocity.

💪
Stronger push

Real-life Example: Tug-of-War

  • When teams pull with equal force, the rope doesn't move (balanced).
  • When one team pulls harder, the rope moves (unbalanced).
  • The net force determines the outcome.

🎙️ Tug-of-war is a perfect example. If both teams pull with the same strength, the rope stays in the middle – balanced forces. But as soon as one team applies more force, they win, because the forces become unbalanced, and the rope moves.

Newton's First Law of Motion: Inertia

  • An object at rest stays at rest, and an object in motion stays in motion with the same speed and in the same direction, unless acted upon by an unbalanced force.
  • Also known as the Law of Inertia.
  • Inertia is the tendency of an object to resist changes in its state of motion.

🎙️ This law basically tells us that objects are 'lazy' and prefer to keep doing whatever they're already doing. If it's still, it wants to stay still. If it's moving, it wants to keep moving at that same speed and direction.

Inertia in Action

  • When a bus suddenly starts, passengers fall backward.
  • When a bus suddenly brakes, passengers lurch forward.
  • Shaking a tree to make fruits fall.

🎙️ These are common experiences. When the bus starts, your body tries to stay at rest. When it stops, your body tries to continue moving forward. Shaking a tree provides an unbalanced force on the branch, but the fruit's inertia makes it want to stay put, causing it to detach.

Mass and Inertia

  • Mass is a measure of an object's inertia.
  • More mass means more inertia.
  • Harder to change the state of motion for heavier objects.

🎙️ Think about pushing a toy car versus pushing a real car. Which one is harder to get moving or stop? The real car has much more mass, and therefore much more inertia, making it more resistant to changes in its motion.

Momentum

  • The quantity of motion an object has.
  • Defined as the product of mass and velocity (p = mv).
  • Vector quantity, having both magnitude and direction.
  • SI unit: kilogram-meter per second (kg m/s).

🎙️ Momentum tells us how much 'oomph' an object has. A heavy object moving slowly can have the same momentum as a light object moving very fast. It's a crucial concept in understanding collisions and interactions.

🎙️ Consider the impact of a cricket ball versus a tennis ball hitting you. The cricket ball, being heavier, has significantly more momentum at the same speed, leading to a much greater impact force due to its higher inertia.

Newton's Second Law of Motion

  • The rate of change of momentum of an object is directly proportional to the applied unbalanced force in the direction of the force.
  • Mathematically expressed as F = ma (Force = mass × acceleration).
  • A larger force produces a larger acceleration.

🎙️ This law is one of the most important in physics. It quantifies the relationship between force, mass, and acceleration. It tells us that to make something accelerate, you need to apply a force, and the heavier it is, the more force you'll need.

🚀

Understanding F = ma

  • Force is directly proportional to acceleration (F ∝ a, if mass is constant).
  • Acceleration is inversely proportional to mass (a ∝ 1/m, if force is constant).
  • This means more mass requires more force for the same acceleration.

🎙️ This slide breaks down the famous formula. If you push something harder (more force), it speeds up faster (more acceleration). But if you're pushing a heavier object with the same force, it won't speed up as much.

Worked Example: Calculating Force

  • Problem: A car of mass 1000 kg accelerates at 2 m/s².
  • Calculate the force required.
  • Solution: F = ma = 1000 kg × 2 m/s² = 2000 N.

🎙️ Here's a straightforward application of F=ma. We simply plug in the given values for mass and acceleration to find the force. Remember to always use the correct SI units for your calculations.

Newton's Third Law of Motion

  • To every action, there is always an equal and opposite reaction.
  • Forces always occur in pairs.
  • These action-reaction forces act on different objects.

🎙️ This law is often misunderstood. It states that forces never exist alone. If you push something, that something pushes back on you with the exact same strength, but in the opposite direction. Crucially, these forces act on different objects.

↔️
Action-Reaction
18 / 25

Third Law in Action

  • Walking: You push the ground backward, the ground pushes you forward.
  • Rocket Propulsion: Rocket expels gases downward, gases push rocket upward.
  • Swimming: You push water backward, water pushes you forward.

🎙️ Every movement we make involves action-reaction pairs. When you walk, it's not just you pushing the ground; the ground is actively pushing back on you, enabling your movement. Rockets work on the same principle, pushing exhaust gases down to propel themselves up.

Real-life Example: Recoil of a Gun

  • When a gun fires a bullet, the gun exerts a forward force on the bullet (action).
  • The bullet exerts an equal and opposite backward force on the gun (reaction).
  • This backward force is called recoil.

🎙️ This is a classic example. The powerful explosion pushes the bullet forward, but simultaneously, the bullet pushes the gun backward. This is why a shooter feels a 'kick' or recoil when firing a weapon.

Conservation of Momentum

  • In an isolated system, the total momentum remains constant.
  • Momentum is neither created nor destroyed.
  • Applies to collisions and explosions.
  • Total momentum before interaction = Total momentum after interaction.

🎙️ This principle is extremely powerful. It states that even if objects collide or break apart, the total momentum of the system, if no external forces are acting, will always stay the same. It's a fundamental conservation law in physics.

Conservation of Momentum in Action

  • Consider two trolleys colliding.
  • Momentum lost by one trolley is gained by the other.
  • The sum of their momenta before and after collision is the same.

🎙️ Imagine two billiard balls hitting each other. Even though their individual speeds and directions change dramatically, the total momentum of the two-ball system remains constant before and after the collision, assuming no friction.

Chapter Recap: Key Concepts

  • Force: Push or pull, causes change in motion/shape.
  • Balanced/Unbalanced Forces: Net force zero vs non-zero.
  • Newton's First Law (Inertia): Resistance to change in motion.
  • Newton's Second Law (F=ma): Force, mass, and acceleration.
  • Newton's Third Law: Action-reaction pairs.
  • Conservation of Momentum: Total momentum constant in isolated systems.

🎙️ We've covered a lot of ground today! Remember, force is the interaction, inertia is the resistance, F=ma tells us how they relate, and action-reaction pairs are everywhere. The conservation of momentum ties it all together for systems.

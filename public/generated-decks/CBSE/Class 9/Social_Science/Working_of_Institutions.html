🎙️ To understand how institutions work, let's look at a significant event in Indian history: the Mandal Commission case. This decision had a profound impact on Indian society and perfectly illustrates the interplay between different government bodies.

Mandal Commission Report (1980): The Proposal

  • Second Backward Classes Commission, headed by B.P. Mandal.
  • Tasked to identify socially and educationally backward classes.
  • Recommended 27% reservation for SEBCs in central government jobs.

🎙️ The Mandal Commission was set up in 1979 by the Janata Party government. After extensive research, it submitted its report in 1980. The core recommendation was to provide 27% reservation for SEBCs, in addition to the existing reservations for SCs and STs, to address historical injustices and promote social equality.

Identifying backwardness

The Government's Decision (1990): The Office Memorandum

  • For 10 years, no action was taken on the report.
  • In August 1990, the V.P. Singh-led National Front government implemented it.
  • Issued through an 'Office Memorandum' from the Ministry of Personnel.

🎙️ Despite the report being submitted in 1980, it remained dormant for a decade. It was the National Front government, led by Prime Minister V.P. Singh, that decided to implement the recommendations in 1990. This decision was formally announced through an Office Memorandum, a common way for the Executive to issue policy directives.

Steps in Policy Implementation: Office Memorandum

  • The President of India signs the formal order.
  • The Prime Minister announces the decision.
  • The Cabinet takes the final decision on policy matters.
  • The Ministry of Personnel issues the Office Memorandum.

🎙️ This sequence shows the chain of command in policy implementation. While the President is the head of state and signs formal orders, the Prime Minister, as head of government, and the Cabinet are the key decision-makers. The specific ministry then issues the formal order, like the Office Memorandum in this case.

Supreme Court's Verdict (1992): Affirmation with Modifications

  • The Supreme Court upheld the 27% reservation for SEBCs.
  • It introduced the 'creamy layer' concept (excluding affluent individuals).
  • The court's decision is binding on the government and all parties.

🎙️ In a landmark judgment, the Supreme Court affirmed the constitutional validity of the 27% reservation. What is the Legislature? (Parliament)

  • The law-making body of the government.
  • Comprises the President, Lok Sabha (House of the People), and Rajya Sabha (Council of States).
  • Represents the will of the people through elected representatives.

🎙️ The Legislature, primarily our Parliament, is where laws are debated, discussed, and finally enacted. It's the voice of the people, as its members are directly or indirectly elected. The Parliament ensures that laws reflect the needs and aspirations of the diverse Indian population.

🏛️
India's law-making body

How Laws are Made in India

  • A 'Bill' is introduced in either House of Parliament.
  • It undergoes debate, scrutiny, and amendments.
  • Must be passed by both Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha.
  • Receives President's assent to become an 'Act' (Law).

🎙️ The process of law-making is meticulous and involves several stages to ensure thorough consideration. A bill can be introduced by a minister or a private member. Once passed by both Houses, the President's signature is the final step, transforming it into a legally binding law.

2Debate & Vote
3Passed by Both Houses

Political Executive vs. Permanent Executive

  • Political Executive: Elected by the people, temporary (e.g., PM, Cabinet Ministers).
  • Permanent Executive: Appointed through merit, long-term (e.g., IAS, IPS officers).
  • Political Executive has ultimate authority, accountable to the public.

🎙️ It's crucial to distinguish between these two parts of the Executive. Political leaders are elected and serve for a fixed term, making them directly accountable to the citizens. Permanent executives, or bureaucrats, provide expertise and continuity, serving regardless of which party is in power.

What is the Judiciary? (Courts)

  • The system of courts that interprets laws and dispenses justice.
  • Comprises the Supreme Court, High Courts, and District Courts.
  • Protects the rights of citizens and upholds the Constitution.

🎙️ The Judiciary is the third pillar of our democracy. It's not just about resolving disputes between individuals; it also acts as a guardian of the Constitution, ensuring that laws passed by the Legislature and actions taken by the Executive are in line with its principles.

Guardians of justice

Independence of the Judiciary

  • Judges are appointed by the President, not directly by political parties.
  • Difficult to remove judges, ensuring security of tenure.
  • Protects judges from political pressure, allowing impartial decisions.

🎙️ The independence of the Judiciary is a cornerstone of our democracy. It means judges can make decisions without fear or favour, free from interference by the Legislature or Executive. This ensures that justice is dispensed fairly and the rights of citizens are protected, even against the government.

Judicial Review: Checking Government Power

  • Power of the Supreme Court and High Courts to examine laws and executive orders.
  • Can declare laws/orders unconstitutional if they violate fundamental rights.
  • Example: Basic Structure Doctrine, preventing constitutional amendments that alter core principles.

🎙️ Judicial review is a powerful tool that allows the courts to act as a check on the powers of the other two branches. If a law or government action goes against the Constitution, the Judiciary can strike it down. This power ensures that the government operates within its constitutional limits.

Why Institutions Protect Democracy

  • **Checks and Balances:** Prevent concentration and abuse of power.
  • **Accountability:** Make government responsible to the people.
  • **Fairness & Justice:** Ensure impartial decision-making and dispute resolution.
  • **Stability:** Provide continuity and predictability in governance.

🎙️ These institutions are not isolated; they work together, constantly checking each other's power. This system of checks and balances is vital to prevent any single branch from becoming too powerful, thereby safeguarding the democratic principles of our nation and protecting citizens' rights.

Key Terms Recap

  • Legislature: Law-making body (Parliament).
  • Executive: Law-implementing body (Government, PM, Cabinet, Bureaucracy).
  • Judiciary: Interprets laws, dispenses justice (Courts).
  • Office Memorandum: Official government order.
  • Judicial Review: Power of courts to check constitutionality of laws/actions.

🎙️ Here's a quick summary of the important terms we've covered today. Make sure you understand each one, as they are fundamental to comprehending the working of government institutions.

Recap: Pillars of Indian Governance

  • **Legislature:** Makes laws (Parliament - Lok Sabha, Rajya Sabha).
  • **Executive:** Implements laws (PM, Cabinet, Bureaucracy).
  • **Judiciary:** Interprets laws, ensures justice (Supreme Court, High Courts).
  • These institutions provide a framework for democratic governance.
  • They ensure checks and balances, accountability, and protection of rights.

🎙️ We've covered the three main pillars of Indian democracy and their interconnected roles. Each institution is essential, contributing to the smooth functioning of the government and safeguarding the democratic values enshrined in our Constitution.

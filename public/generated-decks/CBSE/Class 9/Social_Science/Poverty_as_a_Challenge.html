Defining Poverty: A State of Deprivation

  • A situation where a person lacks the financial resources and essentials for a minimum standard of living.
  • Includes lack of food, safe drinking water, sanitation, healthcare, and education.
  • Often leads to hunger, malnutrition, lack of shelter, and illiteracy.

🎙️ Formally, poverty is a condition where individuals cannot afford the basic necessities of life. It's a complex issue, impacting physical well-being, mental health, and social participation.

Two Faces of Poverty: Absolute vs. Relative

  • Absolute Poverty: Inability to meet basic needs for survival (food, water, shelter).
  • Relative Poverty: Being poor compared to others in the same society.
  • India primarily focuses on absolute poverty due to its scale.

🎙️ It's important to distinguish between these two. Absolute poverty is about survival – a stark reality for many. Relative poverty, on the other hand, is about inequality within a society, even if basic needs are met.

Why Poverty Matters: Impact on Development

  • Individual Impact: Hunger, disease, illiteracy, child labour, lack of opportunities.

🎙️ Poverty isn't just a personal tragedy; it's a national challenge. It cripples human potential, strains public services, and slows down a country's overall development, creating a cycle that's hard to break.

The Poverty Line: 'Minimum Score to Pass'

  • Imagine an exam where you need a minimum score to pass.
  • The 'poverty line' is like that minimum score for economic well-being.
  • It's a threshold that separates the poor from the non-poor.

🎙️ Just like a passing mark, the poverty line is a benchmark. If your income or consumption falls below this line, you are officially classified as poor. It's a critical tool for governments.

📏
Poverty Threshold

Defining the Poverty Line: Income & Consumption

  • It's a common method to measure poverty based on income or consumption levels.
  • A minimum level of food, clothing, footwear, fuel, light, education, and medical requirements.
  • If a family's income or consumption falls below this level, they are 'below the poverty line' (BPL).

🎙️ The poverty line is a monetary value. It calculates the minimum amount of money required to purchase a basket of goods and services considered essential for survival and basic dignity in a given country.

💰
Economic Threshold

Estimating Poverty Line in India

  • Based on a minimum calorie requirement and monetary value.
  • Calculated by the National Sample Survey Organisation (NSSO).
  • Poverty line is reviewed periodically (e.g., Tendulkar Committee, Rangarajan Committee).

🎙️ In India, the poverty line is estimated by looking at both food (calorie) and non-food (other essentials) consumption. The NSSO collects the data, and expert groups define the actual thresholds, which are updated regularly.

Calorie & Monetary Norms (2011-12 Data)

  • Rural Areas: 2400 calories per person per day; ₹816 per month.
  • Urban Areas: 2100 calories per person per day; ₹1000 per month.
  • Higher calorie requirement in rural areas due to more physical labour.

🎙️ Notice the difference: rural areas have a higher calorie norm but a slightly lower monetary value. This reflects the difference in cost of living and the nature of work. These figures were crucial for identifying beneficiaries.

Why the Poverty Line is Crucial

  • Identifies the poor for targeted government schemes (e.g., PDS, subsidies).
  • Helps monitor the progress of anti-poverty programs.
  • Facilitates resource allocation and policy formulation.

🎙️ The poverty line is not just a statistical number; it's a policy tool. It allows the government to focus its efforts and resources on those who need them most, making anti-poverty measures more effective.

Vulnerability to Poverty: 'Walking on Thin Ice'

  • Imagine walking on thin ice – some are more likely to fall through.
  • Vulnerability describes the greater probability of certain communities/individuals becoming poor.
  • It's about the risk and susceptibility to poverty.

🎙️ Vulnerability means having a higher chance of falling into or remaining in poverty. It's about being exposed to greater risks – like natural disasters, job loss, or illness – without the resources to cope.

Defining Vulnerability: The Risk Factor

  • A measure that describes the likelihood of certain communities (or individuals) of being poor in the coming future.
  • Refers to the adverse impact of a hazard on a community.
  • Groups more vulnerable face greater risks during natural calamities, economic downturns.

🎙️ Vulnerability isn't just about current poverty; it's about future risk. It considers who is most likely to suffer when a crisis hits, because they lack assets, social support, or stable income.

⛈️
Facing Adversity

Vulnerable Groups in India

  • Social Groups: Scheduled Castes (SC), Scheduled Tribes (ST) households.
  • Economic Groups: Rural agricultural labourers, urban casual labourers.
  • Women, children (especially girl child), and the elderly are also highly vulnerable.

🎙️ In India, certain groups historically and socially face higher vulnerability. They often have limited access to education, healthcare, and stable employment, making them more susceptible to poverty's grip.

Social Exclusion: 'Being Left Out of the Circle'

  • Imagine a group of friends playing, but some are consistently excluded.
  • Social exclusion is the process by which individuals or groups are systematically blocked.
  • Prevents full participation in society and access to opportunities.

🎙️ Social exclusion is a powerful concept. It's not just about being poor, but about being denied opportunities and resources that are available to the majority, often due to social norms or discrimination.

Defining Social Exclusion: Process & Outcome

  • A process through which individuals or groups are denied access to rights, resources, and opportunities.
  • Often linked to caste, religion, gender, disability.
  • Leads to living in poor surroundings with poor people, excluded from better opportunities.

🎙️ Social exclusion refers to the systematic disadvantage of individuals or groups in society. It's both the process of being excluded and the resulting state of being unable to participate fully in social, economic, and political life.

Impact of Social Exclusion: Perpetuating Poverty

  • Limited access to education, healthcare, clean water, and sanitation.
  • Lack of good jobs and economic opportunities.
  • Creates a cycle where excluded groups remain poor across generations.

🎙️ Social exclusion directly contributes to poverty. When groups are denied access to essential services and opportunities, they cannot improve their economic status, trapping them and their future generations in poverty.

Poverty Trends in India: A Mixed Picture

  • Significant decline in poverty ratio since the 1970s (from 45% in 1993-94 to 21.9% in 2011-12).
  • However, the absolute number of poor remains substantial.
  • Poverty reduction has been uneven across different states and social groups.

🎙️ India has made considerable progress in reducing poverty over the decades, which is a positive sign. However, the sheer size of our population means even a smaller percentage still represents a large number of people.

Inter-State Disparities in Poverty

  • High Poverty States: Bihar (33.7%), Odisha (32.6%) – among the poorest.
  • Moderate Poverty States: Uttar Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh, Assam.
  • Low Poverty States: Kerala (7.1%), Punjab (8.3%), Himachal Pradesh, Tamil Nadu.

🎙️ Poverty is not uniformly spread across India. Some states, particularly in the east, face much higher poverty rates due to historical, economic, and social factors. States with good human resource development like Kerala show lower poverty.

Causes of Poverty in India: Historical Roots

  • British Colonial Rule: Exploitation, de-industrialization, destruction of traditional crafts.
  • Low Growth Rate: Stagnant economy due to colonial policies.
  • Unequal Land Distribution: Legacy of zamindari system, landless labourers.

🎙️ Many of India's poverty challenges have deep historical roots. The British colonial period systematically drained India's wealth and crippled its industries, leaving behind a legacy of underdevelopment and inequality.

📜
Colonial Legacy

Causes of Poverty in India: Economic Factors

  • High Population Growth: Strains resources, increases competition for jobs.
  • Unemployment: Lack of sufficient job creation for growing workforce.
  • Lack of Access to Resources: Limited access to land, capital, technology for small farmers.

🎙️ Post-independence, factors like rapid population growth put immense pressure on limited resources. Insufficient job creation, especially in the formal sector, and unequal access to productive assets continue to fuel poverty.

Causes of Poverty in India: Socio-Cultural Factors

  • Caste System & Discrimination: Limits opportunities, perpetuates social exclusion.
  • Gender Inequality: Women often denied education, property rights, economic agency.
  • Illiteracy & Lack of Skills: Low human capital, inability to secure better jobs.
  • Debt Trap: Many poor families borrow for social ceremonies, leading to chronic debt.

🎙️ Social and cultural norms also play a significant role. Discrimination, gender inequality, and the burden of social customs often push families deeper into poverty, creating a cycle that is hard to escape.

Anti-Poverty Measures: Two-Pronged Approach

  • 1. Promotion of Economic Growth: Aim to increase overall wealth and opportunities.
  • 2. Targeted Anti-Poverty Programmes: Specific schemes for vulnerable groups.
  • Examples: MGNREGA, PMRY, SGSY, Antyodaya Anna Yojana.

🎙️ India's strategy to combat poverty has two main pillars: fostering overall economic growth so that its benefits 'trickle down,' and directly implementing specific programs for the poor to provide safety nets and opportunities.

Common Mistakes Students Make

  • Confusing Absolute and Relative Poverty: Remember context (survival vs. comparison).
  • Not citing Indian examples: Always link concepts to Indian reality.
  • Ignoring multi-dimensional nature: Poverty is not just about income.
  • Forgetting government schemes: Name specific programs in answers.

🎙️ Students often mix up the types of poverty or give generic answers. Always remember to use concrete Indian examples and emphasize that poverty is a complex issue, not just a lack of money. Also, recall the key government initiatives.

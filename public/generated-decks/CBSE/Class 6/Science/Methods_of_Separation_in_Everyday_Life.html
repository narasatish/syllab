Why Do We Need to Separate Substances?

  • To remove harmful components.
  • To obtain useful substances.
  • To get pure substances.
  • To improve quality.

🎙️ Think about why we separate things. Sometimes, it's to get rid of something bad, like stones from rice. Other times, it's to get something good, like butter from milk. We also separate to make things pure or better quality for use.

What is Separation?

  • Process of dividing a mixture.
  • Results in two or more distinct products.
  • Based on physical properties.

🎙️ Simply put, separation is about taking a mixture and splitting it into its individual parts. We use different methods based on the physical differences of the substances, like their size, weight, or how they interact with water.

Method 1: Handpicking

  • Simplest method of separation.
  • Used for slightly larger impurities.
  • Done by hand.

🎙️ Handpicking is exactly what it sounds like – using your hands to pick out unwanted substances. It's one of the oldest and simplest methods, often used when the unwanted particles are large enough to be seen and picked easily.

🖐️
Pick by Hand
6 / 25

Handpicking Example: Separating Stones from Rice

  • Common in homes for cleaning grains.
  • Stones are different in size and colour.
  • Ensures safe and clean food.

🎙️ Before cooking, our parents often handpick small stones or husk from rice, pulses, or cereals. The stones are usually larger and darker, making them easy to spot and remove by hand, ensuring our food is clean and safe to eat.

Method 2: Threshing

  • Separating grain from stalks.
  • Done by beating stalks or using machines.
  • Prevents damage to grains.

🎙️ Threshing is a method used by farmers to separate the grains, like wheat or paddy, from their stalks. Traditionally, this was done by beating the stalks against a hard surface, but nowadays, large machines called threshers are used to do the job faster and more efficiently.

🌾
Grain Separation
8 / 25

Threshing Example: Separating Grains from Stalks

  • Stalks are dried first.
  • Grains loosen and fall off.
  • Important step after harvesting.

🎙️ Imagine a field of wheat after harvest. The grains are still attached to the stalks. Threshing helps to detach these grains. Farmers either manually beat the bundles of stalks or use machines, causing the grains to separate and fall off.

Method 3: Winnowing

  • Uses wind or moving air.
  • Separates lighter from heavier components.
  • Commonly used by farmers.

🎙️ Winnowing is a clever method that uses wind or a current of air to separate lighter particles, like husk or chaff, from heavier grains. Farmers often stand at a height and drop the mixture, letting the wind carry away the lighter impurities.

🌬️
Wind Separation
10 / 25

Winnowing Example: Separating Chaff from Grain

  • Chaff is lighter and blown away.
  • Heavier grains fall straight down.
  • Results in clean grain.

🎙️ After threshing, grains often have lighter impurities like chaff. When this mixture is dropped from a height, the wind blows the light chaff away, forming a separate heap. The heavier grains fall vertically, forming another heap, thus separating them.

Method 4: Sieving

  • Uses a sieve with small holes.
  • Separates particles of different sizes.
  • Larger particles remain on the sieve.

🎙️ Sieving is a method where a sieve, which is like a mesh with tiny holes, is used to separate components of different sizes. Smaller particles pass through the holes, while larger ones are retained on the sieve.

🥣
Sieve for Size
13 / 25

Sieving Example: Separating Bran from Flour

  • Fine flour passes through the sieve.
  • Larger bran particles remain.
  • Ensures smooth, fine flour for cooking.

🎙️ When we make chapatis at home, flour is often sieved to remove the bran and other coarse impurities. The fine flour passes through the sieve, while the slightly larger bran particles stay on top, giving us smoother flour for baking.

🍞🍞🍞🍞🍞🍞🍞🍞

Method 5: Sedimentation

  • Process where heavier particles settle down.
  • Used for insoluble substances in liquids.
  • Requires undisturbed time.

🎙️ Sedimentation is when heavier, insoluble particles in a liquid settle down at the bottom of the container. This happens if you leave the mixture undisturbed for some time. Think of mud settling in a glass of water.

⬇️
Settling Down
15 / 25

Sedimentation Example: Mud from Muddy Water

  • Mud particles are heavier than water.
  • They sink to the bottom.
  • Leaves clearer water above.

🎙️ If you take a glass of muddy water and let it stand for a while, you'll notice that the mud particles, being heavier, slowly sink and collect at the bottom. The water above becomes relatively clear, ready for the next step of separation.

2Allow to Settle

Method 6: Decantation

  • Pouring out the liquid carefully.
  • Leaves the settled solid behind.
  • Often follows sedimentation.

🎙️ Decantation is the process of gently pouring the clear liquid (called the supernatant) into another container, without disturbing the settled solid at the bottom. It's often used after sedimentation to separate the liquid from the solid.

💧
Careful Pouring
17 / 25

Decantation Example: Separating Water from Settled Mud

  • Carefully pour the clear water.
  • Avoid disturbing the settled mud.
  • Obtains a cleaner liquid.

🎙️ Following our muddy water example, once the mud has settled, you can carefully tilt the glass and pour the clear water into another container. This way, you separate the clear water from the mud without mixing them again.

Method 7: Filtration

  • Uses a filter medium (paper, cloth).
  • Separates insoluble solids from liquids.
  • Solids are retained, liquid passes through.

🎙️ Filtration is a very common method that uses a filter, like a filter paper or a piece of cloth, to separate tiny insoluble solid particles from a liquid. The liquid passes through the filter, but the solid particles are trapped.

☕
Filter It Out

Filtration Example: Separating Tea Leaves from Tea

  • Tea leaves are insoluble in water.
  • Strainer acts as a filter.
  • Get clear tea for drinking.

🎙️ Every morning, when you drink tea, you use filtration! The tea leaves, which are solid, are separated from the liquid tea using a tea strainer. The strainer's tiny holes allow the liquid to pass but hold back the leaves.

Method 8: Evaporation

  • Liquid changes into vapour.
  • Used to separate a soluble solid from a liquid.
  • Leaves the solid behind.

🎙️ Evaporation is the process where a liquid turns into a gas or vapour, usually due to heat. This method is excellent for separating a solid that has dissolved in a liquid, as the liquid evaporates, leaving the solid behind.

☀️
Water to Vapour
22 / 25

Evaporation Example: Separating Salt from Water

  • Water evaporates when heated.
  • Salt is left behind as solid crystals.
  • Used to obtain salt from seawater.

🎙️ This is how we get salt! Seawater contains dissolved salt. When seawater is left in shallow pits under the sun, the water evaporates, leaving behind the solid salt. This is a large-scale application of evaporation.

Method 9: Condensation

  • Vapour changes back into liquid.
  • Occurs when vapour cools down.
  • Often used after evaporation to collect the liquid.

🎙️ Condensation is the opposite of evaporation. It's the process where a gas or vapour changes back into its liquid form when it cools down. This is important if we want to recover the liquid that evaporated, not just the solid.

☁️
Vapour to Liquid

Condensation Example: Water Droplets from Steam

  • Steam cools when it touches a cold surface.
  • Forms tiny water droplets.
  • Seen on a lid over boiling water.

🎙️ Think about boiling water. The steam rising from it turns into tiny water droplets when it touches a cool surface, like the underside of a lid. This is condensation in action, showing vapour turning back into liquid.

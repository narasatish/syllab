What is Temperature?

  • It is the degree of hotness or coldness of an object.
  • It's a measure of the average kinetic energy of particles.
  • Temperature helps us quantify hotness.

🎙️ Temperature is not about how much heat an object has, but how hot or cold it feels. It's a precise way to describe the 'state' of an object in terms of its heat energy.

The Clinical Thermometer - Structure

  • Measures human body temperature.
  • Has a kink (constriction) near the bulb.
  • Contains mercury (or is digital).

🎙️ A clinical thermometer is designed specifically to measure our body temperature. The kink is a clever feature that prevents the mercury from falling back quickly, allowing us to read the temperature easily.

🤒
For Body Temperature
9 / 25

Reading a Clinical Thermometer

  • Range: 35°C to 42°C (or 94°F to 108°F).
  • The mercury expands with heat, showing the temperature.
  • Each small division represents a part of a degree.

🎙️ To read it, first, ensure the mercury level is below 35°C. Place it under the tongue or armpit for about a minute, then take it out and read the highest point the mercury has reached.

2Place under tongue

🎙️ It's very important to clean the thermometer with an antiseptic solution before and after use to prevent the spread of germs. Also, be gentle; they are made of glass and can break easily.

The Laboratory Thermometer - Structure

  • Measures temperature of objects/liquids in labs.
  • Has a much wider range than clinical thermometers.
  • It does not have a kink (constriction).

🎙️ Unlike the clinical thermometer, the laboratory thermometer is used for measuring temperatures in science experiments, like the temperature of water or other liquids. Notice it doesn't have a kink.

🧪
For Experiments

Reading a Laboratory Thermometer

  • Range: -10°C to 110°C (approximate).
  • Mercury level falls quickly once removed from substance.
  • Read the scale while the bulb is immersed.

🎙️ The range of a laboratory thermometer is much wider. Because it has no kink, you must read the temperature while the bulb is still immersed in the substance you are measuring.

Important Precautions for Laboratory Thermometers

  • Hold vertically, not tilted.
  • Bulb fully immersed in the substance.
  • Do not touch the container walls or bottom.

🎙️ Holding it vertically ensures an accurate reading. The bulb must be completely surrounded by the substance, but not touching the bottom or sides of the beaker, as that would give an incorrect reading of the container, not the liquid.

Worked Example: Reading a Thermometer Scale

  • Identify major markings (e.g., 30°C, 40°C).
  • Count the number of minor divisions between them.
  • Calculate the value of each minor division.

🎙️ Often, there are 10 small divisions between two major markings (like 30°C and 40°C). This means each small division represents 1°C. If there are 5 divisions, each is 2°C. Always check the scale!

Units of Temperature Measurement

  • Celsius (°C): Most common, water freezes at 0°C, boils at 100°C.
  • Fahrenheit (°F): Another common unit, water freezes at 32°F, boils at 212°F.
  • Kelvin (K): Used in advanced science, 0K is absolute zero.

🎙️ We usually use Celsius in India, but you might see Fahrenheit, especially for weather forecasts in some countries or older thermometers. It's good to know both exist.

How Does Heat Travel?

  • Heat always flows from a hotter object to a colder object.
  • Three main ways: Conduction, Convection, and Radiation.
  • This flow continues until temperatures equalize.

🎙️ Temperature tells us how hot or cold something is. Now, let's briefly understand how heat itself moves from one place to another. It always moves from hot to cold.

Heat Transfer by Conduction

  • Heat transfers through direct contact between particles.
  • It is common in solids, especially metals.
  • Particles vibrate and pass energy to neighbours.

🎙️ Imagine holding a metal spoon in a hot cup of tea. The end of the spoon you're holding slowly gets warm. That's conduction! Heat passes directly from particle to particle.

Heat transfers from hot to cold regions through three methods: conduction (direct contact)
23 / 25

Conductors and Insulators

  • Conductors: Materials that allow heat to pass through easily (e.g., metals).
  • Insulators: Materials that do not allow heat to pass through easily (e.g., wood, plastic).
  • This property is crucial for many applications.

🎙️ This is why cooking utensils have metal bases (conductors) and plastic handles (insulators). Good conductors help cook food, while insulators protect our hands from the heat.

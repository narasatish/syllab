What is a Fraction?

  • A fraction represents a part of a whole.
  • It shows how many equal parts of a whole are taken.
  • Example: 1/2 means 1 part out of 2 equal parts.

🎙️ So, a fraction is essentially a way to describe a portion of something that has been divided into equal pieces. It's like taking a whole cake, cutting it into several equal slices, and then talking about how many slices you have or want.

Parts of a Fraction: Numerator and Denominator

  • Numerator: The top number.

🎙️ Every fraction has two main parts: the numerator and the denominator. The denominator tells us the total number of equal parts the whole is divided into, while the numerator tells us how many of those parts we are considering.

Types of Fractions: Proper Fractions

  • Numerator is smaller than the denominator.
  • Represents a value less than one whole.
  • Example: 1/2, 3/4, 5/8.

🎙️ Our first type is the Proper Fraction. Think of it as a 'proper' share – you're taking less than the whole thing. If you have 3 out of 4 pieces of cake, that's a proper fraction because you haven't taken the whole cake yet.

Types of Fractions: Improper Fractions

  • Numerator is greater than or equal to the denominator.
  • Represents a value equal to or greater than one whole.
  • Example: 5/4, 7/3, 8/8.

🎙️ Next, we have Improper Fractions. These are when the top number is bigger than or the same as the bottom number. It means you have at least one whole, and maybe some more parts. For instance, if you have 5 halves of an apple, that's more than two whole apples!

Types of Fractions: Mixed Fractions

  • Combination of a whole number and a proper fraction.
  • Another way to represent improper fractions.
  • Example: 1 1/2 (one and a half), 2 3/4.

🎙️ Mixed fractions are like having a full box of sweets and then some extra loose sweets. It's a whole number combined with a proper fraction. For example, if you eat 1 whole chapati and then half of another, you've eaten 1 1/2 chapatis.

Converting Improper to Mixed Fractions

  • Divide the numerator by the denominator.
  • The quotient is the whole number.
  • The remainder is the new numerator.
  • The denominator stays the same.

🎙️ We can convert improper fractions into mixed fractions. It's like figuring out how many full boxes you can make from a pile of loose items. You divide the top number by the bottom number, and the result gives you the whole part and the remaining fractional part.

1Divide Numerator by Denominator
2Quotient = Whole Number
3Remainder = New Numerator
4Denominator Stays Same

Converting Mixed to Improper Fractions

  • Multiply the whole number by the denominator.
  • Add the numerator to the product.
  • This sum is the new numerator.
  • The denominator stays the same.

🎙️ The reverse is also possible! To convert a mixed fraction back to an improper fraction, you combine the whole parts with the fractional part. It's like opening all the full boxes and adding their contents to the loose items to get a total count.

1Whole x Denominator
2Add Numerator
3New Numerator
4Denominator Stays Same

Equivalent Fractions: Same Value, Different Look

  • Fractions that represent the same value.
  • Look different but are equal in amount.
  • Example: 1/2 is equivalent to 2/4 and 3/6.

🎙️ Imagine you have a cake cut into two equal halves, and another identical cake cut into four equal quarters. If you take one half of the first cake, and two quarters of the second cake, you actually have the same amount of cake. These are equivalent fractions.

⚖️
Equal in value

How to Find Equivalent Fractions

  • Multiply the numerator and denominator by the same non-zero number.
  • Divide the numerator and denominator by the same non-zero number (simplifying).
  • Example: 1/2 x (2/2) = 2/4.

🎙️ To find equivalent fractions, you must always perform the same operation on both the numerator and the denominator. This ensures that the 'balance' of the fraction is maintained, and its value remains unchanged, even if it looks different.

1Multiply N & D by same number
2Divide N & D by same number

Comparing Fractions: Like Fractions

  • Fractions with the same denominator.
  • Easy to compare directly.
  • Example: 3/5 and 2/5.

🎙️ When fractions have the same denominator, they are called 'like fractions'. Think of it as comparing pieces of the same size. If you have two slices of a 5-slice pizza and your friend has three slices of the same 5-slice pizza, it's easy to see who has more.

Comparing Like Fractions: Just Compare Numerators!

  • When denominators are the same, compare the numerators.
  • The fraction with the larger numerator is greater.
  • Example: 3/5 > 2/5 (since 3 > 2).

🎙️ This is the simplest form of comparison. Since the pieces are all the same size, you just need to look at how many pieces each fraction represents. More pieces mean a larger fraction. It's very straightforward!

Comparing Fractions: Unlike Fractions

  • Fractions with different denominators.
  • Cannot be compared directly.
  • Example: 1/2 and 2/3.

🎙️ Unlike fractions are a bit trickier because you're comparing pieces of different sizes. Imagine comparing a slice from a pizza cut into 4 pieces, with a slice from a pizza cut into 8 pieces. You can't just compare the number of slices directly.

Comparing Unlike Fractions: The LCM Method

  • Find the LCM (Least Common Multiple) of the denominators.
  • Convert both fractions to equivalent fractions with the LCM as the new denominator.
  • Compare the new numerators.

🎙️ To compare unlike fractions, we need to make their denominators the same. This is where the LCM comes in handy. By finding the LCM, we convert them into 'like fractions' and then we can easily compare their numerators.

1Find LCM of denominators
2Convert to equivalent fractions
3Compare new numerators

Adding Fractions: Like Fractions

  • Add the numerators.
  • Keep the denominator the same.
  • Example: 1/4 + 2/4 = 3/4.

🎙️ Adding like fractions is as simple as adding whole numbers. If you have one piece of a four-piece cake and then get two more pieces of the same four-piece cake, you now have three pieces in total. The size of the pieces (denominator) doesn't change.

Adding Fractions: Unlike Fractions

  • Find the LCM of the denominators.
  • Convert to equivalent fractions with the LCM as denominator.
  • Add the new numerators, keep the denominator same.

🎙️ Just like comparing, to add unlike fractions, we first need to make their denominators the same using the LCM. Once they become like fractions, the addition process is straightforward. This step is crucial for accurate results.

1Find LCM of denominators
2Convert to equivalent fractions
3Add new numerators

Subtracting Fractions: Like Fractions

  • Subtract the numerators.
  • Keep the denominator the same.

🎙️ Subtracting like fractions is also very simple. If you have three laddoos out of a box of five, and you eat one, you are left with two laddoos from that same box. The denominator (total parts) remains constant.

Subtracting Fractions: Unlike Fractions

  • Find the LCM of the denominators.
  • Convert to equivalent fractions with the LCM as denominator.
  • Subtract the new numerators, keep the denominator same.

🎙️ Similar to addition, when subtracting unlike fractions, the first step is always to find a common denominator using the LCM. This allows us to work with 'pieces' of the same size, making the subtraction meaningful and correct.

1Find LCM of denominators
2Convert to equivalent fractions
3Subtract new numerators

Common Mistakes in Fractions

🎙️ Students often make a few common errors. Remember, you never add or subtract the denominators! Also, skipping the LCM step for unlike fractions will lead to wrong answers. Always double-check your conversion steps for mixed fractions.

Factors: The Building Blocks

  • A factor of a number divides it completely.
  • The remainder is always zero.
  • Factors are always less than or equal to the number.

🎙️ Think of factors as the numbers you multiply together to get another number. For example, if you have 6 cookies, you can arrange them in 2 rows of 3, or 3 rows of 2. So, 2 and 3 are factors of 6.

How to Find Factors?

  • Start with 1 and the number itself.
  • Check divisibility by numbers from 2 upwards.
  • Stop when you reach a factor that has already been found.

🎙️ Let's find the factors of 12. We start with 1 and 12. Then we check 2 (2x6=12), then 3 (3x4=12). When we check 4, we see 4 is already listed as a factor. So we stop. The factors are 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, and 12.

Multiples: Repeated Additions

  • A multiple of a number is the product of that number and any whole number.
  • Multiples are like skip-counting.
  • Multiples are always greater than or equal to the number.

🎙️ Multiples are what you get when you multiply a number by 1, 2, 3, and so on. For example, the multiples of 2 are 2, 4, 6, 8, 10... It's like skip counting by that number. They go on forever!

How to Find Multiples?

  • Multiply the number by 1, 2, 3, 4, and so on.
  • There are infinitely many multiples for any number.
  • Example: Multiples of 3 are 3, 6, 9, 12...

🎙️ To find multiples of 3, we simply multiply 3 by consecutive whole numbers: 3x1=3, 3x2=6, 3x3=9, 3x4=12, and so on. Notice how the blue circles are grouped in threes, representing the multiples.

Prime Numbers: Only Two Factors

  • A prime number has exactly two factors.
  • These factors are 1 and the number itself.
  • Examples: 2, 3, 5, 7, 11...

🎙️ Prime numbers are special because they can only be divided evenly by 1 and themselves. They are like the unique building blocks in the number system. Can you think of any other prime numbers?

Composite Numbers: More Than Two Factors

  • A composite number has more than two factors.
  • It can be divided by numbers other than 1 and itself.
  • Examples: 4, 6, 8, 9, 10...

🎙️ Composite numbers are the opposite of prime numbers. They can be broken down into smaller factors. For example, 6 can be written as 2 x 3, so its factors are 1, 2, 3, and 6. This makes it a composite number.

Prime vs. Composite: A Comparison

  • Prime: Only 1 and itself as factors.
  • Composite: More than two factors.
  • All numbers greater than 1 are either prime or composite.

🎙️ Here's a quick way to remember: think of prime numbers as 'single' numbers that cannot be broken down further. Composite numbers are 'made up' of other numbers, having multiple factors. This chart visually shows the difference in the number of factors.

The Special Number: One (1)

  • The number 1 is neither prime nor composite.
  • It has only one factor: itself.
  • Prime numbers need exactly two factors.

🎙️ The number 1 holds a unique position. It doesn't fit the definition of a prime number because it doesn't have two distinct factors. It also doesn't fit the definition of a composite number because it doesn't have more than two factors. So, 1 is in a category of its own!

Divisibility Rules: Smart Shortcuts!

  • Rules to quickly check if a number is divisible by another.
  • No need for long division every time.
  • Saves time and makes calculations easier.

🎙️ Divisibility rules are like secret codes that tell us if one number can be perfectly divided by another without leaving a remainder. Learning these rules will make you a math wizard! Let's explore some common ones.

Divisibility Rule for 2

  • A number is divisible by 2 if its last digit is even.
  • Even digits are 0, 2, 4, 6, 8.
  • Example: 24 (ends in 4) is divisible by 2.

🎙️ This is one of the easiest rules! Just look at the very last digit of the number. If it's 0, 2, 4, 6, or 8, then the entire number can be divided by 2. For instance, 138 is divisible by 2 because it ends in 8.

Divisibility Rule for 3

  • A number is divisible by 3 if the sum of its digits is divisible by 3.
  • Example: For 123, 1+2+3 = 6. 6 is divisible by 3, so 123 is too.

🎙️ This rule requires a little addition. Add up all the digits of the number. If that sum is a multiple of 3, then the original number is divisible by 3. Try it with 456: 4+5+6 = 15. Since 15 is divisible by 3, so is 456!

Divisibility Rule for 5

  • A number is divisible by 5 if its last digit is 0 or 5.
  • Example: 75 (ends in 5) is divisible by 5.
  • Example: 120 (ends in 0) is divisible by 5.

🎙️ Another straightforward rule! If a number ends with either a 0 or a 5, then it is guaranteed to be divisible by 5. Think about counting by fives: 5, 10, 15, 20... they all end in 0 or 5.

Divisibility Rule for 10

  • A number is divisible by 10 if its last digit is 0.
  • Example: 230 (ends in 0) is divisible by 10.
  • Numbers like 10, 20, 30, 100 are all divisible by 10.

🎙️ This rule is very simple! Any number that ends with a zero can be divided perfectly by 10. This makes sense because our number system is based on tens. Numbers like 50, 100, 1000 all end in zero.

HCF: Highest Common Factor

  • HCF stands for Highest Common Factor.
  • It is the largest factor that two or more numbers share.
  • Also known as Greatest Common Divisor (GCD).

🎙️ HCF is about finding the biggest number that can divide into two or more numbers without leaving a remainder. Think of it as finding the largest common 'piece' that fits into all numbers. It's very useful in simplifying fractions or dividing things into equal groups.

The biggest shared piece

Finding HCF: Listing Factors Method

  • List all factors for each number.
  • Identify the factors common to all numbers.
  • The largest of these common factors is the HCF.

🎙️ Let's find the HCF of 12 and 18. First, list all factors of 12. Then, list all factors of 18. Next, circle the factors that appear in both lists. Finally, pick the largest number from the circled common factors. That's your HCF!

1Factors of 12: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12
2Factors of 18: 1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 18
3Common Factors: 1, 2, 3, 6
4Highest Common Factor (HCF): 6

LCM: Lowest Common Multiple

  • LCM stands for Lowest Common Multiple.
  • It is the smallest non-zero multiple common to two or more numbers.
  • Useful for adding/subtracting fractions with different denominators.

🎙️ LCM is about finding the smallest number that is a multiple of two or more numbers. Imagine two friends skipping, one by 2s and one by 3s. The LCM tells you the first number they will both land on. It's the smallest number that all numbers can divide into.

23 / 25

Finding LCM: Listing Multiples Method

  • List multiples for each number until a common one is found.
  • The first (lowest) common multiple is the LCM.
  • Example: LCM of 4 and 6.

🎙️ To find the LCM of 4 and 6, we list out the multiples of 4: 4, 8, 12, 16... Then we list the multiples of 6: 6, 12, 18... The very first number that appears in both lists is 12. So, the LCM of 4 and 6 is 12.

1Multiples of 4: 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, ...
2Multiples of 6: 6, 12, 18, 24, ...
3First Common Multiple: 12
4Lowest Common Multiple (LCM): 12

HCF & LCM: Real-Life Applications

  • HCF: Dividing items into largest equal groups.
  • LCM: Finding when events will repeat at the same time.
  • Example: Scheduling buses, sharing items, tiling a floor.

🎙️ HCF helps us solve problems like 'What is the greatest number of students among whom 24 pencils and 36 erasers can be distributed equally?' LCM helps with problems like 'Two bells ring at intervals of 5 and 7 minutes respectively. When will they ring together again?' These concepts are super practical!

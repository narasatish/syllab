Factors: The Building Blocks

  • A factor of a number divides it completely, leaving no remainder.
  • Every number has at least two factors: 1 and itself.
  • Example: Factors of 12 are 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12.

🎙️ Imagine you have a number, and you want to break it down into smaller parts that multiply to give that number. These smaller parts are its factors. They are like the building blocks that construct the original number.

Real-life Factors: Sharing Equally

  • You have 10 cookies. How many ways can you share them equally?
  • You can give 1 cookie to 10 friends (1x10).
  • You can give 2 cookies to 5 friends (2x5).
  • You can give 5 cookies to 2 friends (5x2).

Multiples: Skip Counting Fun

  • A multiple of a number is the result of multiplying it by another whole number.
  • Multiples go on infinitely.
  • Example: Multiples of 3 are 3, 6, 9, 12, 15, ...

🎙️ Multiples are like skip counting! If you start at a number and keep adding it to itself, you get its multiples. They are the numbers that appear in the multiplication table of a given number. They just keep getting bigger and bigger.

Prime Numbers: Unique Gems

  • A prime number has only two factors: 1 and itself.
  • Examples: 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13...
  • The number 1 is NOT a prime number.
  • 2 is the only even prime number.

🎙️ Prime numbers are special because they can only be divided evenly by 1 and themselves. They are like the 'atoms' of numbers, the smallest indivisible units in terms of multiplication. Identifying them is a key skill.

Composite Numbers: Made of Primes

  • A composite number has more than two factors.
  • Examples: 4, 6, 8, 9, 10, 12...
  • Every composite number can be written as a product of prime numbers.
  • The number 1 is neither prime nor composite.

🎙️ Composite numbers are the opposite of prime numbers; they can be broken down into more than just 1 and themselves. They are 'composed' of other factors. Understanding them helps us with concepts like prime factorization later on.

Real-life Primes & Composites: Grouping Objects

  • If you have 5 candies, you can only group them as 1 group of 5 or 5 groups of 1. (Prime)
  • If you have 6 candies, you can group them as 1x6, 2x3, 3x2, 6x1. (Composite)
  • Prime numbers are hard to share equally in many ways.

🎙️ Imagine you have a certain number of items and you want to arrange them into equal rows or columns. If the number of items is prime, you'll find very few ways to arrange them equally. If it's composite, you'll have more options.

🎙️ Instead of performing long division every time, we can use simple rules to check if a number is divisible by another. These divisibility tests are powerful shortcuts that make working with numbers much faster and easier.

Divisibility by 2 and 5

  • By 2: A number is divisible by 2 if its last digit is 0, 2, 4, 6, or 8 (even).
  • Example: 134 is divisible by 2 (ends in 4).
  • By 5: A number is divisible by 5 if its last digit is 0 or 5.
  • Example: 245 is divisible by 5 (ends in 5).

🎙️ These are some of the easiest divisibility rules. Just look at the very last digit of the number. If it's even, it's divisible by 2. If it ends in a 0 or 5, it's divisible by 5. Simple, right?

Divisibility by 3 and 9

  • By 3: A number is divisible by 3 if the sum of its digits is divisible by 3.
  • Example: 123 (1+2+3=6), 6 is divisible by 3, so 123 is too.
  • By 9: A number is divisible by 9 if the sum of its digits is divisible by 9.
  • Example: 729 (7+2+9=18), 18 is divisible by 9, so 729 is too.

🎙️ For 3 and 9, we need to do a little addition. Add up all the digits in the number. If that sum is a multiple of 3, the original number is divisible by 3. The same logic applies to 9.

Divisibility by 4 and 8

  • By 4: A number is divisible by 4 if the number formed by its last two digits is divisible by 4.
  • Example: 516 (16 is divisible by 4, so 516 is too).
  • By 8: A number is divisible by 8 if the number formed by its last three digits is divisible by 8.
  • Example: 1280 (280 is divisible by 8, so 1280 is too).

🎙️ These rules depend on the last two or three digits. For 4, focus on the number made by the tens and units digit. For 8, look at the number formed by the hundreds, tens, and units digits. It's about checking smaller parts of the number.

Divisibility by 6 and 10

  • By 6: A number is divisible by 6 if it is divisible by BOTH 2 and 3.
  • Example: 18 (even, 1+8=9, so divisible by 2 & 3, thus by 6).
  • By 10: A number is divisible by 10 if its last digit is 0.
  • Example: 350 (ends in 0, so divisible by 10).

🎙️ The divisibility rule for 6 combines the rules for 2 and 3. If a number passes both tests, it's divisible by 6. For 10, it's very straightforward – just check if the number ends with a zero.

Prime Factorisation: Breaking Down Numbers

  • Writing a number as a product of its prime factors.
  • Every composite number has a unique prime factorisation.
  • We can use methods like the factor tree or division method.

🎙️ Prime factorisation is like finding the unique DNA of a number. It's expressing a number as a multiplication of only prime numbers. This skill is crucial for finding HCF and LCM later on.

1Start with the number
2Divide by smallest prime
3Continue until all factors are prime
4Write the product
18 / 25

Worked Example: Prime Factorisation of 36

  • Start with 36.
  • Divide by 2: 36 = 2 x 18.
  • Divide 18 by 2: 18 = 2 x 9.
  • Divide 9 by 3: 9 = 3 x 3.
  • So, 36 = 2 x 2 x 3 x 3 = 2² x 3².

🎙️ Let's work through an example using the division method. We keep dividing by the smallest possible prime number until we are left with only prime numbers at the end. This process ensures we find all prime factors.

HCF: Highest Common Factor

  • The largest factor that two or more numbers share.
  • Also known as Greatest Common Divisor (GCD).
  • Useful for dividing items into largest possible equal groups.

🎙️ HCF stands for Highest Common Factor. It's the biggest number that can divide two or more numbers without leaving any remainder. Think of it as finding the largest common 'piece' they share.

⛰️
Largest Shared

Real-life HCF: Arranging Books

  • You have 12 English books and 18 Math books.
  • You want to stack them in equal piles, with each pile having books of only one subject.
  • What is the greatest number of books you can put in each pile?
  • HCF(12, 18) = 6. You can make piles of 6 books.

🎙️ Imagine you're a librarian trying to organize books efficiently. HCF helps you find the maximum number of books you can put in each identical stack, ensuring no books are left over and piles are uniform. This makes the library neat!

LCM: Lowest Common Multiple

  • The smallest common multiple of two or more numbers.
  • It's the first number that appears in the list of multiples for all numbers.
  • Useful for finding when events will repeat at the same time.

🎙️ LCM stands for Lowest Common Multiple. It's the smallest number that is a multiple of two or more given numbers. It's like finding the first point where their 'skip counting' lines meet.

🌊
Smallest Shared

Real-life LCM: Bus Schedules

  • Bus A leaves every 15 minutes. Bus B leaves every 20 minutes.
  • If they both leave at 8:00 AM, when will they next leave together?
  • LCM(15, 20) = 60.
  • They will next leave together after 60 minutes, at 9:00 AM.

🎙️ LCM is very helpful for real-world problems involving cycles or schedules. If two buses leave at different intervals, LCM tells us when they will both depart at the same time again. This helps in planning and coordination.

Worked Example: HCF and LCM of 12 and 18

  • Prime factors of 12: 2 x 2 x 3
  • Prime factors of 18: 2 x 3 x 3
  • HCF: Common prime factors = 2 x 3 = 6
  • LCM: All prime factors (highest power) = 2² x 3² = 4 x 9 = 36

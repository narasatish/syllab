What are Factors?

  • Numbers that divide another number exactly.
  • They leave no remainder.
  • Every number has at least two factors: 1 and itself.

🎙️ Before we dive into prime numbers, let's quickly recall what factors are. Factors are like ingredients that make up a number. If you can divide a number by another number and get a perfect result without any leftover, then that number is a factor.

🧩
Building blocks of a number

Let's Find Factors of 12!

  • Can 12 be divided by 1? Yes, 12 ÷ 1 = 12.
  • Can 12 be divided by 2? Yes, 12 ÷ 2 = 6.
  • Can 12 be divided by 3? Yes, 12 ÷ 3 = 4.
  • Factors of 12 are: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12.

🎙️ Imagine you have 12 boxes. You can arrange them in one row of 12, two rows of 6, three rows of 4, and so on. The numbers that represent these rows or columns are the factors. So, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, and 12 are all factors of 12.

Even and Odd Numbers

  • Odd numbers are not divisible by 2 (e.g., 1, 3, 5).

Prime Numbers: Only Two Factors

  • A prime number has ONLY two factors.
  • These two factors are 1 and the number itself.
  • Examples: 2, 3, 5, 7, 11...

🎙️ The defining characteristic of a prime number is that it can only be divided exactly by two numbers: 1 and itself. No other number can divide it without leaving a remainder. Think of them as numbers that are 'indivisible' by others.

Composite Numbers: More Than Two Factors

  • Numbers that have MORE than two factors.
  • They can be divided by 1, themselves, and other numbers.
  • Think of them as numbers made from smaller prime 'parts'.

🎙️ On the other side of the coin, we have Composite Numbers. These are numbers that are not prime. They have more than just two factors, meaning they can be broken down or divided by other numbers besides 1 and themselves.

🧱

Composite Numbers: Many Factors

  • Example: Factors of 4 are 1, 2, 4 (three factors).
  • Example: Factors of 6 are 1, 2, 3, 6 (four factors).
  • Any number that is not prime (except 1) is composite.

🎙️ Let's take 4 as an example. It has factors 1, 2, and 4. Since it has three factors (more than two), 4 is a composite number. Similarly, 6 has factors 1, 2, 3, and 6, making it composite.

The Unique Number 1

  • Number 1 has only ONE factor: itself.
  • It does not fit the definition of prime (two factors).
  • It does not fit the definition of composite (more than two factors).
  • So, 1 is neither prime nor composite!

🎙️ Number 1 is a very special case. It only has one factor, which is 1 itself. Because it doesn't have exactly two factors (like primes) or more than two factors (like composites), it stands alone. So, remember, 1 is neither a prime nor a composite number.

Finding Primes with the Sieve!

  • A method to find prime numbers.
  • Invented by Eratosthenes, an ancient Greek mathematician.
  • It's like filtering out the non-prime numbers.

🎙️ Now, let's learn a super cool trick to find prime numbers up to a certain limit! It's called the Sieve of Eratosthenes, named after its inventor. Imagine you have a list of numbers, and you 'sieve' out all the composite ones, leaving only the primes.

A clever way to find primes

Sieve Step 1: Exclude 1

  • Write down numbers from 1 to 100 (or any limit).
  • Cross out number 1.
  • Remember, 1 is neither prime nor composite.

🎙️ First, you'd write down all the numbers you want to check, say from 1 to 100. Then, the very first thing we do is cross out 1, because we just learned that it's neither prime nor composite. It doesn't fit in either category.

Cross out 1
16 / 25

Sieve Step 2: Circle 2, Cross Multiples

  • Circle the first prime number, 2.
  • Cross out all multiples of 2 (4, 6, 8, 10...).
  • These are all composite numbers.

🎙️ Next, we find the smallest uncrossed number, which is 2. Circle it, as it's our first prime. Then, systematically go through your list and cross out every multiple of 2 (4, 6, 8, 10, etc.). All these numbers are composite because they have 2 as a factor.

✔️
Circle 2, then cross out 4, 6, 8, 10...
17 / 25

Sieve Step 3: Circle 3, Cross Multiples

  • Find the next uncrossed number, 3.
  • Circle 3, as it's a prime number.
  • Cross out all multiples of 3 (6, 9, 12, 15...).

🎙️ Now, move to the next uncrossed number, which is 3. Circle 3, because it's prime. Then, just like with 2, cross out all multiples of 3 that haven't been crossed out yet (like 9, 15, 21, etc.). Some might already be crossed from being multiples of 2.

3️⃣
Circle 3, then cross out 6, 9, 12, 15...
18 / 25

Sieve Step 4: Keep Going!

  • Continue this process with the next uncrossed number (e.g., 5).
  • Circle it, then cross out all its multiples.
  • Repeat until all numbers are either circled or crossed out.

🎙️ You continue this pattern. The next uncrossed number will be 5. Circle 5 and cross out its multiples. Then 7, circle 7 and cross out its multiples. You keep doing this until all numbers in your list are either circled (prime) or crossed out (composite).

➡️
Repeat for 5, 7, 11...

Prime Numbers in Daily Life

  • Imagine you have 7 apples.
  • You can only arrange them in one group of 7, or 7 groups of 1.
  • This is a prime number arrangement!

🎙️ Prime numbers might seem abstract, but they appear in simple situations too. If you have 7 apples, you can't divide them evenly among 2, 3, or 4 friends without cutting them. You either give all 7 to one person, or one apple to each of seven people. That's a prime number in action!

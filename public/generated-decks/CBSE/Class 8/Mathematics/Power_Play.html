Introducing Exponents: Base & Power

  • An exponent tells us how many times to multiply a number by itself.
  • The 'base' is the number being multiplied.
  • The 'exponent' (or power) is the small number written above and to the right.

🎙️ So, instead of writing 2 x 2 x 2 x 2 x 2, we can write it in a much shorter way using exponents. The number being multiplied repeatedly is called the 'base', and the small number telling us how many times to multiply it is called the 'exponent' or 'power'. It's a powerful way to show repeated multiplication.

How Do We Read Exponents?

  • '2 raised to the power of 3' or '2 to the power 3'
  • Special cases: 'squared' (power of 2), 'cubed' (power of 3).
  • Example: 5² is '5 squared', 4³ is '4 cubed'.

🎙️ When we see a number with an exponent, we read it in a specific way. For example, 2 with a small 3 above it is read as '2 raised to the power of 3'. For the power of 2, we often say 'squared', like 5 squared. For the power of 3, we say 'cubed', like 4 cubed. These are special terms you should remember!

Law 1: Product of Powers (a^m * a^n)

  • When multiplying powers with the same base, add the exponents.
  • Formula: a^m * a^n = a^(m+n)
  • Example: 2³ * 2² = (2*2*2) * (2*2) = 2⁵

🎙️ This law says that when you multiply two numbers that have the same base, you can simply add their exponents. So, if you have 'a' raised to the power 'm' multiplied by 'a' raised to the power 'n', the answer is 'a' raised to the power 'm plus n'. It's a fantastic shortcut for multiplication!

Law 2: Quotient of Powers (a^m / a^n)

  • When dividing powers with the same base, subtract the exponents.
  • Formula: a^m / a^n = a^(m-n)
  • Example: 5⁵ / 5² = (5*5*5*5*5) / (5*5) = 5³

🎙️ This law states that when you divide two numbers with the same base, you subtract the exponent of the denominator from the exponent of the numerator. So, 'a' to the power 'm' divided by 'a' to the power 'n' becomes 'a' to the power 'm minus n'. Remember, the base must be the same for this law to apply.

Zero Exponent Rule: a^0 = 1

  • Any non-zero number raised to the power of zero is 1.
  • Example: 100^0 = 1, (-5)^0 = 1
  • Why? Using a^m / a^n = a^(m-n), if m=n, then a^m / a^m = a^(m-m) = a^0. And a^m / a^m = 1.

🎙️ This is a very important rule: any non-zero number raised to the power of zero is always 1. It doesn't matter if the base is big or small, positive or negative! We can prove this using our division law: if you divide a number by itself, the exponents subtract to zero, and the result is 1.

🎙️ What about negative exponents? They might seem a bit tricky at first, but they're quite logical. Think of it like going up and down floors in a building. A positive exponent might mean going up. Negative Exponents Rule: a^-m = 1/a^m

  • A negative exponent means taking the reciprocal of the base raised to the positive exponent.
  • Formula: a^-m = 1/a^m
  • Example: 2⁻³ = 1/2³ = 1/8

🎙️ So, a negative exponent means you take the reciprocal of the base raised to the *positive* version of that exponent. If you have 'a' to the power of negative 'm', it becomes 1 divided by 'a' to the power of positive 'm'. It's like moving the term from the numerator to the denominator, or vice-versa, and changing the sign of the exponent.

Law 3: Power of a Power ((a^m)^n)

  • When raising a power to another power, multiply the exponents.
  • Formula: (a^m)^n = a^(m*n)
  • Example: (3²)³ = (3*3) * (3*3) * (3*3) = 3⁶

🎙️ This law is super useful! It says that if you have a number 'a' raised to the power 'm', and then that entire thing is raised to another power 'n', you simply multiply the two exponents together. So, it becomes 'a' raised to the power of 'm times n'. It's like a double dose of power!

Law 4: Power of a Product ((ab)^m)

  • When a product is raised to a power, each factor in the product is raised to that power.
  • Formula: (ab)^m = a^m * b^m
  • Example: (2*3)² = 2² * 3² = 4 * 9 = 36

🎙️ This law tells us that if you have a product of two or more numbers inside parentheses, and that whole product is raised to an exponent, then you can apply that exponent to each number in the product individually. So, (a times b) to the power 'm' becomes 'a' to the power 'm' multiplied by 'b' to the power 'm'.

Law 5: Power of a Quotient ((a/b)^m)

  • When a quotient is raised to a power, both the numerator and the denominator are raised to that power.
  • Formula: (a/b)^m = a^m / b^m
  • Example: (4/2)² = 4² / 2² = 16 / 4 = 4

🎙️ This law is very similar to the power of a product. If you have a fraction (a quotient) raised to an exponent, then both the numerator and the denominator get raised to that exponent. So, (a divided by b) to the power 'm' becomes 'a' to the power 'm' divided by 'b' to the power 'm'.

Why Do We Need Standard Form?

  • Dealing with extremely large numbers (e.g., distance to stars).
  • Dealing with extremely small numbers (e.g., size of an atom).
  • Standard form makes them easy to write, read, and compare!

🎙️ Have you ever seen numbers like 300,000,000 meters per second for the speed of light, or 0.0000000001 meters for the size of an atom? These numbers are very long and cumbersome to write. Standard form, also known as scientific notation, is a neat way to express these huge or tiny numbers in a compact and understandable way.

Standard Form: M x 10^n

  • A number is in standard form if it's written as M × 10ⁿ.
  • M is a number between 1 and 10 (1 ≤ M < 10).
  • n is an integer (positive for large numbers, negative for small numbers).
  • Example: 3,00,000,000 = 3 x 10⁸

🎙️ In standard form, a number is written as M multiplied by 10 raised to the power of n. Here, M must be a number greater than or equal to 1, but less than 10. The exponent 'n' tells us how many places the decimal point has moved. If you move the decimal to the left for a large number, 'n' is positive. If you move it to the right for a small number, 'n' is negative.

Common Mistakes to Avoid!

  • Confusing (a^m)^n with a^m * a^n (multiplying vs. adding exponents).
  • Forgetting that a^0 = 1 (not 0 or a).
  • Incorrectly handling negative exponents (e.g., 2⁻³ is not -8).
  • Applying exponent only to one term in a product (e.g., (2x)² is not 2x²).

🎙️ Even experienced mathematicians make small errors sometimes! Here are some common mistakes students make with exponents. Remember to distinguish between multiplying powers and power of a power. Don't forget the zero exponent rule, and be careful with negative exponents. Always apply the exponent to *all* parts of a product or quotient inside the parentheses.

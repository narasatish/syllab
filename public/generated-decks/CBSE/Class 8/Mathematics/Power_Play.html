What are Exponents?

  • A shortcut for repeated multiplication.
  • Shows how many times a number is multiplied by itself.
  • Consists of a 'base' and an 'exponent'.

🎙️ Exponents are like a shorthand for when you have to multiply the same number many times. Instead of writing 2 * 2 * 2 * 2, we can write it in a much neater way using exponents. It helps us express numbers more compactly.

Parts of an Exponent

  • Base: The number being multiplied.
  • Exponent: How many times the base is multiplied.
  • Example: In 2^3, '2' is the base, '3' is the exponent.

🎙️ Look at the example 2^3. Here, '2' is our base – the number we start with. The small '3' on top is the exponent, telling us to multiply the base '2' by itself three times. So, 2^3 means 2 multiplied by 2, multiplied by 2 again, which equals 8.

Positive Power Play!

  • When the exponent is a positive whole number (1, 2, 3...).
  • a^n = a × a × a × ... (n times).
  • Example: 5^2 = 5 × 5 = 25.

🎙️ When we have a positive exponent, it simply means we multiply the base by itself that many times. For instance, if you see 5^2, it's just 5 multiplied by 5. This is the most basic form of exponents and helps us understand how numbers grow quickly.

Negative Power Play!

  • When the exponent is a negative whole number (-1, -2, -3...).
  • a^-n = 1 / a^n (reciprocal of the positive exponent).
  • Example: 2^-3 = 1 / 2^3 = 1/8.

🎙️ Now, what happens if the exponent is negative? A negative exponent doesn't mean the number itself is negative. It means we take the reciprocal of the base raised to the positive version of that exponent. So, a^-n is simply 1 divided by a^n. It's about making numbers smaller, not negative.

Understanding a^-n

  • It's like taking a fraction.
  • Moves the base to the denominator.
  • Example: 4^-1 = 1/4; 4^-2 = 1/4^2 = 1/16.

🎙️ Think of negative exponents as flipping a fraction. If you have a number with a negative exponent, you can move it to the denominator of a fraction, and the exponent becomes positive. This concept is crucial for understanding very small quantities, like the size of atoms.

Law 1: Multiply Powers with Same Base

  • When multiplying powers with the same base, add the exponents.
  • Formula: a^m × a^n = a^(m+n).
  • Example: 2^3 × 2^2 = 2^(3+2) = 2^5.

🎙️ This is our first important law! If you're multiplying two numbers that have the same base but different exponents, you can simply add their exponents together while keeping the base the same. It's like combining their 'powers' into one stronger power. This saves a lot of calculation time!

✖️
Combining Strength

Apply Law 1: Simplify 3^2 × 3^4

  • Add the exponents.

🎙️ Let's use Law 1! We have 3^2 multiplied by 3^4. Since both bases are 3, we can just add the exponents 2 and 4. This gives us 6. So, the simplified expression is 3^6. Much easier than calculating 9 multiplied by 81!

Law 2: Divide Powers with Same Base

  • When dividing powers with the same base, subtract the exponents.
  • Formula: a^m / a^n = a^(m-n).
  • Example: 5^6 / 5^2 = 5^(6-2) = 5^4.

🎙️ Our second law is for division! If you're dividing two numbers with the same base, you subtract the exponent of the denominator from the exponent of the numerator. It's the opposite of multiplication, where we added exponents. This helps us simplify complex fractions with powers.

Apply Law 2: Simplify 7^5 / 7^2

  • Check if bases are the same.
  • Subtract the exponents (numerator minus denominator).
  • Write the final simplified expression.

🎙️ Here, we have 7^5 divided by 7^2. Both bases are 7, so we can apply Law 2. We subtract the exponent in the denominator (2) from the exponent in the numerator (5). This gives us 5 minus 2, which is 3. So, the simplified answer is 7^3.

Law 3: Power of a Power

  • When raising a power to another power, multiply the exponents.
  • Formula: (a^m)^n = a^(m × n).
  • Example: (3^2)^4 = 3^(2×4) = 3^8.

🎙️ This law is about a 'power within a power'. If you have a base raised to an exponent, and that whole expression is then raised to another exponent, you simply multiply the two exponents together. This creates a much larger exponent and a much larger number very quickly!

Apply Law 3: Simplify (2^3)^2

  • Identify the inner and outer exponents.
  • Multiply the two exponents.

Law 4: Product Raised to a Power

  • When a product of bases is raised to a power, apply the power to each base.
  • Formula: (ab)^m = a^m × b^m.
  • Example: (2×3)^4 = 2^4 × 3^4.

🎙️ This law is about distributing the power. If you have two different bases multiplied together, and that whole product is raised to an exponent, you can apply that exponent to each base individually. It's like the exponent is 'shared' with both parts of the product.

Law 5: Quotient Raised to a Power

  • When a quotient of bases is raised to a power, apply the power to both numerator and denominator.
  • Formula: (a/b)^m = a^m / b^m.
  • Example: (2/5)^3 = 2^3 / 5^3.

🎙️ Similar to the product rule, this law applies to division. If you have a fraction (a quotient) raised to an exponent, you can apply that exponent to both the numerator and the denominator separately. This helps in simplifying fractions that involve powers.

The Zero Power: a^0 = 1

  • Any non-zero number raised to the power of zero is 1.
  • Example: 7^0 = 1, (100)^0 = 1.
  • Why? Think of a^m / a^m = a^(m-m) = a^0. Also, a^m / a^m = 1.

🎙️ This is a very interesting and often surprising rule! Any number, except zero itself, when raised to the power of zero, always equals 1. We can understand this using the division law: if you divide a number by itself, the result is 1. Using exponents, a^m divided by a^m is a^(m-m) which is a^0. So, a^0 must be 1!

Standard Form: Writing Big & Small Numbers

  • A way to write very large or very small numbers compactly.
  • Written as k × 10^n, where 1 ≤ k < 10 and n is an integer.
  • Example: 3,000,000 = 3 × 10^6.

🎙️ Standard form, also known as scientific notation, is incredibly useful in science and engineering. It allows us to express numbers like the speed of light or the mass of an electron without writing out dozens of zeros. The key is to have one non-zero digit before the decimal point, multiplied by a power of 10.

Power Play: Key Takeaways!

  • Exponents are shorthand for repeated multiplication.
  • Positive exponents mean repeated multiplication (a^n).
  • Negative exponents mean reciprocals (a^-n = 1/a^n).
  • Five Laws: Product, Quotient, Power of a Power, Product/Quotient to a Power, Zero Exponent.

What is Area?

  • The amount of surface a 2D shape covers.
  • Measured in square units.
  • It's the space 'inside' the boundary of a flat figure.

🎙️ Area is simply the amount of flat space that a two-dimensional shape occupies. Think of it as how much paint you would need to cover a surface, or how many tiles you would need to cover a floor. It's always about the inside of a shape.

Units of Area

  • Always expressed in 'square' units.
  • Common units: square centimetres (cm²), square metres (m²).
  • Larger areas use square kilometres (km²).
  • Remember: unit × unit = unit².

🎙️ Since area involves two dimensions – length and width – its units are always squared. If you measure in centimetres, the area will be in square centimetres. If you measure in metres, it's square metres. This 'square' tells us we're talking about a surface.

Area of a Square

  • A square has four equal sides.
  • All angles are 90 degrees.
  • Formula: Side × Side or Side².

🎙️ Let's start with a simple shape: the square. Since all its sides are equal, calculating its area is straightforward. You just multiply the length of one side by itself. For example, a square with a side of 5 cm has an area of 5 cm times 5 cm, which is 25 square cm.

⏹️
Equal Sides

Example: Square Garden

  • A square garden has a side of 7 metres.
  • Find its area.

🎙️ Here's how we apply the formula. If our square garden has a side of 7 metres, we substitute this value into our formula. So, 7 metres multiplied by 7 metres gives us 49 square metres. Always remember to include the correct square units in your final answer.

1Side = 7 m
2Formula: Side²
3Area = 7 m × 7 m
4Area = 49 m²

Area of a Rectangle

  • A rectangle has opposite sides equal.
  • All angles are 90 degrees.
  • Formula: Length × Width (or Breadth).

🎙️ Next, we have the rectangle, which is very similar to a square but with possibly different lengths for its adjacent sides. Its area is found by multiplying its length by its width. This formula is fundamental and widely used.

Example: Rectangular Field

  • A field is 12 m long and 5 m wide.
  • Calculate its area.

🎙️ Let's calculate the area of a rectangular field. With a length of 12 metres and a width of 5 metres, we simply multiply these two values. The area comes out to be 60 square metres. This tells us the total ground space the field occupies.

1Length = 12 m
2Width = 5 m
3Formula: L × W
4Area = 12 m × 5 m = 60 m²

Area of a Triangle

  • A triangle is a three-sided polygon.
  • Formula: ½ × Base × Height.
  • The height must be perpendicular to the base.

🎙️ Now, let's move to triangles. The area of a triangle is half the product of its base and its corresponding height. It's like half of a rectangle or parallelogram. The key is to identify the correct base and its perpendicular height.

Base & Height in a Triangle

  • Base: Any side of the triangle.
  • Height: Perpendicular distance from the opposite vertex to the base.
  • It forms a 90-degree angle with the base.

🎙️ It's crucial to correctly identify the base and height. The base can be any side, but the height must be measured straight up from the opposite corner to that base, forming a right angle. This perpendicular distance is what we use in the formula.

Example: Triangular Plot

  • A triangular plot has a base of 10 m and height of 6 m.
  • Find its area.

🎙️ Let's apply the triangle area formula. With a base of 10 metres and a height of 6 metres, we multiply them and then divide by two. The area of this triangular plot is 30 square metres. This method works for all types of triangles, whether right-angled or not.

1Base = 10 m
2Height = 6 m
3Formula: ½ × B × H
4Area = ½ × 10 m × 6 m = 30 m²

Area of a Parallelogram

  • Opposite sides are parallel and equal.
  • Formula: Base × Height.
  • It's like a 'slanted' rectangle.

🎙️ A parallelogram is a four-sided figure where opposite sides are parallel. Its area formula is similar to a rectangle but uses its perpendicular height, not its slanted side. Imagine cutting off a triangular piece from one end and moving it to the other to form a rectangle.

Base & Height in a Parallelogram

  • Base: Any side of the parallelogram.
  • Height: Perpendicular distance between the base and its opposite parallel side.
  • Not the slanted side length!

🎙️ Just like with triangles, identifying the correct height is key for a parallelogram. The height is the perpendicular distance between the chosen base and the side parallel to it. It's not the length of the slanted side. Make sure it forms a 90-degree angle with the base.

Example: Parallelogram Window

Area of a Rhombus

  • A rhombus has all four sides equal in length.
  • Opposite angles are equal.
  • Formula: ½ × d₁ × d₂ (where d₁ and d₂ are diagonals).

🎙️ A rhombus is a special type of parallelogram where all four sides are equal. Its area is most easily calculated using the lengths of its two diagonals. The diagonals of a rhombus bisect each other at right angles.

🔸
Diagonals

Diagonals in a Rhombus

  • Diagonals are lines connecting opposite vertices.
  • They intersect at 90 degrees.
  • We need the lengths of both diagonals.

🎙️ For a rhombus, the diagonals are very important. They cut across the shape, connecting opposite corners. When using the formula, ensure you have the full length of both diagonals, not just half. The product of these lengths, divided by two, gives the area.

Example: Rhombus Kite

  • A kite is shaped like a rhombus with diagonals 12 cm and 8 cm.
  • Find its area.

🎙️ Imagine a kite in the shape of a rhombus. If its diagonals are 12 cm and 8 cm, we can quickly find its area. Multiply 12 by 8, which is 96, and then divide by 2. So, the area of the kite is 48 square centimetres.

1d₁ = 12 cm
2d₂ = 8 cm
3Formula: ½ × d₁ × d₂
4Area = ½ × 12 cm × 8 cm = 48 cm²

Area of a Trapezium

  • A trapezium has one pair of parallel sides.
  • Formula: ½ × (a + b) × h (where a, b are parallel sides, h is height).

🎙️ Finally, let's look at the trapezium. This shape has only one pair of parallel sides. Its area formula involves adding the lengths of these parallel sides, multiplying by the height, and then dividing by two. This formula is very useful for shapes with non-uniform widths.

Parallel Sides & Height in a Trapezium

  • Parallel Sides (a & b): The two sides that never meet.
  • Height (h): Perpendicular distance between the parallel sides.
  • Always measure straight across!

🎙️ For a trapezium, it's vital to correctly identify the two parallel sides, labeled 'a' and 'b'. The height 'h' is the perpendicular distance between these two parallel sides. It's the shortest distance straight across, not a slanted side.

Example: Trapezoidal Field

  • A field is shaped like a trapezium with parallel sides 15 m and 9 m, and height 7 m.
  • Find its area.

🎙️ Let's calculate the area of a trapezoidal field. First, add the lengths of the parallel sides (15 + 9 = 24). Then, multiply this sum by the height (24 × 7 = 168). Finally, divide by two (168 / 2 = 84). So, the field's area is 84 square metres.

1a = 15 m, b = 9 m
2Height = 7 m
3Formula: ½ × (a+b) × h
4Area = ½ × (15+9) × 7 = 84 m²

Area: A Quick Recap

  • Area: Space covered by a 2D shape.
  • Units: Always square units (cm², m²).
  • Square: Side × Side
  • Rectangle: Length × Width
  • Triangle: ½ × Base × Height
  • Parallelogram: Base × Height
  • Rhombus: ½ × d₁ × d₂
  • Trapezium: ½ × (a + b) × h

What are Ratios?

  • Comparing two quantities of the same kind.
  • Expressed as a:b or a/b.
  • Units must be the same for comparison.

🎙️ A ratio is simply a way to compare two quantities. For example, if you have 3 red apples and 2 green apples, the ratio of red to green apples is 3:2. Remember, for a valid ratio, the quantities must be measured in the same units.

Writing and Reading Ratios

  • The order of quantities matters (a:b is different from b:a).
  • Can represent part-to-part or part-to-whole relationships.

🎙️ When we write a ratio like 3:2, it means '3 to 2'. The first number corresponds to the first quantity mentioned, and the second number to the second. We can also express ratios as fractions, like 3/2.

Simplifying Ratios to their Simplest Form

  • Divide both parts by their Highest Common Factor (HCF).
  • Similar to simplifying fractions.
  • Makes ratios easier to understand and compare.

🎙️ Just like fractions, ratios can often be simplified. To do this, find the Highest Common Factor (HCF) of the two numbers in the ratio and divide both by it. This gives us the ratio in its simplest and most understandable form.

1Start with ratio 10:15
2Find HCF of 10 and 15 (it's 5)
3Divide both by 5
4Simplified ratio is 2:3

What is Proportion?

  • Equality of two ratios.
  • Shows a consistent relationship between quantities.
  • Expressed as a:b :: c:d or a/b = c/d.

🎙️ Now that we know about ratios, let's talk about proportion. Proportion is when two ratios are equal to each other. It's like saying, 'The relationship between these two things is the same as the relationship between those two other things.'

Checking for Proportion

  • To check if a:b and c:d are in proportion, see if a/b = c/d.
  • Alternatively, simplify both ratios and compare them.
  • Example: 2:3 and 4:6 are in proportion because 2/3 = 4/6.

🎙️ To check if two ratios are in proportion, you can simplify them or cross-multiply. If their simplified forms are identical, or if their cross-products are equal, then they form a proportion. This means their relative sizes are the same.

Extremes and Means in a Proportion

  • In a proportion a:b :: c:d, 'a' and 'd' are called the Extremes.
  • 'b' and 'c' are called the Means.
  • Extremes are the outer terms, Means are the inner terms.

🎙️ Every proportion has four terms. The first and fourth terms are called the 'extremes' because they are on the outer edges. The second and third terms are called the 'means' because they are in the middle. This distinction is important for a key property.

The Fundamental Property of Proportion

  • Product of Extremes = Product of Means.
  • If a:b :: c:d, then a × d = b × c.
  • This property is crucial for finding unknown terms.

🎙️ This is a golden rule for proportions! If four numbers are in proportion, the product of the extremes will always be equal to the product of the means. This property allows us to solve for any missing term in a proportion.

1Given: 2:3 :: 4:6
2Extremes: 2, 6
3Means: 3, 4
42 x 6 = 12, 3 x 4 = 12
5Product of Extremes = Product of Means!

Real-Life Example: Scaling Maps with Proportion

  • A map scale of 1:100,000 means 1 cm on map = 100,000 cm (1 km) in reality.
  • If 5 cm on a map, actual distance is 5 x 100,000 cm = 5 km.
  • Proportion helps maintain accurate distances and scale.

🎙️ Maps are a perfect example of proportion! The scale of a map is a ratio that tells you how much the real world has been shrunk to fit on the paper. Using this ratio, you can find the actual distance between two places by measuring their distance on the map.

Worked Example 1: Finding the Missing Term

  • Find the value of x if 3:5 :: x:20.
  • Using Product of Extremes = Product of Means:
  • 3 × 20 = 5 × x
  • 60 = 5x, so x = 12.

🎙️ Let's put the property of proportion into practice. Here, we have an unknown value, 'x'. By applying the rule that the product of the extremes equals the product of the means, we can easily set up an equation and solve for 'x'. See how useful it is?

Types of Proportion

  • There are two main types of proportional relationships:
  • 1. Direct Proportion
  • 2. Inverse Proportion

🎙️ Proportional reasoning isn't just one thing; there are two main types of relationships we'll explore: direct and inverse proportion. Understanding the difference between them is key to solving real-world problems. Let's look at each one.

Understanding Direct Proportion

  • As one quantity increases, the other quantity also increases.
  • As one quantity decreases, the other quantity also decreases.
  • The ratio between them remains constant.

🎙️ In direct proportion, two quantities move in the same direction. Think about buying more of something – if you buy more apples, the total cost increases. The ratio of cost to quantity remains the same, which is the price per apple.

⬆️⬆️
Both Up, Both Down

The Constant Ratio in Direct Proportion

  • If x and y are in direct proportion, then x/y = k (a constant).
  • This means x₁/y₁ = x₂/y₂ for different values.
  • Example: More fuel consumed, more distance covered.

🎙️ The key characteristic of direct proportion is that the ratio of the two quantities is always constant. If you double one quantity, the other also doubles. This constant ratio, often called 'k', helps us find unknown values easily.

Real-Life Example: Direct Proportion - Cost and Quantity

  • If 1 pen costs ₹10, then 2 pens cost ₹20, 3 pens cost ₹30.
  • As the number of pens increases, the total cost increases.
  • The ratio (Cost/Pens) is always ₹10/pen (constant).

🎙️ This is a very common scenario. The more items you buy, the more money you spend. The cost of each item is the constant ratio here. This relationship is directly proportional because both quantities (number of items and total cost) increase or decrease together.

Worked Example 2: Solving Direct Proportion Problems

  • If 5 kg of sugar costs ₹200, what will 8 kg cost?
  • Let the cost of 8 kg be x.
  • Set up proportion: 5/200 = 8/x (Direct Proportion)
  • Cross-multiply: 5x = 200 × 8 => 5x = 1600 => x = ₹320.

🎙️ Here's how to solve a direct proportion problem. We set up a proportion where the ratios of quantity to cost are equal. Then, we use cross-multiplication (product of extremes = product of means) to find the unknown cost. Simple, right?

Understanding Inverse Proportion

  • As one quantity increases, the other quantity decreases.
  • As one quantity decreases, the other quantity increases.
  • Their product remains constant.

🎙️ Now, let's look at inverse proportion. Here, the quantities move in opposite directions. If one quantity goes up, the other goes down. Think about building a wall: if you have more workers, it takes less time to finish the job.

⬆️⬇️
Opposite Change

The Constant Product in Inverse Proportion

  • If x and y are in inverse proportion, then x × y = k (a constant).
  • This means x₁y₁ = x₂y₂ for different values.
  • Example: More workers means less time to complete a task.

🎙️ For inverse proportion, it's not the ratio, but the product of the two quantities that remains constant. If you double one quantity, the other quantity is halved. This constant product helps us solve problems where quantities have an inverse relationship.

Real-Life Example: Inverse Proportion - Speed and Time

  • If you travel at 60 km/h, a journey takes 2 hours.
  • If you travel at 120 km/h, the same journey takes 1 hour.
  • Higher speed means less time to cover the same fixed distance.

🎙️ This is a classic example. If you want to reach your destination faster, you need to increase your speed. Speed and time taken for a fixed distance are inversely proportional. Doubling your speed halves the time taken.

Worked Example 3: Solving Inverse Proportion Problems

  • 3 workers can build a wall in 10 days. How many days will 6 workers take?
  • Let the number of days for 6 workers be x.
  • Using constant product: 3 × 10 = 6 × x (Inverse Proportion)
  • 30 = 6x => x = 5 days.

🎙️ For inverse proportion problems, we use the constant product rule. The product of (number of workers × time taken) remains the same. So, we set up the equation and solve for the unknown number of days. Notice how the days decreased as workers increased.

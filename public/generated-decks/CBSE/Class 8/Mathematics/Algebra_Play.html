What is Algebra?

  • It's a branch of Math using letters (variables) for unknown numbers.
  • Helps us represent problems in a simple way.
  • Find solutions to real-world puzzles.

🎙️ Algebra is like a special language in mathematics where we use letters, often 'x' or 'y', to stand for numbers we don't know yet. It's a powerful tool that helps us translate everyday problems into mathematical statements and find their answers.

Variables and Constants

  • Variables: Letters (x, y, a) that can take different values.
  • Constants: Numbers (5, -2, 100) that have fixed values.
  • They are the building blocks of algebraic expressions.

🎙️ Think of variables as chameleons – their value can change depending on the situation. Constants, on the other hand, are like solid rocks – their value never changes. Understanding these two is fundamental to algebra.

What are Algebraic Expressions?

  • A combination of variables, constants, and operations (+, -, ×, ÷).
  • Examples: 2x + 5, y - 7, 3ab.
  • They don't have an equals sign.

🎙️ An algebraic expression is like a mathematical phrase. It combines variables, constants, and basic operations. It's important to remember that an expression doesn't have an equals sign; it just represents a value.

Terms of an Expression

  • Parts of an expression separated by '+' or '-' signs.
  • Example: In 3x + 2y - 7, the terms are 3x, 2y, and -7.
  • Each term can be a variable, a constant, or a product of both.

🎙️ When you look at an algebraic expression, the terms are the individual pieces that are being added or subtracted. Each term has its own coefficient and variable part, if any. Identifying terms correctly is key.

Like and Unlike Terms

  • Like Terms: Have the same variables raised to the same power (e.g., 3x and 5x).
  • Unlike Terms: Have different variables or different powers (e.g., 3x and 5y, or 3x and 5x²).
  • Only like terms can be added or subtracted.

🎙️ This is a super important concept! Imagine you're sorting fruits – you can add apples to apples, but you can't add apples to oranges. Similarly, in algebra, you can only combine 'like' terms. They must have the exact same variable part.

  • You buy 3 apples (a) and 2 bananas (b). Total cost: 3a + 2b.

Adding Algebraic Expressions

  • Step 1: Identify and group like terms.
  • Step 2: Add their coefficients (the numbers in front of variables).
  • Step 3: Keep the variable part the same.

🎙️ Adding expressions is straightforward once you've mastered identifying like terms. The process is simple: gather all the terms that are alike, add their numerical parts, and the variable part stays exactly as it is.

Worked Example: Addition

  • Add: (3x + 5y) and (2x - 2y)
  • Group like terms: (3x + 2x) + (5y - 2y)
  • Add coefficients: (3+2)x + (5-2)y
  • Result: 5x + 3y

🎙️ Let's walk through an example. We have two expressions. First, we put all the 'x' terms together and all the 'y' terms together. Then, we just add or subtract the numbers in front of 'x' and 'y' respectively to get our final answer.

Subtracting Algebraic Expressions

  • Step 1: Change the sign of each term in the expression being subtracted.
  • Step 2: Then, proceed as in addition (group like terms).
  • Step 3: Subtract their coefficients.

🎙️ Subtraction is similar to addition, but with one crucial extra step. When you subtract an expression, you essentially add the opposite of each term. So, change all the signs of the second expression, and then it becomes an addition problem.

Worked Example: Subtraction

  • Subtract (2x - 2y) from (3x + 5y)
  • Change signs of (2x - 2y) to (-2x + 2y)
  • Now add: (3x + 5y) + (-2x + 2y)
  • Group like terms: (3x - 2x) + (5y + 2y)
  • Result: x + 7y

🎙️ Here's how we subtract. Notice how the '2x' becomes '-2x' and '-2y' becomes '+2y'. After this sign change, it's just like our addition examples. Group the 'x' terms and 'y' terms, and perform the operations.

What is an Equation?

  • A statement that two algebraic expressions are equal.
  • Always contains an equals sign (=).
  • Example: 2x + 5 = 11.
  • The goal is often to find the value of the variable.

🎙️ An equation is like a balanced scale. Whatever is on one side must be exactly equal to what's on the other side. The equals sign is the key feature here, and our main task is usually to find the unknown value that makes this balance true.

Left Hand Side (LHS) and Right Hand Side (RHS)

  • LHS: The expression on the left of the equals sign.
  • RHS: The expression on the right of the equals sign.
  • For an equation to be true, LHS must equal RHS.

🎙️ Every equation has two sides: the Left Hand Side and the Right Hand Side. Think of them as the two pans of a weighing scale. To keep the scale balanced, any operation you do on one side, you must also do on the other.

Solving Simple Equations: Trial & Error

  • Substitute different values for the variable.
  • Check if LHS equals RHS.
  • Keep trying until you find the correct value.

🎙️ The trial and error method is like playing a guessing game. You pick a number, put it into the equation, and see if it works. If not, you try another number. It's good for simple equations, but can be slow for complex ones.

Solving Simple Equations: Balancing Method

  • Perform the same operation on both sides of the equation.
  • Goal: Isolate the variable on one side.
  • Inverse operations are key (e.g., add to undo subtract, divide to undo multiply).

🎙️ This is the most efficient method. Imagine your balanced scale again. If you add 5 to one side, you must add 5 to the other to keep it balanced. We use inverse operations to 'undo' what's happening to the variable, step by step.

3Isolate Variable

Worked Example: Solving an Equation

  • Solve: 2x + 3 = 9
  • Subtract 3 from both sides: 2x + 3 - 3 = 9 - 3 => 2x = 6
  • Divide both sides by 2: 2x / 2 = 6 / 2 => x = 3
  • Check: 2(3) + 3 = 6 + 3 = 9 (LHS = RHS)

🎙️ Let's solve this equation using the balancing method. First, we get rid of the '+3' by subtracting 3 from both sides. Then, we get rid of the 'multiply by 2' by dividing both sides by 2. Finally, we check our answer to ensure it's correct.

Real-Life Example: Age Problem

  • Ravi is 5 years older than his sister, Siya.
  • Their combined age is 25 years.
  • Let Siya's age be 'x'. Then Ravi's age is 'x + 5'.
  • Equation: x + (x + 5) = 25 => 2x + 5 = 25.
  • Solving: 2x = 20 => x = 10. Siya is 10, Ravi is 15.

🎙️ Algebra is great for solving age-related puzzles! By representing Siya's unknown age as 'x', we can write Ravi's age in terms of 'x'. Then, we form an equation based on their combined age and solve for 'x' to find their actual ages.

Using Algebra in Geometry

  • Find the perimeter of a rectangle with length 'l' and width 'w': P = 2l + 2w.
  • If length is (x+2) and width is x, perimeter P = 2(x+2) + 2x = 4x + 4.
  • Algebra helps us express relationships and solve for unknown dimensions.

🎙️ Algebra isn't just for numbers; it's super useful in geometry too! We can use variables to represent unknown lengths or widths of shapes. This allows us to write formulas and solve for those unknown dimensions when given the perimeter or area.

Forming Equations from Statements

  • Read the problem carefully to identify the unknown quantity.
  • Assign a variable (e.g., x) to the unknown.
  • Translate the words into mathematical operations and relationships.
  • Example: 'Twice a number increased by 5 is 15' becomes 2x + 5 = 15.

🎙️ One of the most powerful applications of algebra is translating word problems into equations. This requires careful reading and understanding of keywords. 'Increased by' means addition, 'twice' means multiplication by 2, and 'is' means equals.

Worked Example: Word Problem

  • Problem: The sum of two consecutive numbers is 35. Find the numbers.
  • Let the first number be x.
  • The next consecutive number is x + 1.
  • Equation: x + (x + 1) = 35 => 2x + 1 = 35.
  • Solve: 2x = 34 => x = 17. The numbers are 17 and 18.

🎙️ Let's solve a classic word problem. We start by defining our unknown, the first number, as 'x'. Then, we express the second number in terms of 'x'. Setting up the equation correctly is half the battle; solving it then becomes straightforward.

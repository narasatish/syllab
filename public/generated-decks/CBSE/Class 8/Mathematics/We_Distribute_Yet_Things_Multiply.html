The Distributive Property

  • A way to simplify calculations.
  • Links multiplication with addition or subtraction.
  • a × (b + c) = (a × b) + (a × c)

🎙️ This property is a cornerstone of algebra. It tells us how multiplication interacts with addition or subtraction inside parentheses. You 'distribute' the multiplication to each term inside the bracket.

1a × (b + c)
2= (a × b) + (a × c)

Distributive Property with Numbers

  • Let's calculate 5 × (3 + 2).
  • Method 1: Add first, then multiply: 5 × 5 = 25.
  • Method 2: Distribute first: (5 × 3) + (5 × 2) = 15 + 10 = 25.

🎙️ See, both methods give us the same answer! The distributive property provides an alternative way to solve problems, which can be very useful, especially when dealing with larger numbers or variables.

Real-Life: Sharing Chocolates

  • You have 3 friends.
  • You want to give each friend 2 dark chocolates and 1 milk chocolate.
  • Total chocolates = 3 × (2 + 1) = 3 × 3 = 9.

🎙️ Here, '3' is distributed to '2 dark chocolates' and '1 milk chocolate'. So, each friend gets 3 chocolates, and for 3 friends, that's 9 chocolates in total. It's a simple everyday example of distribution.

Distributive Property with Rational Numbers

  • Applies to fractions and decimals too!
  • Example: 1/2 × (1/3 + 1/4)
  • = (1/2 × 1/3) + (1/2 × 1/4)
  • = 1/6 + 1/8 = 4/24 + 3/24 = 7/24

🎙️ The beauty of mathematical properties is that they often extend to different types of numbers. The distributive property works perfectly with rational numbers, making complex fraction calculations easier to manage.

What are Exponents?

  • A shorthand for repeated multiplication.
  • Base: The number being multiplied.
  • Exponent (Power): How many times the base is multiplied by itself.
  • Example: 2³ = 2 × 2 × 2

🎙️ Think of it as a compact way to write very long multiplication problems. The base is the number doing the multiplying, and the exponent tells us how many times it's asked to do its job.

Rule 1: Multiplying Powers (Same Base)

  • When multiplying powers with the same base, add the exponents.
  • a^m × a^n = a^(m+n)
  • Example: 2³ × 2² = (2×2×2) × (2×2) = 2⁵

🎙️ This rule makes sense if you expand the terms. You're just counting the total number of times the base is multiplied. So, three 2s multiplied by two 2s gives a total of five 2s multiplied.

Example: Bacteria Growth

  • A bacteria colony doubles every hour.
  • If you start with 2¹ bacteria.
  • After 1 hour: 2¹ × 2¹ = 2² bacteria.
  • After 2 hours: 2² × 2¹ = 2³ bacteria.

🎙️ This is a perfect real-world example of how things multiply exponentially. Each time period, the existing amount multiplies again, leading to rapid growth, just like adding exponents when bases are the same.

Rule 2: Dividing Powers (Same Base)

  • When dividing powers with the same base, subtract the exponents.
  • a^m ÷ a^n = a^(m-n)
  • Example: 5⁵ ÷ 5² = (5×5×5×5×5) / (5×5) = 5³

🎙️ Similar to multiplication, if you expand the terms, you'll see that common factors in the numerator and denominator cancel out, leaving you with fewer factors of the base, hence subtracting the exponents.

Rule 3: Power of a Power

  • When raising a power to another power, multiply the exponents.
  • (a^m)^n = a^(m×n)
  • Example: (3²)⁴ = (3×3) × (3×3) × (3×3) × (3×3) = 3⁸

🎙️ Here, you're repeatedly multiplying a group of multiplications. So, if 3² means 3 multiplied by itself twice, then (3²)⁴ means that '3 times 3' is multiplied by itself four times, leading to 3 multiplied 8 times.

Rule 4: Power of a Product

  • When a product is raised to a power, each factor is raised to that power.
  • (a × b)^m = a^m × b^m
  • Example: (2 × 3)³ = 6³ = 216. Also, 2³ × 3³ = 8 × 27 = 216.

🎙️ This rule shows that the power distributes over multiplication. If you have a product inside parentheses, and it's raised to an exponent, you can apply that exponent to each individual factor.

Rule 5: Power of a Quotient

  • When a quotient is raised to a power, both numerator and denominator are raised to that power.
  • (a / b)^m = a^m / b^m
  • Example: (2/3)² = 2²/3² = 4/9

🎙️ Similar to the power of a product, the power distributes over division. Both the top and bottom parts of the fraction get the same exponent, making it easier to calculate.

Rule 6: Zero Exponent

  • Any non-zero number raised to the power of zero is 1.
  • a⁰ = 1 (where a ≠ 0)
  • Example: 7⁰ = 1, (x+y)⁰ = 1

🎙️ This might seem counter-intuitive at first. But if you consider the division rule (a^m / a^m = a^(m-m) = a^0), and we know a^m / a^m = 1, then it logically follows that a^0 must be 1.

Rule 7: Negative Exponents

  • A negative exponent means taking the reciprocal of the base raised to the positive exponent.
  • a^-m = 1 / a^m
  • Example: 5⁻² = 1 / 5² = 1/25

🎙️ Negative exponents don't mean the number becomes negative; they mean you 'flip' the base and make the exponent positive. It's about moving the term from the numerator to the denominator, or vice versa.

Putting it All Together: Compound Growth

  • Many real-world situations involve repeated multiplication.
  • Population growth, compound interest, viral spread.
  • Initial amount distributes, then multiplies over time.

🎙️ This is where our chapter title truly shines. You start with something, it distributes or is acted upon, and then that new amount multiplies again and again. Exponents are key to understanding this.

Example: Savings Account

  • You deposit ₹1000 at 10% annual interest.
  • After 1 year: ₹1000 × (1 + 0.10) = ₹1100.
  • After 2 years: ₹1000 × (1 + 0.10)² = ₹1210.
  • The interest 'distributes' and then multiplies the principal.

🎙️ Compound interest is a fantastic example. The interest earned each year is added to the principal, and then *that new total* earns interest the next year. It's multiplication building upon multiplication.

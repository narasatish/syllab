What is Income?

  • Income is the total money a person or business receives.
  • It's earned from selling goods, services, or wages.
  • It's the 'money inflow' into your business.

🎙️ So, formally, Income is all the money that flows into a business. For a shop, it's the money from selling products. For a doctor, it's the fees from patients. It's the primary way a business gets money.

What is Expense?

  • Expense is the money a person or business spends.
  • It's for buying goods, services, or running costs.
  • It's the 'money outflow' from your business.

🎙️ An expense is any cost incurred in the process of generating income. To run a business, you need to buy things, pay bills, and sometimes even pay people. All these outgoing payments are expenses.

Types of Expenses: Fixed vs. Variable

  • **Fixed Expenses**: Stay the same every month (e.g., rent, salaries).
  • **Variable Expenses**: Change based on sales or production (e.g., cost of stock, delivery charges).

🎙️ Expenses aren't all the same. Some, like rent, are constant regardless of how much Mr. Sharma sells. These are fixed. Others, like how much stock he buys, depend on how many customers he expects, making them variable.

What is Profit?

  • Profit is the money left after all expenses are paid.
  • It's calculated as Income minus Expenses.
  • If Income is GREATER THAN Expenses, there is Profit!

🎙️ Profit is the ultimate goal for most businesses. It's what's left over after you've covered all your costs. If Mr. Sharma's income is more than his expenses, he makes a profit, which means his business is successful.

What is Loss?

  • Loss occurs when expenses are MORE than income.
  • It means the business spent more than it earned.
  • If Income is LESS THAN Expenses, there is Loss!

🎙️ Unfortunately, businesses can also experience a loss. This happens when the money going out (expenses) is more than the money coming in (income). A loss indicates that the business is not covering its costs and needs to make changes.

Calculating Mr. Sharma's Profit (Simple)

  • Income from sales: ₹5000
  • Expenses (stock, rent): ₹3000
  • Profit = Income - Expenses = ₹5000 - ₹3000 = ₹2000

🎙️ Let's apply the formula to Mr. Sharma. If he earned ₹5000 and spent ₹3000, his profit is the difference. The ₹2000 is his reward for running the business successfully, and he can use it to grow or save.

Problem: Priya's Juice Stall

  • Priya runs a small juice stall. Her daily sales (Income) are ₹1200.
  • She spends ₹400 on fruits, ₹150 on cups, and ₹50 on ice.
  • What is Priya's daily profit?

🎙️ Here's a scenario. Priya is a young entrepreneur. She has a clear income figure, and we know her daily expenses. Can you mentally apply the steps we just discussed to find her daily profit?

🥤
19 / 30

Solution: Priya's Juice Stall Profit

  • Total Income = ₹1200
  • Total Expenses = ₹400 (fruits) + ₹150 (cups) + ₹50 (ice) = ₹600
  • Profit = Income - Expenses = ₹1200 - ₹600 = ₹600

🎙️ Following the steps, we first identify the total income. Then, we add up all the individual expenses to get the total expenses. Finally, we subtract total expenses from total income to find Priya's profit of ₹600 for the day.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Confusing Income with Profit: Income is total money in, Profit is what's left.
  • Forgetting to include all expenses: Small costs add up!
  • Not understanding Loss as negative profit: It means spending more than earning.

🎙️ Students often make these mistakes. Remember, income is just one side of the coin; profit considers both sides. Always be thorough in listing all expenses, no matter how small, as they impact the final result.

Recap: Key Definitions

  • **Income**: Total money received by a business.
  • **Expense**: Total money spent by a business.
  • **Profit**: Occurs when Income > Expenses.
  • **Loss**: Occurs when Expenses > Income.

🎙️ Let's quickly review the core concepts. Income is what comes in, expense is what goes out. The comparison between these two determines if a business makes a profit or incurs a loss. These are the building blocks of financial literacy.

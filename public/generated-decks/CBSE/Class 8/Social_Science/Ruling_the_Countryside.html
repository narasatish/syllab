The Company Becomes the Diwan: A Game Changer

  • After the Battle of Buxar (1764), the Mughal Emperor appointed the Company as the Diwan of Bengal.
  • This gave the Company vast powers over the financial administration of Bengal.
  • Analogy: Imagine a local government suddenly getting the power to collect all taxes directly.

🎙️ The Battle of Buxar was a pivotal moment. The Company's victory led to the Mughal Emperor Shah Alam II granting them the Diwani of Bengal. This meant they were officially responsible for collecting revenue and administering justice in Bengal, Bihar, and Orissa. It was a huge leap from being mere traders to becoming political and financial administrators.

What Diwani Really Meant: Power and Purpose

  • The Diwani allowed the Company to use the vast revenue resources of Bengal.
  • This revenue was crucial for funding their growing military expenses in India.
  • It also helped them purchase Indian goods for export, reducing reliance on silver imports.

🎙️ Before Diwani, the Company had to import gold and silver from Britain to buy goods in India. With the Diwani, they could finance their purchases, maintain their army, and expand their administration using the revenues generated from Bengal itself. This was a massive economic advantage and a fundamental shift in their operations.

Early Challenges: The Bengal Famine of 1770

  • The Company's initial focus was on maximising revenue, often ignoring local conditions.
  • This contributed to the devastating Bengal Famine of 1770, which killed millions.
  • One-third of the population of Bengal perished, highlighting the fragility of the economy.

🎙️ The Company's insatiable demand for higher revenue, coupled with existing agricultural vulnerabilities, led to one of the worst famines in Indian history. The Company's policies did not provide relief, and the famine severely depleted the agricultural population, further impacting revenue collection in the long run.

Why Revenue Was Crucial: The Company's Lifeline

  • To buy cotton and silk textiles from India for export to Britain.
  • To maintain a large army and fight wars against other Indian rulers and European rivals.
  • To fund the construction of administrative buildings and infrastructure.

🎙️ The Company was constantly expanding its influence and fighting wars. All this required immense funds. The revenue from Bengal became the primary source to sustain their military machine, support their trade, and establish their administrative apparatus, effectively making India pay for its own conquest and exploitation.

The Need for a Fixed Revenue System: Stability & Predictability

  • The Company faced challenges with fluctuating revenue collection and administrative costs.
  • They wanted a stable, predictable income to plan for long-term expansion and trade.
  • They also believed that a fixed system would encourage agricultural improvement.

🎙️ After the initial chaos and the famine, the Company realised that constantly negotiating or extracting revenue was inefficient and unpredictable. They needed a system that guaranteed a regular income, regardless of yearly harvest variations, and ideally, one that would also motivate landholders to invest in agriculture.

Introducing the Permanent Settlement (1793)

  • Introduced by Lord Cornwallis in 1793 in Bengal, Bihar, and Orissa.
  • Analogy: Imagine a long-term lease agreement where your rent is fixed forever.
  • The Company hoped this would create a loyal class of Indian landowners.

🎙️ Lord Cornwallis, the then Governor-General, believed that a fixed revenue demand would provide security to both the Company and the landowners. It was a radical step, aiming to create a stable economic base and foster a class of loyal supporters for the British Raj.

⚖️
A New Land Law

Key Features of Permanent Settlement: Zamindars as Owners

  • Zamindars were recognised as the owners of the land, not just revenue collectors.
  • They had to pay a fixed amount of revenue to the Company, permanently.
  • If Zamindars failed to pay, their zamindari (land) could be auctioned off.

🎙️ Under this system, the Zamindars, who were traditionally revenue collectors, were elevated to the status of landowners. This meant they were responsible for collecting rent from the peasants and paying a fixed sum to the Company. The burden of collection and the risk of non-payment now fell squarely on the Zamindars.

Zamindar's New Role

The Role of Zamindars: Expected vs. Reality

  • Expected: Zamindars would invest in land improvement, leading to increased production.
  • Reality: Many Zamindars found the fixed revenue demand too high, especially initially.
  • They often focused on extracting maximum rent from peasants rather than improving land.

🎙️ The Company's expectation was that by giving Zamindars ownership, they would have an incentive to improve the land, leading to higher agricultural output. However, the initial revenue demand was set very high, and many Zamindars struggled to meet it, especially during poor harvests. Their primary focus became survival, often at the expense of the peasants.

🧐
A Flawed Expectation

Impact on Zamindars: Winners and Losers

  • Many Zamindars lost their lands early on due to failure to pay the high revenue.
  • New buyers, often urban merchants, became Zamindars, sometimes as absentee landlords.
  • Over time, successful Zamindars grew wealthy as agricultural prices and production increased.

🎙️ The Permanent Settlement created a new class of Zamindars. While some original Zamindars were ruined, others, especially those who acquired land later, thrived as the value of land and agricultural produce grew, while their payment to the Company remained fixed. This led to a stark contrast in fortunes.

Impact on Peasants (Ryots): Exploitation and Debt

  • Peasants found the rents they had to pay to Zamindars oppressively high.
  • They were often forced to take loans from moneylenders to pay rent, leading to debt cycles.
  • Loss of traditional rights and eviction became common for non-payment.

🎙️ For the actual cultivators, the Permanent Settlement was largely a curse. They were at the mercy of the Zamindars, who could demand exorbitant rents and evict them without notice. This instability and economic pressure pushed many peasants into chronic debt, leading to widespread distress.

Why Permanent Settlement Failed (for the Company)

  • Company lost out on increased revenue as agricultural prices rose; their share was fixed.
  • Zamindars were often neglectful of land improvement, as they focused on rent collection.
  • It created widespread discontent among the peasantry, leading to social unrest.

🎙️ While initially seen as a solution, the Permanent Settlement proved problematic for the Company in the long run. As the economy grew and land became more productive, the Company's fixed revenue meant they couldn't benefit from the increased prosperity. This led them to seek new revenue systems in other parts of India.

New Systems of Revenue: Adapting to Different Regions

  • The Permanent Settlement was not extended to all regions of British India.
  • Company officials realised the need for more flexible and direct revenue systems.
  • They wanted systems that allowed for periodic revision of revenue demands.

🎙️ As the British expanded their territories into South and North-Western India, they encountered different landholding patterns and social structures. The lessons learned from the Permanent Settlement, particularly its inflexibility, prompted them to devise new methods of revenue collection tailored to these regions.

The Mahalwari System (1822): The Village as a Unit

  • Introduced by Holt Mackenzie in the North-Western Provinces (parts of UP, MP, Punjab).
  • Analogy: Instead of individual landlords, the entire village (Mahal) collectively pays revenue.
  • The revenue demand was revised periodically, unlike the Permanent Settlement.

🎙️ In North India, villages often had strong community ties and collective landholding traditions. The Mahalwari system acknowledged this, making the village headman responsible for collecting revenue from all farmers in the 'mahal' or village and paying it to the Company. This system allowed for revenue reassessment, giving the Company more flexibility.

🏘️
Village-based Revenue

Key Features of Mahalwari: Periodic Revisions

  • Revenue was assessed on the entire village (Mahal) and its lands.
  • The village headman (or a group) was responsible for collection and payment.
  • The revenue demand was not fixed permanently but revised periodically, usually every 20-30 years.

🎙️ This system addressed the Company's concern about fixed revenue. By allowing periodic revisions, they could increase their share as agricultural output or prices rose. However, the burden on village headmen and the potential for increased demand still created pressure on the farmers.

The Ryotwari System (1820s): Direct Deal with Cultivators

  • Introduced by Captain Alexander Read and Thomas Munro in Southern India (Madras, Bombay Presidencies).
  • Analogy: The government directly collects taxes from you, the farmer, without intermediaries.
  • Aimed to eliminate Zamindars and deal directly with the 'ryots' (cultivators).

🎙️ In Southern India, there were no traditional Zamindars on the same scale as Bengal. The Ryotwari system was designed to deal directly with the individual cultivators or 'ryots.' This was seen as a more equitable system, as it removed the exploitative Zamindar intermediary.

Key Features of Ryotwari: Flexible and Direct

  • Revenue was settled directly with the individual ryot (cultivator).
  • Land was surveyed carefully, and revenue was assessed based on land quality and size.
  • The revenue demand was not fixed but subject to periodic revision, often every 30 years.

🎙️ The Ryotwari system involved extensive land surveys to accurately assess the revenue potential of each plot. While it removed the Zamindar, the revenue demand itself was often very high, and the Company officials, driven by the desire to increase revenue, could be oppressive in their collection methods, leading to similar distress for the ryots.

Compare Revenue Systems: Permanent, Mahalwari, Ryotwari

  • Permanent: Zamindar as owner, fixed revenue, no revision (Bengal).
  • Mahalwari: Village as unit, headman collects, revised periodically (North-West).
  • Ryotwari: Direct with ryot, surveyed land, revised periodically (South).

🎙️ Understanding the differences between these three systems is crucial. Each was an attempt by the British to maximise revenue while adapting to local conditions, but all ultimately placed a heavy burden on the Indian peasantry and transformed land relations.

Crops for Europe: India as a Raw Material Supplier

  • The Industrial Revolution in Britain created a huge demand for raw materials.
  • India was seen as a prime source for crops like opium, indigo, cotton, jute, and tea.
  • Farmers were often forced or coerced to cultivate these 'cash crops' instead of food grains.

🎙️ Britain's booming industries needed a steady supply of raw materials. India, with its vast agricultural potential, became a crucial supplier. This shifted Indian agriculture from subsistence farming to commercial farming, often at the expense of food security and local needs.

The Indigo Cultivation System: A Cycle of Debt

  • Indigo was a natural dye highly demanded in Europe for textile industries.
  • Planters (often Europeans) forced peasants to grow indigo on their best lands.
  • Peasants were given advances (loans) at low rates, trapping them in a cycle of debt.

🎙️ Indigo cultivation was particularly exploitative. Planters would give small loans to peasants, forcing them to grow indigo on prime land, which could otherwise be used for food crops. The prices offered for indigo were very low, and the advances often kept peasants perpetually indebted, unable to switch crops.

The Indigo Rebellion (1859-60): Peasants Fight Back

  • Frustrated by exploitation, peasants in Bengal refused to grow indigo.
  • They attacked indigo factories with spears, swords, and bows and arrows.
  • Women also participated, fighting with pots and pans.

🎙️ The Indigo Rebellion was a powerful testament to peasant resistance. Driven to desperation, the ryots of Bengal collectively decided to defy the planters. This was a spontaneous uprising, but it quickly gained momentum and showed the collective strength of the oppressed.

The Indigo Rebellion: Causes, Events, Significance

  • Causes: Forced cultivation, low prices, debt, planter brutality.
  • Events: Mass refusal to grow indigo, attacks on factories, support from local Zamindars/headmen.
  • Significance: Led to the Indigo Commission, exposed planter atrocities, and eventually ended indigo cultivation in Bengal.

🎙️ The rebellion forced the British government to intervene. The Indigo Commission was set up, which largely sided with the peasants, condemning the planters' practices. This led to the decline of indigo cultivation in Bengal, demonstrating that collective action could bring change.

Recap: Ruling the Countryside

  • Company became Diwan, gaining financial control over Bengal.
  • Introduced Permanent Settlement (Zamindars), Mahalwari (villages), and Ryotwari (ryots) to maximise revenue.
  • Forced cultivation of cash crops like indigo led to exploitation and peasant revolts.

🎙️ In summary, the British East India Company transformed itself from a trading power to a ruling authority by gaining Diwani rights. They implemented diverse revenue policies, each with distinct features and profound impacts on the Indian agrarian society. The push for cash crops like indigo led to immense hardship, culminating in powerful peasant uprisings that eventually brought some relief and exposed the exploitative nature of colonial rule.

What is Combustion?

  • A chemical process
  • Substance reacts with oxygen
  • Produces heat and light

🎙️ Combustion is essentially a fancy word for burning. It's a chemical reaction where a substance rapidly combines with oxygen from the air. This reaction releases a lot of energy, which we usually see as heat and sometimes light.

Combustible Substances

  • Materials that burn
  • React with oxygen
  • Examples: wood, paper, LPG

🎙️ Any substance that can undergo combustion is called a combustible substance. Think about things you often see burning, like wood in a campfire or the gas in your kitchen stove. These are all examples of materials that readily react with oxygen.

Non-Combustible Substances

  • Materials that do not burn
  • Do not react with oxygen
  • Examples: stone, glass, water

🎙️ On the other hand, non-combustible substances are materials that do not burn or support combustion. You can try to light a stone or a piece of glass, but they simply won't catch fire. They don't have the chemical properties to react with oxygen in that way.

Conditions Necessary for Combustion

  • Fuel (combustible substance)
  • Air (oxygen supply)
  • Heat (ignition temperature)

🎙️ For combustion to occur, three essential conditions must be met simultaneously. We need a combustible material, a continuous supply of oxygen from the air, and enough heat to start the burning process. If any one of these is missing, combustion will not happen.

✅
3 Essentials

Condition 1: Presence of Fuel

  • The substance that burns
  • Source of energy
  • Examples: petrol, coal, paper

🎙️ The first condition is the presence of a fuel. This is the material that will actually burn and release energy. Without something to burn, there can be no fire, simple as that. Different fuels burn in different ways and at different rates.

⛽
The Burnable

Condition 2: Presence of Air (Oxygen)

  • Oxygen supports combustion
  • Air provides oxygen
  • No oxygen, no fire

🎙️ The second crucial condition is the presence of oxygen, usually supplied by the air around us. Oxygen is the reactant that combines with the fuel during combustion. If you cut off the oxygen supply, even a burning fire will quickly extinguish.

Oxygen Supply

Condition 3: Ignition Temperature

  • Minimum temperature required
  • To start burning
  • Different for different substances

🎙️ The third condition is reaching the ignition temperature. This is the lowest temperature at which a substance will catch fire and continue to burn. You can't just put a matchstick near wood; it needs to reach its specific ignition temperature to ignite.

Example: Paper vs. Wood

  • Paper catches fire easily
  • Wood needs more heat
  • Different ignition temperatures

🎙️ Think about trying to light a piece of paper versus a log of wood. Paper catches fire much more quickly because its ignition temperature is lower. Wood, being denser, requires more heat to reach its higher ignition temperature before it begins to burn.

Inflammable Substances

  • Very low ignition temperature
  • Catch fire easily
  • Examples: petrol, alcohol, LPG

🎙️ Substances that have a very low ignition temperature and can easily catch fire with a flame are called inflammable substances. These materials are extremely dangerous and must be handled with great care due to their high flammability. Always look for warning labels.

Types of Combustion

  • Rapid combustion
  • Spontaneous combustion
  • Explosion

🎙️ Combustion isn't always the same; it can occur in different forms depending on how quickly and intensely the reaction happens. We can broadly classify combustion into three main types: rapid, spontaneous, and explosion, each with distinct characteristics.

Type 1: Rapid Combustion

  • Fast burning
  • Produces heat and light quickly
  • Example: burning LPG gas

🎙️ Rapid combustion is what we commonly observe in our daily lives. It's when a substance burns quickly, producing heat and light in a short amount of time. The burning of cooking gas (LPG) in your kitchen stove is a perfect example of rapid combustion.

Type 2: Spontaneous Combustion

  • No external heat source needed
  • Ignites on its own
  • Example: forest fires from coal dust

🎙️ Spontaneous combustion is quite fascinating and dangerous. Here, a substance suddenly bursts into flames without any apparent external heat source. This can happen when materials slowly oxidize and accumulate heat until they reach their ignition temperature, like in some coal mines or hay bales.

Type 3: Explosion

  • Sudden, violent reaction
  • Produces heat, light, and sound
  • Example: firecrackers

🎙️ An explosion is a very rapid and violent type of combustion where a large amount of gas is produced, along with heat, light, and a loud sound. This happens when a substance burns very quickly in a confined space. Fireworks and firecrackers are common examples.

What is a Flame?

  • Visible part of fire
  • Gaseous substances burn
  • Produces light and heat

🎙️ When a gaseous substance burns, it often produces a visible flame. A flame is essentially the region where combustible gases are burning, emitting light and heat. Not all burning produces a flame; for example, coal glows without a visible flame.

Structure of a Flame

  • Three distinct zones
  • Vary in temperature and colour
  • Outer, Middle, Inner zones

🎙️ A typical candle flame, for instance, isn't uniform. It has a distinct structure made up of three main zones, each with different characteristics. These zones vary in temperature, colour, and the extent of combustion occurring within them.

Outer Zone (Non-luminous)

  • Complete combustion
  • Hottest part of the flame
  • Blue colour, faint light

🎙️ The outermost zone of a flame is where complete combustion of the fuel takes place because there's plenty of oxygen available. It's the hottest part of the flame and typically appears blue or almost invisible, emitting very little light, hence 'non-luminous'.

🔵
Hottest Part
19 / 25

Middle Zone (Luminous)

  • Partial combustion
  • Moderately hot
  • Yellow colour, bright light

🎙️ The middle zone is where partial combustion occurs due to insufficient oxygen supply. This zone is moderately hot and is characterized by its bright yellow colour, which is why it's called the luminous zone. It's the part that gives a candle flame its characteristic glow.

🟠
Bright Glow
20 / 25

Inner Zone (Dark Zone)

  • Unburnt fuel vapours
  • Least hot part
  • Black/dark colour

🎙️ The innermost zone of the flame, right near the wick, is the dark zone. This area consists of unburnt wax vapours and is the least hot part of the flame. It appears dark or black because there is no combustion happening here due to the absence of oxygen.

Fire Control and Extinguishing

  • Remove fuel
  • Cut off air supply
  • Cool down the fuel

🎙️ Understanding fire is not just about how it starts, but also how to control and extinguish it. Since we know the three conditions necessary for combustion, we can stop a fire by removing any one or more of these conditions. This is the basis of fire fighting.

🚒
Fire Safety

Methods of Fire Control

  • Water cools down fuel
  • Fire blanket cuts off air
  • Removing nearby combustibles

🎙️ Common fire extinguishing methods directly target these conditions. Water cools down the fuel below its ignition temperature. A fire blanket or sand cuts off the oxygen supply. And sometimes, removing nearby combustible materials prevents the fire from spreading.

Recap: Key Takeaways

  • Combustion needs fuel, oxygen, and ignition temperature.
  • Substances can be combustible or non-combustible.
  • Flame has outer (hottest), middle, and inner (coolest) zones.
  • Fire can be controlled by removing any of the three conditions.

🎙️ So, to summarize our lesson, we've learned that combustion is a chemical process needing fuel, oxygen, and sufficient heat. We explored different types of combustion and understood the distinct zones within a flame. Most importantly, we now know the principles behind fire control and safety.

Understanding Combustion

  • Combustion is a chemical process.
  • A substance reacts with oxygen.
  • It produces heat and often light.

🎙️ At its core, combustion is simply burning. It’s a chemical reaction where a substance combines with oxygen, usually from the air. The most noticeable outputs of this reaction are heat and often light, which we see as a flame or a glow. Think of burning wood in a campfire.

What is a Combustible Substance?

  • Materials that undergo combustion.
  • They can catch fire and burn.
  • Examples: Wood, paper, petrol, LPG.

🎙️ A combustible substance is any material that can burn. These are the 'fuels' for our combustion reactions. When you light a match, the wood of the matchstick is a combustible substance. Other common examples include paper, cloth, and cooking gas like LPG.

Non-Combustible Materials

  • Materials that do NOT burn.
  • They do not react with oxygen to produce heat and light.
  • Examples: Glass, stone, iron, water.

🎙️ On the other hand, non-combustible materials are those that simply do not burn, no matter how much heat you apply. Try to burn a piece of glass or a stone – they will just get hot, but won't catch fire. This property is very important in fire safety.

Conditions for Combustion: Fuel

  • Presence of a combustible substance (fuel).
  • This is what actually burns.
  • No fuel, no fire!

🎙️ For combustion to happen, we need three main things. The first and most obvious is the fuel itself – the combustible substance. If there's nothing to burn, then there can be no fire. Imagine trying to start a fire in an empty fireplace!

Conditions for Combustion: Oxygen

  • Presence of a supporter of combustion (oxygen/air).
  • Oxygen helps the substance burn.
  • Cutting off air can extinguish fire.

🎙️ The second condition is oxygen, usually supplied by the air around us. Oxygen acts as the supporter of combustion. You can observe this by lighting a candle and then covering it with a glass; the flame goes out as the oxygen inside the glass is used up.

Conditions for Combustion: Ignition Temperature

  • The minimum temperature a substance needs.
  • At this temperature, it catches fire.
  • Different substances have different ignition temperatures.

🎙️ The third crucial condition is the ignition temperature. This is the lowest temperature at which a substance will start burning and continue to burn. Until a substance reaches its ignition temperature, it won't catch fire, even if fuel and oxygen are present.

Real-life Ignition Temperature

  • Kerosene has a lower ignition temperature than wood.
  • That's why kerosene catches fire more easily.
  • Paper has a very low ignition temperature.

🎙️ Consider burning wood and kerosene. Kerosene oil catches fire much more easily than a piece of wood. This is because kerosene has a much lower ignition temperature compared to wood. This difference is why we need to be extra careful with fuels like kerosene.

What are Inflammable Substances?

  • Substances with very low ignition temperature.
  • They catch fire easily with a small flame.
  • Examples: Petrol, alcohol, LPG, CNG.

🎙️ Inflammable substances are a special category of combustible materials. They have extremely low ignition temperatures, meaning they can catch fire very easily, often with just a spark or a small flame. This makes them very dangerous and requires careful handling, like petrol at a fuel station.

Controlling Fire: The Fire Triangle

  • Fire needs Fuel, Oxygen, and Heat (ignition temp).
  • Remove any one of these to extinguish a fire.
  • This concept is called the 'Fire Triangle'.

🎙️ Since we know fire needs three things – fuel, oxygen, and heat – to exist, we can control or extinguish a fire by removing any one of these conditions. This is the fundamental principle behind all fire-fighting methods, often visualized as the 'Fire Triangle'.

1Fuel
2Oxygen
3Heat

Methods of Fire Control

  • Removing the fuel: Stop the supply of combustible material.
  • Cutting off the air supply: Smothering the fire.
  • Cooling the combustible material: Lowering its temperature below ignition.

🎙️ Firefighters use these principles. They might remove surrounding combustible materials, or more commonly, spray water to cool down the burning substance and cut off the oxygen supply. For electrical fires, they use different methods to avoid further hazards.

Fire Extinguishers

  • Devices used to put out small fires.
  • Often contain carbon dioxide (CO2).
  • CO2 cuts off oxygen and cools the substance.

🎙️ Fire extinguishers are common tools for putting out small fires. Many of them contain carbon dioxide under pressure. When released, CO2 is heavier than oxygen, so it forms a blanket around the fire, cutting off the air supply. It also helps cool down the fuel, making it an effective fire-fighting agent.

What is a Flame?

  • The visible, gaseous part of a fire.
  • Formed when vaporized substances burn.
  • Not all burning substances produce a flame.

🎙️ When we see fire, we often see a flame. A flame is essentially the visible, glowing gas produced during combustion. It appears when the combustible substance first vaporizes, and then these vapours burn. But remember, not everything that burns produces a flame.

Substances that Produce Flame

  • Substances that vaporize during burning.
  • The vapours burn to form a flame.
  • Examples: Wax, kerosene, LPG.

🎙️ Materials like wax in a candle, kerosene in a lamp, or LPG in your kitchen stove, all produce a flame. This is because when they are heated, they first turn into vapours, and it's these vapours that actually burn, creating the characteristic flame.

Structure of a Candle Flame

  • A candle flame has different zones.
  • Inner zone: Darkest, unburnt wax vapours.
  • Middle zone: Luminous, partial combustion, yellow.
  • Outer zone: Non-luminous, complete combustion, hottest.

🎙️ If you look closely at a candle flame, you'll notice it's not uniform. It has distinct zones. The inner zone is dark and contains unburnt wax vapours. The middle zone is yellow and bright due to incomplete combustion. The outermost zone is the hottest and almost invisible, where complete combustion occurs.

Fuel Efficiency

  • The amount of heat energy produced by a fuel.
  • Measured when 1 kg of fuel is completely burnt.
  • Called 'Calorific Value', unit is kJ/kg.

🎙️ Not all fuels produce the same amount of heat. Fuel efficiency is a way to compare how much energy different fuels can provide. It's measured by the calorific value, which tells us the amount of heat produced when a unit mass (usually 1 kg) of fuel is completely burned. A higher calorific value means more energy.

Characteristics of an Ideal Fuel

  • High calorific value (produces lots of heat).
  • Burns easily at a moderate rate.
  • Cheap, readily available, and safe to handle.
  • Does not produce harmful substances or pollution.

🎙️ An ideal fuel would have many desirable qualities. It should give a lot of heat, be easy to store and transport, and burn at a manageable rate. Most importantly, it should be affordable, readily available, and not cause environmental pollution or health issues. Finding a truly 'ideal' fuel is a challenge.

Harmful Effects of Burning Fuels

  • Air pollution: Release of unburnt carbon particles, CO, SO2.
  • Global warming: Increased CO2 leading to rise in Earth's temperature.
  • Acid rain: Oxides of sulphur and nitrogen dissolving in rain.

🎙️ While fuels are essential, their burning has significant environmental drawbacks. The release of harmful gases and particles causes air pollution, contributing to respiratory diseases. Increased carbon dioxide leads to global warming, and oxides of sulphur and nitrogen cause acid rain, damaging buildings and ecosystems.

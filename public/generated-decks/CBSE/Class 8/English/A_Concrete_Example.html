What is a Concrete Example?

  • Something you can see, hear, touch, taste, or smell.
  • It's real and specific, not just an idea.
  • Helps others imagine exactly what you mean.

🎙️ Think of 'concrete' like the material used to build roads and buildings – it's solid and real. A concrete example is something tangible, something that appeals to our five senses. It's the opposite of something vague or abstract; it brings an idea down to earth.

Abstract vs. Concrete Ideas

  • Abstract: Ideas, feelings, qualities (e.g., love, justice, beauty).
  • Concrete: Objects, people, places, events (e.g., a red apple, your best friend, the school playground).
  • Concrete examples help explain abstract ideas.

🎙️ This is a key distinction. Abstract ideas are things you can't physically touch or see directly, like emotions or concepts. Concrete things, however, are observable and specific. Our goal is to use concrete examples to make those abstract ideas understandable.

Why Do We Need Concrete Examples?

  • Makes your explanations clearer.
  • Helps your audience understand and remember.
  • Adds interest and avoids boredom.
  • Shows you truly understand the topic.

🎙️ Imagine someone trying to explain a new game without showing you how to play, just talking about the 'rules' in general terms. It would be hard to grasp, right? Concrete examples are like showing someone the game in action. They illuminate your ideas and make them stick.

Example 1: Explaining "Happiness"

  • Abstract idea: Happiness.
  • Vague: 'I feel happy.'
  • Concrete example: 'I felt a burst of happiness when my puppy licked my face this morning.'

🎙️ Happiness is an abstract feeling. Simply saying 'I am happy' doesn't tell us much. But when you describe a specific moment, like your puppy licking your face, we can picture it, feel the warmth, and truly understand your happiness. That's the power of a concrete example.

Identifying Concrete Details

  • Look for words that describe senses: sight, sound, smell, taste, or touch.
  • Ask: Can I picture this? Can I hear it? Can I feel it?
  • These details bring your writing to life.

🎙️ To create concrete examples, you need to train your mind to look for details. Instead of saying 'The weather was bad,' think about what 'bad' means concretely: 'The wind howled, rattling the windows, and rain lashed against the panes.' These are details you can sense.

Practice: Abstract to Concrete - "Freedom"

  • Abstract: Freedom
  • Step 1: Think of a situation where someone feels free.
  • Step 2: What do they see, hear, or do?
  • Example: 'The bird felt freedom as it soared high above the trees, its wings catching the wind, singing a joyful song.'

🎙️ Freedom is a big, abstract word. How do we make it concrete? First, imagine a scene. Then, fill that scene with sensory details. The bird soaring, the wind, the song – these make the abstract idea of freedom tangible and relatable.

Practice: Abstract to Concrete - "Bravery"

  • Abstract: Bravery
  • Step 1: Imagine someone being brave.
  • Step 2: What actions do they take? What do they look like?
  • Example: 'She showed bravery when she stood up for her friend, her voice firm, even when her knees were trembling.'

🎙️ Bravery isn't just a feeling; it's often shown through actions. Here, we don't just say 'she was brave.' We describe the specific action of standing up, the firm voice, and even the internal struggle of trembling knees, making the bravery more impactful.

Building a Concrete Example: A 3-Step Process

  • Step 1: Start with your abstract idea (e.g., 'loneliness').
  • Step 2: Brainstorm specific situations or images related to it (e.g., an empty room, a single chair).
  • Step 3: Add sensory details (what does it look, sound, or feel like?).

🎙️ This is your recipe for creating powerful concrete examples. Always start with the general idea, then narrow it down to a specific scene, and finally, enrich that scene with details that engage the senses. Practice these steps, and it will become second nature.

Example 2: Describing a "Sad Mood"

  • Abstract: She felt sad.
  • Vague: 'She was very sad.'
  • Concrete example: 'A heavy sadness settled over her like a grey blanket, and tears pricked at her eyes as she stared out at the drizzling rain.'

🎙️ Instead of just telling us 'she was sad,' the concrete example 'shows' us her sadness. The 'grey blanket,' 'tears pricking,' and 'drizzling rain' all contribute to a vivid picture of her mood, making it much more impactful than a simple statement.

Worked Example: Improving a Sentence

  • Original: 'The old house was scary.'
  • Improved (with concrete examples): 'The decaying house stood silent, its broken windows like vacant eyes, and the wind whistled through the gaps, sending shivers down my spine.'

🎙️ Look at the difference! 'Scary' is abstract. The improved sentence uses concrete details like 'decaying,' 'broken windows like vacant eyes,' and 'wind whistled' to show us why the house is scary. This is a powerful technique to master.

Avoiding Vague Language

  • Words like 'good,' 'bad,' 'nice,' 'stuff,' 'things' are often vague.
  • Replace them with precise, descriptive words.
  • Vague language leaves the reader guessing.
  • Concrete language leaves no doubt.

🎙️ Many common words are actually quite vague. When you use them, your reader doesn't get a clear picture. Challenge yourself to replace these general terms with specific, concrete language that paints a precise image or feeling. This makes your communication much stronger.

The Power of Sensory Details

  • Sight: colours, shapes, sizes, light.
  • Sound: noises, voices, music, silence.
  • Smell: aromas, stenches, perfumes.
  • Taste: sweet, sour, bitter, salty, spicy.
  • Touch: textures, temperatures, sensations (rough, smooth, cold).

🎙️ Remember your five senses! The more senses you can engage with your concrete examples, the more immersive and real your descriptions will be. Don't just think about what something looks like; consider how it sounds, smells, tastes, and feels too.

Worked Example: Adding Sensory Details

  • Original: 'The food was delicious.'
  • Adding details: 'The crispy samosas, still hot from the oil, had a spicy potato filling that made my tongue tingle, and the sweet mango chutney cooled it down perfectly.'

🎙️ Delicious is vague. By adding details about the 'crispy samosas,' 'hot oil,' 'spicy potato filling,' 'tingling tongue,' and 'sweet mango chutney,' we've transformed a simple statement into a mouth-watering experience. See how many senses we engaged there?

When NOT to Use Too Many Details

  • Balance is key: too many details can make writing cluttered.
  • Focus on the most impactful and relevant details.
  • Sometimes, a simple statement is enough.
  • The goal is clarity, not just quantity of details.

🎙️ While concrete examples are powerful, it's also important to use them wisely. You don't need to describe every single tiny detail. Choose the ones that truly enhance your message and avoid overwhelming your reader. It's about quality over quantity.

  • The study of hydrocarbons and their derivatives.
  • Deals with compounds primarily containing carbon and hydrogen.
  • Encompasses millions of known compounds due to carbon's unique properties.

🎙️ Organic chemistry is essentially the study of carbon-containing compounds. From the simplest methane to complex proteins and DNA, carbon is the central atom. Its ability to form stable bonds with itself and other elements leads to an incredible diversity of molecules.

Carbon's Special Properties: Tetravalency & Catenation

  • **Tetravalency:** Carbon forms four covalent bonds.
  • **Catenation:** Ability to form long chains and rings with itself.
  • Strong C-C and C-H bonds contribute to stability.

🎙️ Carbon's tetravalency allows it to form a wide array of structures, while catenation is its unique ability to link with other carbon atoms. This self-linking property is why we see such vast and complex organic molecules, from straight chains to intricate branched structures and rings.

Hybridization in Organic Compounds: sp³, sp², sp

  • **sp³ Hybridization:** Single bonds, tetrahedral geometry (e.g., alkanes).
  • **sp² Hybridization:** Double bonds, trigonal planar geometry (e.g., alkenes).
  • **sp Hybridization:** Triple bonds, linear geometry (e.g., alkynes).

🎙️ Hybridization is a key concept that explains the bonding and geometry around carbon atoms. The type of hybridization directly influences the bond angles and the overall shape of the molecule. Understanding this helps predict reactivity and physical properties.

Shapes of Organic Molecules & Bond Angles

  • Molecular geometry is determined by hybridization and VSEPR theory.
  • sp³ carbons lead to tetrahedral shape (approx. 109.5° bond angle).
  • sp² carbons lead to trigonal planar shape (approx. 120° bond angle).
  • sp carbons lead to linear shape (180° bond angle).

🎙️ The three-dimensional arrangement of atoms, or molecular shape, is crucial for how molecules interact. For example, the precise angles in a protein molecule determine its function. Remember these standard bond angles based on hybridization.

Classification of Organic Compounds

  • **Acyclic/Open-chain:** Straight or branched chains (e.g., alkanes).
  • **Cyclic/Closed-chain:** Carbon atoms form rings.
  • **Alicyclic:** Ring compounds resembling open-chain aliphatic compounds.
  • **Aromatic:** Special stability due to delocalized pi electrons (e.g., benzene).

🎙️ Organic compounds are broadly classified based on their carbon skeleton. This classification helps us organize the vast number of organic molecules and predict their general behavior. Aromatic compounds, with their unique stability, form a very important class.

Functional Groups: The Heart of Reactivity

  • Examples: -OH (hydroxyl), -CHO (aldehyde), -COOH (carboxyl).
  • The rest of the molecule is often referred to as the 'alkyl' or 'aryl' group.

🎙️ Functional groups are the reactive centers of organic molecules. They dictate how a compound will behave chemically, regardless of the size of the carbon chain it's attached to. Identifying functional groups is the first step in understanding organic reactions.

IUPAC Nomenclature: Basic Rules for Naming

  • Identify the longest continuous carbon chain (parent chain).
  • Number the parent chain to give substituents/functional groups the lowest possible numbers.
  • Identify and name substituents, arranging them alphabetically.
  • Combine prefix, parent chain, and suffix to form the full name.

🎙️ The International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC) provides a systematic way to name organic compounds. Mastering these rules is essential for clear communication in chemistry. Always follow the priority rules for numbering and functional groups.

1Find Parent Chain
2Number Atoms
3Name Substituents
4Construct Name

Worked Example: Naming Unsaturated Hydrocarbons

  • **Structure:** CH₃-CH=CH-CH₂-CH₃
  • **Step 1:** Longest chain containing double bond is 5 carbons (pentene).
  • **Step 2:** Number from the end closer to the double bond (position 2).
  • **IUPAC Name:** Pent-2-ene

🎙️ When dealing with alkenes or alkynes, the double or triple bond must be included in the parent chain and given the lowest possible number. Here, the double bond starts at carbon 2, giving us pent-2-ene. Remember to indicate the position of the multiple bond.

Worked Example: Naming Compounds with Functional Groups

  • **Structure:** CH₃-CH₂-CH(OH)-CH₃
  • **Step 1:** Longest chain containing -OH group is 4 carbons (butan-).
  • **Step 2:** Number from end giving -OH lowest number (position 2).
  • **IUPAC Name:** Butan-2-ol

🎙️ For compounds with functional groups, the functional group dictates the suffix and must be given priority in numbering. In this example, the hydroxyl group (-OH) is at position 2, making it butan-2-ol. Always prioritize the main functional group.

Isomerism: Same Formula, Different Structures

  • Compounds with the same molecular formula but different structural or spatial arrangements.
  • Leads to different physical and chemical properties.
  • Two main types: Structural Isomerism and Stereoisomerism.

🎙️ Isomerism is a fascinating concept where compounds share the same elemental composition but are distinct molecules. This difference in arrangement, even subtle, can lead to vastly different properties, which is critical in drug design and materials science.

Structural Isomerism: Chain, Position, Functional Group

  • **Chain Isomerism:** Different carbon skeleton arrangements (e.g., n-butane vs. isobutane).
  • **Position Isomerism:** Different positions of functional group or substituent (e.g., propan-1-ol vs. propan-2-ol).
  • **Functional Group Isomerism:** Different functional groups (e.g., ethanol vs. dimethylether).

🎙️ Structural isomers differ in the connectivity of their atoms. Chain isomers have different branching, position isomers have the same functional group at a different spot, and functional group isomers have completely different functional groups despite the same formula. Stereoisomerism: Geometrical (Cis-Trans)

  • Arises due to restricted rotation around a double bond or in a ring.
  • **Cis-isomer:** Identical groups on the same side of the double bond.
  • **Trans-isomer:** Identical groups on opposite sides of the double bond.

🎙️ Geometrical isomerism, also known as cis-trans isomerism, occurs when there's a lack of free rotation. The relative positions of substituents around a double bond or in a cyclic structure lead to distinct molecules with different properties. This is common in alkenes.

Stereoisomerism: Optical Isomerism (Chirality)

  • Molecules that are non-superimposable mirror images of each other (enantiomers).
  • Requires a chiral center (carbon atom bonded to four different groups).
  • Enantiomers rotate plane-polarized light in opposite directions.

🎙️ Optical isomerism is vital in biochemistry and pharmaceuticals. A molecule is chiral if it cannot be superimposed on its mirror image, much like your left and right hands. The presence of a chiral carbon is usually the key indicator. Enantiomers have identical physical properties except for their interaction with plane-polarized light.

Reaction Mechanisms: Bond Fission

  • **Homolytic Fission:** Symmetrical breaking of a covalent bond, each atom takes one electron.
  • Forms highly reactive **free radicals** (species with unpaired electrons).
  • **Heterolytic Fission:** Unsymmetrical breaking of a covalent bond, one atom takes both electrons.
  • Forms **ions** (carbocations or carbanions).

🎙️ Understanding how bonds break is fundamental to reaction mechanisms. Homolytic fission typically occurs under UV light or heat, leading to free radical reactions. Heterolytic fission is more common in polar solvents and leads to ionic reactions, which are often faster.

Reactive Intermediates: Carbocations, Carbanions, Free Radicals

  • **Carbocation:** Carbon atom with a positive charge and only three bonds (electron deficient).
  • **Carbanion:** Carbon atom with a negative charge and a lone pair of electrons (electron rich).
  • **Free Radical:** Carbon atom with an unpaired electron (highly reactive).
  • Stability order: Tertiary > Secondary > Primary for carbocations and free radicals; Primary > Secondary > Tertiary for carbanions.

🎙️ These intermediates are short-lived, highly reactive species formed during the course of a reaction. Their stability significantly influences the reaction pathway and product formation. Knowing their relative stabilities is key to predicting reaction outcomes.

Electron Displacement Effects: Inductive Effect

  • Permanent displacement of sigma (σ) electrons along a carbon chain.
  • Caused by the difference in electronegativity between atoms.
  • **-I Effect:** Electron-withdrawing groups (e.g., -NO₂, -Cl).
  • **+I Effect:** Electron-donating groups (e.g., -CH₃, -C₂H₅).

🎙️ The inductive effect is a permanent electronic effect that influences the electron density in a molecule. It's a 'through-bond' effect that decreases rapidly with distance. It plays a significant role in determining the acidity, basicity, and reactivity of organic compounds.

Electron Displacement Effects: Resonance Effect

  • Delocalization of pi (π) electrons or lone pairs over conjugated systems.
  • Represented by multiple resonance structures (contributing structures).
  • The actual molecule is a resonance hybrid, more stable than any single structure.
  • **+R Effect:** Electron-donating groups (e.g., -OH, -NH₂).
  • **-R Effect:** Electron-withdrawing groups (e.g., -NO₂, -CHO).

🎙️ Resonance, also known as mesomeric effect, involves the delocalization of electrons within a molecule, often in systems with alternating single and double bonds. This delocalization stabilizes the molecule and influences its reactivity, especially in aromatic compounds. Remember to use double-headed arrows for resonance structures.

Dalton's Atomic Theory

  • Atoms are indivisible particles.
  • All atoms of an element are identical.
  • Atoms of different elements differ in properties.
  • Atoms combine in simple whole-number ratios.

🎙️ John Dalton, in 1808, proposed the first scientific theory about atoms. He envisioned atoms as solid, indivisible spheres, forming the basis for modern chemistry. His theory was a breakthrough but had limitations.

Limitations of Dalton's Theory

  • Couldn't explain subatomic particles.
  • Couldn't explain isotopes/isobars.
  • Couldn't explain why atoms combine in certain ways.

🎙️ While revolutionary, Dalton's theory couldn't account for the existence of subatomic particles like electrons and protons, which were discovered later. It also failed to explain isotopes or the nature of chemical bonding.

Discovery of Electron: Cathode Ray Experiment

  • Performed by J.J. Thomson.
  • High voltage applied to gas in discharge tube.
  • Rays observed moving from cathode to anode.
  • These rays were called Cathode Rays.

🎙️ The journey to understanding subatomic particles began with J.J. Thomson's cathode ray experiment. He applied high voltage across electrodes in a vacuum tube, observing rays emitted from the negative electrode, or cathode.

Properties of Electrons: e/m Ratio

  • Cathode rays consist of negatively charged particles.
  • Deflected by electric and magnetic fields.
  • Charge-to-mass (e/m) ratio is constant for all electrons.
  • Identical regardless of gas or electrode material.

🎙️ Thomson showed that cathode rays were streams of negatively charged particles, later named electrons. He determined their charge-to-mass ratio, a fundamental constant for all electrons, indicating they are universal constituents of matter.

Discovery of Proton: Anode Ray Experiment

  • Performed by E. Goldstein.
  • Modified discharge tube with perforated cathode.
  • Rays observed moving from anode to cathode.
  • These rays were called Anode/Canal Rays.

🎙️ Following the discovery of electrons, E. Goldstein conducted experiments with perforated cathodes. He observed new rays moving in the opposite direction to cathode rays, indicating the presence of positively charged particles.

Properties of Protons: Charge & Mass

  • Anode rays consist of positively charged particles.
  • e/m ratio depends on the gas used.
  • Lightest positive particle obtained from hydrogen (proton).
  • Charge equal to electron, mass ~1836 times electron.

🎙️ Anode rays were found to be streams of positive ions. The lightest and most fundamental positive particle, the proton, was discovered when hydrogen gas was used. Its charge is equal and opposite to an electron, but its mass is significantly greater.

Discovery of Neutron: Chadwick's Experiment

  • Discovered by James Chadwick in 1932.
  • Bombarded beryllium with alpha particles.
  • Observed highly penetrating, uncharged radiation.
  • This radiation consisted of neutral particles: neutrons.

🎙️ The final piece of the subatomic puzzle came with James Chadwick's discovery of the neutron. He found that bombarding beryllium with alpha particles produced a highly penetrating radiation that was not deflected by electric or magnetic fields, indicating its neutral nature.

Properties of Neutrons: Mass & Charge

  • Electrically neutral particles.
  • Mass slightly greater than a proton.
  • Present in the nucleus along with protons.
  • Crucial for nuclear stability.

🎙️ Neutrons are neutral particles with a mass almost identical to protons. Their presence in the nucleus is vital for holding the positively charged protons together, overcoming electrostatic repulsion and ensuring nuclear stability.

Atomic Models: Thomson's Plum Pudding Model

  • Proposed by J.J. Thomson in 1904.
  • Atom is a sphere of uniform positive charge.
  • Electrons are embedded like plums in a pudding.
  • Overall atom is electrically neutral.

🎙️ After discovering the electron, Thomson proposed his 'Plum Pudding' model. He envisioned the atom as a sphere of positive charge with negatively charged electrons scattered within it, much like plums in a pudding, ensuring overall neutrality.

Rutherford's Gold Foil Experiment

  • Alpha particles bombarded a thin gold foil.
  • Most particles passed straight through.
  • Some deflected at small angles.
  • Very few bounced back (large angle deflection).

🎙️ Ernest Rutherford's groundbreaking experiment involved firing positively charged alpha particles at a thin gold foil. His observations challenged Thomson's model and paved the way for a new understanding of atomic structure.

Rutherford's Model: Postulates & Limitations

  • Atom has a tiny, dense, positive nucleus.
  • Electrons revolve around the nucleus (planetary model).
  • Most of the atom is empty space.
  • Major Limitation: Couldn't explain atomic stability or line spectra.

🎙️ Based on his experiment, Rutherford proposed the nuclear model: a dense, positively charged nucleus with electrons orbiting it. While revolutionary, it couldn't explain why electrons don't spiral into the nucleus or the discrete line spectra observed from atoms.

Bohr's Model of Atom: Postulates

  • Electrons revolve in fixed, stable orbits (stationary states).
  • Each orbit has a definite energy (quantized energy levels).
  • Electrons do not radiate energy in stationary orbits.
  • Energy is absorbed/emitted only when electrons jump between orbits.

🎙️ Niels Bohr refined Rutherford's model by introducing quantum concepts. He proposed that electrons exist in specific, quantized energy levels or orbits, and only emit or absorb energy when transitioning between these levels.

Bohr's Model: Energy Levels & Spectrum

  • Energy of an electron in an orbit is given by E_n = -13.6/n^2 eV.
  • Explains line spectra of hydrogen and hydrogen-like ions.
  • Each line in the spectrum corresponds to a specific electron transition.
  • Limitations: Only for single-electron species, couldn't explain fine spectra.

🎙️ Bohr's model successfully explained the discrete line spectra of hydrogen, where each line corresponds to an electron moving between specific energy levels. However, it failed for multi-electron atoms and couldn't explain the splitting of spectral lines in magnetic fields.

Limitations of Bohr's Model

  • Applicable only to single-electron species (H, He+, Li2+).
  • Couldn't explain the fine structure of spectral lines.
  • Failed to explain the Zeeman effect (magnetic field) & Stark effect (electric field).
  • Did not account for the dual nature of matter or Heisenberg's uncertainty principle.

🎙️ Despite its success for hydrogen, Bohr's model had significant limitations. It couldn't explain the spectra of more complex atoms, nor could it account for the splitting of spectral lines in external fields or the wave-like nature of electrons, leading to the development of quantum mechanics.

Dual Nature of Matter: De Broglie

  • Proposed by Louis de Broglie (1924).
  • Matter, like radiation, exhibits both particle and wave properties.
  • Wavelength (λ) = h / (mv) where h=Planck's constant, m=mass, v=velocity.
  • Electron diffraction experiments confirmed wave nature.

🎙️ De Broglie revolutionized our understanding by proposing that all matter, including electrons, exhibits both particle and wave characteristics. This 'dual nature' means electrons can behave like tiny particles and also like waves, a concept later experimentally confirmed.

Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle

  • Proposed by Werner Heisenberg.
  • It is impossible to determine simultaneously and precisely the exact position and momentum of a subatomic particle (e.g., electron).
  • Δx * Δp ≥ h / 4π.
  • More precisely we know position, less precisely we know momentum, and vice versa.

🎙️ Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle states that we cannot know both the exact position and momentum of a subatomic particle like an electron at the same time. The more accurately we measure one, the less accurately we can determine the other, fundamentally limiting our knowledge.

Quantum Mechanical Model

  • Based on Schrödinger wave equation.
  • Describes the probability of finding an electron in a region of space.
  • Concept of 'orbital' replaces 'orbit'.
  • Orbital is a 3D region of space where electron probability is maximum.

🎙️ The quantum mechanical model, based on Schrödinger's equation, provides a more sophisticated description of the atom. It replaces Bohr's fixed orbits with 'orbitals,' which are three-dimensional regions indicating the probability of finding an electron.

Quantum Numbers

  • Principal (n): Energy level, size of orbital (n=1,2,3...)
  • Azimuthal (l): Subshell, shape of orbital (l=0 to n-1, s,p,d,f)
  • Magnetic (m_l): Orientation of orbital in space (m_l = -l to +l)
  • Spin (m_s): Spin of electron (+1/2 or -1/2)

🎙️ Quantum numbers are a set of four values that uniquely describe the state of an electron in an atom. They specify the electron's energy level, the shape of its orbital, its orientation in space, and its intrinsic spin.

1n (Size/Energy)
2l (Shape)
3m_l (Orientation)
4m_s (Spin)

Shapes of Orbitals: s, p, d

  • s-orbital: Spherical shape (l=0).
  • p-orbitals: Dumbbell shape (l=1), three orientations (px, py, pz).
  • d-orbitals: More complex shapes (l=2), five orientations.
  • f-orbitals: Even more complex (l=3).

🎙️ The azimuthal quantum number (l) determines the shape of an orbital. s-orbitals are spherical, p-orbitals are dumbbell-shaped and exist in three orientations, while d-orbitals have more complex shapes and five orientations in space.

Rules for Filling Orbitals

  • Aufbau Principle: Electrons fill lowest energy orbitals first.
  • Pauli Exclusion Principle: No two electrons in an atom can have all four quantum numbers identical (max 2 electrons per orbital, with opposite spins).
  • Hund's Rule of Maximum Multiplicity: Pairing of electrons in degenerate orbitals (same energy) does not occur until each orbital has one electron.

🎙️ Three main rules govern how electrons fill atomic orbitals. The Aufbau principle dictates filling from lowest to highest energy, Pauli's principle limits each orbital to two electrons with opposite spins, and Hund's rule ensures maximum multiplicity in degenerate orbitals.

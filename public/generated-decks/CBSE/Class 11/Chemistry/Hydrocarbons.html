Alkanes: The Saturated Hydrocarbons

  • Contain only carbon-carbon single bonds
  • Called 'saturated' because they have maximum hydrogen atoms
  • Simplest type of hydrocarbon

🎙️ Imagine a straight, single-lane road with no turns or intersections – that's like an alkane. All the carbon atoms are connected by single bonds, meaning they are 'saturated' with hydrogen atoms, holding as many as possible. They are the most stable and least reactive type of hydrocarbon.

General Formula & Structure of Alkanes (CnH2n+2)

  • General formula: CnH2n+2
  • Each carbon is sp3 hybridised
  • Tetrahedral geometry around each carbon

🎙️ The general formula CnH2n+2 helps us predict the number of hydrogen atoms for any given number of carbon atoms in an alkane. For example, if n=1, we get CH4, which is methane. Each carbon atom forms four single bonds, resulting in a tetrahedral arrangement.

Naming Alkanes: IUPAC Rules (Part 1)

  • Find the longest continuous carbon chain (parent chain)
  • Number the parent chain to give substituents lowest possible numbers
  • Use prefixes (meth-, eth-, prop-, but-) for chain length

🎙️ Naming alkanes systematically is crucial for clear communication in chemistry. The IUPAC system provides clear rules. First, identify the longest continuous chain of carbon atoms, which forms the parent name. Then, number this chain to ensure any branches or 'substituents' get the lowest possible numbers.

Naming Alkanes: IUPAC Rules (Part 2 - Branched Chains)

  • Name substituents (e.g., methyl, ethyl)
  • If identical substituents, use di-, tri-, tetra- prefixes
  • List substituents alphabetically before the parent name

🎙️ Once you've identified and numbered the parent chain, you need to name the branches attached to it. These are called substituents. For example, a one-carbon branch is 'methyl'. If you have multiple identical branches, use prefixes like 'di-' or 'tri-'. Finally, assemble the name alphabetically, placing the substituent names before the parent alkane name.

Naming Alkenes: IUPAC Rules

  • Longest chain must include the double bond
  • Number chain to give double bond lowest possible number
  • Suffix '-ene' replaces '-ane'

🎙️ Naming alkenes follows similar IUPAC principles but with a crucial difference: the parent chain *must* contain the double bond. The numbering of the chain must prioritize giving the double bond the lowest possible position. The '-ane' suffix of alkanes is replaced by '-ene' to denote the double bond.

Structure of Benzene: Kekulé and Resonance

  • Benzene formula: C6H6
  • Kekulé proposed alternating single and double bonds
  • Actual structure is a resonance hybrid (delocalised electrons)

🎙️ The structure of benzene (C6H6) was a puzzle for a long time. Kekulé's alternating single and double bonds were a good start, but the actual structure is a resonance hybrid. This means the pi electrons are delocalised over the entire ring, making all carbon-carbon bonds equal in length and strength, contributing to its high stability.

Naming Aromatic Compounds: Simple Derivatives

  • Benzene is the parent name
  • Substituents are named as prefixes (e.g., methylbenzene)
  • Common names often used (e.g., toluene for methylbenzene)

🎙️ Naming aromatic compounds often involves using benzene as the base name, with substituents named as prefixes. For example, a methyl group on a benzene ring is methylbenzene. However, many common names like toluene (for methylbenzene) and phenol (for hydroxybenzene) are widely accepted and frequently used, so it's important to know both.

Reactions of Hydrocarbons: Combustion

Reactions of Hydrocarbons: Substitution & Addition

  • Saturated hydrocarbons (alkanes) undergo substitution reactions
  • Unsaturated hydrocarbons (alkenes, alkynes) undergo addition reactions
  • Double/triple bonds break in addition reactions

🎙️ The type of bond present dictates the primary reaction mechanism. Alkanes, being saturated, typically undergo substitution reactions where an atom is replaced by another. Alkenes and alkynes, with their double and triple bonds, readily undergo addition reactions where atoms are added across the multiple bond, without losing any existing atoms.

Common Mistakes in Hydrocarbons

  • Incorrectly identifying longest carbon chain in branched compounds
  • Not giving multiple bonds or substituents the lowest possible numbers
  • Confusing saturated (alkanes) with unsaturated (alkenes/alkynes) properties

🎙️ Students often make mistakes in IUPAC nomenclature, especially with branched chains or when multiple bonds are present. Always double-check your longest chain and ensure your numbering gives the lowest possible locants to functional groups and substituents. Also, remember the fundamental differences in reactivity between saturated and unsaturated hydrocarbons.

What are Hydrocarbons?

  • Organic compounds containing only carbon (C) and hydrogen (H).
  • Form the basis of petroleum, natural gas, and coal.
  • Used as fuels, solvents, and raw materials for plastics.

🎙️ Hydrocarbons are literally everywhere – from the fuel in our cars to the plastics we use. Their diverse structures lead to a wide range of properties and applications. Understanding them is key to chemistry.

Classification: Aliphatic vs. Aromatic

  • Main classification based on carbon chain structure.
  • Aliphatic: Open-chain or non-aromatic cyclic compounds.
  • Aromatic: Contain special benzene-like ring structures.

🎙️ The first major division is between aliphatic and aromatic hydrocarbons. Aliphatic compounds are generally simpler, while aromatic compounds possess unique stability due to their ring systems.

Aliphatic Hydrocarbons: Saturated vs. Unsaturated

  • Saturated: Contain only carbon-carbon single bonds (Alkanes).
  • Unsaturated: Contain carbon-carbon double (Alkenes) or triple (Alkynes) bonds.
  • Degree of saturation affects reactivity.

🎙️ Within aliphatic hydrocarbons, we further classify them based on the type of carbon-carbon bonds present. Single bonds define saturated compounds, while double or triple bonds define unsaturated ones, leading to different chemical behaviors.

Saturated Hydrocarbons: Alkanes

  • General formula: CnH2n+2 (for acyclic alkanes).
  • Contain only C-C single bonds and C-H bonds.
  • Also known as paraffins (little affinity).

🎙️ Alkanes are the simplest family of hydrocarbons. Their 'saturated' nature means all carbon valencies are satisfied by single bonds, making them relatively unreactive compared to unsaturated hydrocarbons.

Nomenclature of Alkanes (IUPAC Rules)

  • Identify the longest continuous carbon chain (parent chain).
  • Number the chain to give substituents the lowest possible numbers.
  • Name and locate substituents, then combine with parent chain name.

🎙️ IUPAC nomenclature provides a systematic way to name all organic compounds. For alkanes, the key is to correctly identify the parent chain and assign the lowest possible locants to any attached groups.

1Find Longest Chain
2Number from End
3Name Substituents
4Combine & Alphabetize

Worked Example: Naming Alkanes

  • Identify the parent chain and substituents.
  • Apply numbering rules for lowest locants.
  • Example 1: CH3-CH(CH3)-CH2-CH3 is 2-Methylbutane.
  • Example 2: CH3-CH(CH3)-CH(CH3)-CH3 is 2,3-Dimethylbutane.

🎙️ Let's practice with a couple of examples. Remember to prioritize the longest chain and ensure your numbering gives the smallest set of numbers to your substituents. This is a common area for mistakes in exams.

Isomerism in Alkanes: Chain Isomerism

  • Compounds with the same molecular formula but different carbon skeletons.
  • Example: Butane (n-butane) and Isobutane (2-methylpropane).
  • Different structures lead to different physical properties.

🎙️ Isomerism is a crucial concept. Chain isomerism in alkanes shows how atoms can be arranged differently to form distinct compounds even with the same molecular formula. This structural difference impacts properties like boiling point.

Preparation of Alkanes

  • From Unsaturated Hydrocarbons: Hydrogenation (Ni, Pt, Pd catalyst).
  • Wurtz Reaction: Alkyl halides react with sodium metal (R-X + Na → R-R).
  • Decarboxylation of Carboxylic Acids: RCOONa + NaOH → RH + Na2CO3.

🎙️ Several methods exist to prepare alkanes. Hydrogenation converts unsaturated hydrocarbons to saturated ones. Wurtz reaction is useful for synthesizing symmetrical alkanes, while decarboxylation reduces the carbon chain length.

Chemical Properties of Alkanes

  • Combustion: Burn in air to produce CO2 and H2O (exothermic).
  • Halogenation: Reaction with halogens in presence of UV light (Free Radical Substitution).
  • Relatively unreactive due to strong C-C and C-H bonds.

🎙️ Alkanes are generally quite stable. Their most significant reactions are combustion, which is why they are excellent fuels, and halogenation, which proceeds via a free radical mechanism under UV light.

Unsaturated Hydrocarbons: Alkenes

  • General formula: CnH2n (for acyclic alkenes).
  • Contain at least one carbon-carbon double bond (C=C).
  • Also known as olefins (oil-forming).

🎙️ Alkenes are unsaturated hydrocarbons characterized by the presence of a double bond. This double bond makes them much more reactive than alkanes, primarily undergoing addition reactions.

Nomenclature of Alkenes (IUPAC Rules)

  • Identify the longest chain containing the C=C bond (parent chain).
  • Number the chain to give the C=C bond the lowest possible number.
  • Change '-ane' suffix to '-ene'; indicate double bond position.

🎙️ Naming alkenes follows similar IUPAC principles, but with the added priority for the double bond. Ensure the parent chain always includes the double bond, and its position is indicated correctly.

1Longest Chain with C=C
2Number C=C Lowest
3Name Substituents
4Suffix '-ene'
13 / 25

Worked Example: Naming Alkenes

  • Identify the parent chain and double bond position.
  • Apply numbering rules, prioritizing the double bond.
  • Example 1: CH3-CH=CH2 is Prop-1-ene (or Propene).
  • Example 2: CH3-CH2-CH=CH2 is But-1-ene.

🎙️ Let's look at some examples. Remember, if there's a choice in numbering, the double bond gets precedence. For simple alkenes like propene, the '1' is often omitted as it's the only possible position.

Geometric Isomerism (cis-trans) in Alkenes

  • Occurs due to restricted rotation around the C=C double bond.
  • Requires two different groups on each carbon of the double bond.
  • Cis-isomers: Identical groups on the same side. Trans-isomers: Identical groups on opposite sides.

🎙️ Geometric isomerism, or cis-trans isomerism, is a type of stereoisomerism. It's crucial to understand the conditions for its existence: restricted rotation and different groups on each double-bonded carbon. These isomers have distinct properties.

Preparation of Alkenes

  • Dehydration of Alcohols: Elimination of water (e.g., H2SO4, heat).
  • Dehydrohalogenation of Alkyl Halides: Elimination of HX (e.g., alcoholic KOH).
  • From Alkynes: Partial hydrogenation (e.g., Lindlar's catalyst for cis-alkenes).

🎙️ Alkenes can be prepared through elimination reactions. Dehydration of alcohols and dehydrohalogenation of alkyl halides are common methods. Partial hydrogenation of alkynes offers a way to selectively form alkenes.

1Alcohol Dehydration
2Alkyl Halide Dehydrohalogenation
3Alkyne Partial Hydrogenation

Chemical Properties of Alkenes: Addition Reactions

  • Hydrogenation: Addition of H2 across C=C (Ni, Pt, Pd catalyst).
  • Halogenation: Addition of X2 (Br2 in CCl4) to form vicinal dihalides.
  • Hydrohalogenation: Addition of HX (HBr, HCl) following Markovnikov's Rule.

🎙️ The double bond in alkenes makes them highly susceptible to addition reactions. These reactions involve breaking the pi bond and forming two new sigma bonds. Markovnikov's rule is vital for understanding regioselectivity in hydrohalogenation.

Worked Example: Alkene Reactions (Markovnikov's Rule)

  • Markovnikov's Rule: In addition of HX to unsymmetrical alkenes, the H adds to the carbon with more hydrogens.
  • Example: Propene + HBr → 2-Bromopropane (major product).
  • The more stable carbocation intermediate is formed.

🎙️ Markovnikov's rule is a cornerstone for predicting products of addition reactions to unsymmetrical alkenes. It's driven by the formation of the most stable carbocation intermediate, which is usually the more substituted one.

Unsaturated Hydrocarbons: Alkynes

  • General formula: CnH2n-2 (for acyclic alkynes).
  • Contain at least one carbon-carbon triple bond (C≡C).
  • Highly unsaturated, even more reactive than alkenes.

🎙️ Alkynes are the most unsaturated of the simple hydrocarbons, featuring a triple bond. This bond consists of one sigma and two pi bonds, making them very reactive, undergoing addition reactions in two steps.

Nomenclature of Alkynes (IUPAC Rules)

  • Identify the longest chain containing the C≡C bond (parent chain).
  • Number the chain to give the C≡C bond the lowest possible number.
  • Change '-ane' suffix to '-yne'; indicate triple bond position.

🎙️ Naming alkynes follows the same hierarchical rules as alkenes, but with the triple bond taking precedence over double bonds if both are present. Ensure the correct suffix '-yne' is used.

Worked Example: Naming Alkynes

  • Identify the parent chain and triple bond position.
  • Apply numbering rules, prioritizing the triple bond.
  • Example 1: CH3-C≡CH is Prop-1-yne (or Propyne).
  • Example 2: CH3-CH2-C≡CH is But-1-yne.

🎙️ Practice is essential for mastering alkyne nomenclature. Always locate the triple bond first and assign it the lowest possible number. Be careful with isomers that might have the triple bond at different positions.

Preparation of Alkynes

  • From Vicinal Dihalides: Double dehydrohalogenation (alcoholic KOH then NaNH2).
  • From Calcium Carbide: Hydrolysis of CaC2 (CaC2 + 2H2O → C2H2 + Ca(OH)2).
  • Used extensively in industrial synthesis.

🎙️ Alkynes can be synthesized from dihalides through two sequential elimination reactions. The reaction of calcium carbide with water is a classic industrial method for producing acetylene, the simplest alkyne.

1Double Dehydrohalogenation
2Calcium Carbide Hydrolysis

Chemical Properties of Alkynes

  • Addition Reactions: Similar to alkenes but occur in two steps (e.g., H2, X2, HX).
  • Acidity of Terminal Alkynes: Terminal H is acidic, reacts with strong bases (e.g., NaNH2).
  • Polymerization: Can polymerize under specific conditions.

🎙️ Alkynes undergo addition reactions twice due to their two pi bonds, often following Markovnikov's rule. Terminal alkynes are unique due to the slight acidity of their terminal hydrogen, allowing them to react with strong bases.

Aromatic Hydrocarbons: Benzene

  • Benzene (C6H6) is the simplest aromatic hydrocarbon.
  • Cyclic, planar molecule with delocalized pi-electron system.
  • Extraordinary stability due to resonance (Hückel's Rule: 4n+2 pi electrons).

🎙️ Aromatic hydrocarbons, with benzene as the prime example, possess unique stability. This stability arises from the delocalization of pi electrons within a cyclic, planar system, as described by Hückel's rule.

Electrophilic Substitution Reactions of Benzene

  • Benzene undergoes electrophilic substitution, not addition.
  • Nitration: Reaction with conc. HNO3/H2SO4 to form nitrobenzene.
  • Halogenation: Reaction with X2 in presence of Lewis acid (FeX3) to form halobenzene.

🎙️ Unlike unsaturated aliphatics, benzene prefers substitution over addition to maintain its aromatic stability. These reactions involve an electrophile replacing a hydrogen atom on the ring. Nitration and halogenation are key examples.

Quick Check & Recap

  • Hydrocarbons classified as Aliphatic (saturated/unsaturated) and Aromatic.
  • Alkanes: Single bonds, CnH2n+2, free radical substitution.
  • Alkenes/Alkynes: Double/Triple bonds, addition reactions, Markovnikov's rule.

Saturated Hydrocarbons: Alkanes

  • Contain only Carbon-Carbon single bonds.
  • General formula: CnH2n+2.
  • Also known as Paraffins (little affinity).

🎙️ Alkanes are the simplest type of hydrocarbon, containing only single bonds between carbon atoms. This makes them 'saturated' with hydrogen atoms. Their general formula, CnH2n+2, is crucial for determining their molecular formula. They are relatively unreactive, hence the name paraffins.

Nomenclature of Alkanes (IUPAC)

  • Identify the longest continuous carbon chain (parent chain).
  • Number the chain to give substituents the lowest possible numbers.
  • Name substituents alphabetically.

🎙️ Systematic naming of organic compounds follows IUPAC rules. For alkanes, the first step is always to find the longest carbon chain. Then, number the chain to ensure any attached groups, called substituents, have the smallest possible numbers. Finally, list substituents alphabetically.

Worked Example: Naming Alkanes

  • Structure: CH3-CH(CH3)-CH2-CH3
  • Longest chain: Butane (4 carbons).
  • Methyl group at C-2. Name: 2-Methylbutane.

🎙️ Let's apply the rules. In this example, the longest continuous chain has four carbons, making it a 'butane'. We have a methyl group attached to the second carbon. So, the correct IUPAC name is 2-Methylbutane. Remember to always provide the position of the substituent.

Common Mistake: Longest Chain Selection

  • Always identify the *absolute* longest continuous chain.
  • Don't just pick the straightest chain.
  • Incorrect: 2-Ethylpropane. Correct: 2-Methylbutane.

🎙️ A frequent error is failing to identify the *true* longest continuous carbon chain. Students often pick the chain that appears straightest. Always trace all possible paths to find the maximum number of carbons. For instance, what might look like a propyl chain could actually be part of a longer butane or pentane chain.

Isomerism in Alkanes

  • Isomers: Same molecular formula, different structural formula.
  • Alkanes exhibit Chain Isomerism.
  • Example: n-Butane and Isobutane (C4H10).

🎙️ Isomerism is a key concept. Isomers are compounds that share the same molecular formula but differ in their structural arrangement. Alkanes primarily show chain isomerism, where the carbon skeleton itself varies. For example, C4H10 can be straight-chain n-butane or branched isobutane.

Physical Properties of Alkanes

  • Non-polar molecules, insoluble in water.
  • Soluble in non-polar solvents.
  • Boiling point increases with molecular mass (C1-C4 gases, C5-C17 liquids, >C18 solids).

🎙️ Alkanes are non-polar, which means they don't mix with polar solvents like water but dissolve well in other non-polar solvents. Their boiling points generally increase with increasing molecular mass and chain length due to stronger Van der Waals forces. This explains why lighter alkanes are gases, while heavier ones are liquids or solids.

Chemical Properties of Alkanes

  • Relatively unreactive (saturated bonds).
  • Combustion: Alkanes burn in oxygen to produce CO2 and H2O (fuels).
  • Substitution Reactions: E.g., Halogenation (UV light required).

🎙️ Due to their strong C-C and C-H single bonds, alkanes are generally quite stable. Their most common reactions include combustion, where they act as excellent fuels, releasing energy. They also undergo substitution reactions, such as halogenation, where a hydrogen atom is replaced by a halogen, typically requiring UV light.

Unsaturated Hydrocarbons: Alkenes

  • Contain at least one Carbon-Carbon double bond (C=C).
  • General formula: CnH2n.
  • Also known as Olefins (oil-forming).

🎙️ Moving on to unsaturated hydrocarbons, alkenes are characterized by the presence of at least one carbon-carbon double bond. This double bond makes them more reactive than alkanes. Their general formula is CnH2n. They are also called olefins, historically because some formed oily liquids.

Nomenclature of Alkenes (IUPAC)

  • Longest chain containing the C=C bond (parent chain, -ene suffix).
  • Number the chain to give C=C bond the lowest possible number.
  • Indicate position of double bond and substituents.

🎙️ Naming alkenes follows similar IUPAC principles, but with key differences. The parent chain must include the double bond, and the suffix changes to '-ene'. The numbering must prioritize giving the double bond the lowest possible position. Then, substituents are named and positioned as usual.

Worked Example: Naming Alkenes

  • Structure: CH3-CH=CH-CH3
  • Longest chain with C=C: But-2-ene (4 carbons).
  • No substituents. Name: But-2-ene.

🎙️ For this alkene, the longest chain containing the double bond has four carbons, so it's a 'butene'. The double bond starts at the second carbon from either end, so we name it But-2-ene. Remember, the number indicates the starting carbon of the double bond.

Isomerism in Alkenes

  • Chain Isomerism (like alkanes).
  • Position Isomerism (position of double bond).
  • Geometric Isomerism (cis-trans) due to restricted rotation.

🎙️ Alkenes exhibit more types of isomerism than alkanes. Besides chain isomerism, they show position isomerism, where the double bond is at a different location. Crucially, they also display geometric (cis-trans) isomerism due to the restricted rotation around the carbon-carbon double bond, leading to different spatial arrangements.

Chemical Properties of Alkenes

  • More reactive than alkanes due to pi bond.
  • Undergo Addition Reactions.
  • Examples: Hydrogenation (H2), Halogenation (Br2), Hydrohalogenation (HCl).

🎙️ The presence of the pi bond in alkenes makes them significantly more reactive than alkanes. Their characteristic reactions are addition reactions, where atoms are added across the double bond, breaking the pi bond. Common examples include hydrogenation, where hydrogen is added, and halogenation, where halogens are added.

Unsaturated Hydrocarbons: Alkynes

  • Contain at least one Carbon-Carbon triple bond (C≡C).
  • General formula: CnH2n-2.
  • Linear geometry around triple bond.

🎙️ Alkynes are another class of unsaturated hydrocarbons, defined by the presence of at least one carbon-carbon triple bond. They are even more unsaturated than alkenes, with a general formula of CnH2n-2. The triple bond forces a linear geometry around the involved carbon atoms.

  • Longest chain containing the C≡C bond (parent chain, -yne suffix).
  • Number the chain to give C≡C bond the lowest possible number.
  • Indicate position of triple bond and substituents.

🎙️ The IUPAC rules for naming alkynes mirror those for alkenes, but with the suffix '-yne' to denote the triple bond. Again, the parent chain must contain the triple bond, and numbering should prioritize giving the triple bond the lowest possible position. All other substituent rules remain the same.

Chemical Properties of Alkynes

  • Highly reactive due to two pi bonds.
  • Undergo Addition Reactions (often twice).
  • Example: Hydrogenation (H2), Halogenation (Br2).

🎙️ Alkynes are even more reactive than alkenes due to the presence of two pi bonds. They readily undergo addition reactions, often adding two equivalents of a reagent across the triple bond to form saturated compounds. For example, hydrogenation can convert an alkyne first to an alkene, then to an alkane.

Aromatic Hydrocarbons: Benzene

  • Special class of cyclic, unsaturated compounds.
  • Benzene (C6H6) is the simplest aromatic compound.
  • Exhibits exceptional stability (aromaticity).

🎙️ Aromatic hydrocarbons are a distinct and important class. Benzene, with its unique six-carbon ring and delocalized pi electrons, is the archetypal aromatic compound. Its special electronic structure grants it exceptional stability, a property known as aromaticity, making it less reactive than typical unsaturated hydrocarbons.

Nomenclature of Substituted Benzenes

  • Monosubstituted benzenes: Substituent + benzene (e.g., Chlorobenzene).
  • Disubstituted benzenes: Use ortho (o-), meta (m-), para (p-) or numbers.
  • Many common names are accepted by IUPAC (e.g., Toluene, Aniline).

🎙️ Naming benzene derivatives has its own set of rules. For monosubstituted benzenes, it's straightforward. For disubstituted compounds, we use prefixes like ortho, meta, and para to indicate relative positions, or simply numbers. Many common names, like toluene for methylbenzene, are widely accepted.

Recap & Key Takeaways

  • Hydrocarbons: C and H compounds, vital fuels and raw materials.
  • Classes: Aliphatic (Alkanes, Alkenes, Alkynes) & Aromatic (Benzene).

Historical Perspective: Early Concepts

  • Oxidation: Combination with oxygen.
  • Reduction: Removal of oxygen.
  • Example: 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO (Mg is oxidized).
  • Example: ZnO + C → Zn + CO (ZnO is reduced).

🎙️ Historically, oxidation was simply defined as the addition of oxygen to a substance, while reduction was the removal of oxygen. Think about the burning of magnesium, where it combines with oxygen. Conversely, extracting metals from their oxides involved removing oxygen, which was termed reduction.

Modern Definition: Oxidation (Electron Transfer)

  • Oxidation is the LOSS of electrons.
  • Species undergoing oxidation is a REDUCING AGENT.
  • Example: Na → Na⁺ + e⁻
  • Example: Fe²⁺ → Fe³⁺ + e⁻

🎙️ With the advent of electron theory, the definition evolved. Oxidation is now precisely defined as the loss of one or more electrons by an atom, ion, or molecule. The species that loses electrons is itself oxidized, and in doing so, it causes another species to be reduced, hence it's called a reducing agent.

Modern Definition: Reduction (Electron Transfer)

  • Reduction is the GAIN of electrons.
  • Species undergoing reduction is an OXIDIZING AGENT.
  • Example: Cl₂ + 2e⁻ → 2Cl⁻
  • Example: Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu

🎙️ Conversely, reduction is the gain of one or more electrons. The species that gains electrons is reduced, and by accepting electrons, it causes another species to be oxidized. Therefore, it's known as an oxidizing agent. Remember, these processes always occur simultaneously.

🧲

Redox Reactions: The Simultaneous Act

  • Oxidation and Reduction always occur together.
  • One cannot happen without the other.
  • Total electrons lost = Total electrons gained.
  • A redox reaction is a 'reduction-oxidation' reaction.

🎙️ This is a crucial point: oxidation and reduction are inseparable. If one species loses electrons, another species must gain them. It's a transfer of electrons. This fundamental principle means that all redox reactions involve both processes happening concurrently. Hence the term 'redox'.

Redox reactions involve electron transfer between reactants. Oxidation = loss of electrons

Oxidizing Agents (Oxidants)

  • Substances that cause oxidation.
  • They themselves get REDUCED.
  • They ACCEPT electrons.
  • Common examples: O₂, F₂, KMnO₄, K₂Cr₂O₇.

🎙️ An oxidizing agent, or oxidant, is a chemical species that causes another substance to lose electrons (oxidize). In doing so, the oxidizing agent itself gains those electrons and is therefore reduced. They are electron acceptors, essentially 'stealing' electrons from other species.

Reducing Agents (Reductants)

  • Substances that cause reduction.
  • They themselves get OXIDIZED.
  • They DONATE electrons.
  • Common examples: H₂, Na, C, SnCl₂.

🎙️ Conversely, a reducing agent, or reductant, is a chemical species that causes another substance to gain electrons (reduce). The reducing agent itself loses electrons and is therefore oxidized. They are electron donors, readily giving up their electrons to other species.

Identifying Redox Reactions: Electron Transfer Method

  • Look for changes in ionic charges.
  • Identify electron loss (oxidation) and gain (reduction).
  • Example: Zn(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + Cu(s)
  • Zn is oxidized (loses 2e⁻), Cu²⁺ is reduced (gains 2e⁻).

🎙️ One direct way to identify redox reactions is by tracking electron transfer. If you see a species' charge increasing, it's losing electrons (oxidation). If its charge is decreasing, it's gaining electrons (reduction). If both occur, it's a redox reaction.

1Check charges
2Identify e- loss
3Identify e- gain
4Confirm both

The Oxidation Number Concept

  • Hypothetical charge on an atom.
  • Assigned according to a set of rules.
  • Useful for tracking electron transfer in covalent compounds.
  • Not always the actual charge.

🎙️ While electron transfer is clear for ionic compounds, it's harder for covalent ones. This is where the concept of oxidation number comes in handy. It's a bookkeeping tool, representing a hypothetical charge assigned to an atom if all bonds were ionic. It helps us track electron movement even in complex molecules.

Rules for Assigning Oxidation Numbers - Part 1

  • 1. Free elements: ON = 0 (e.g., O₂, Na, Cl₂).
  • 2. Monatomic ions: ON = charge (e.g., Na⁺ is +1, Cl⁻ is -1).
  • 3. Group 1 metals: ON = +1 (e.g., Li, Na, K).
  • 4. Group 2 metals: ON = +2 (e.g., Mg, Ca).

🎙️ Let's learn the rules for assigning oxidation numbers. First, any element in its free, uncombined state has an oxidation number of zero. For simple monatomic ions, the oxidation number is equal to its charge. Alkali metals are always +1, and alkaline earth metals are always +2 in their compounds.

1Elements = 0
2Monatomic = charge
3Group 1 = +1
4Group 2 = +2
11 / 25

Rules for Assigning Oxidation Numbers - Part 2

  • 5. Hydrogen: +1 with non-metals, -1 with metals (hydrides).
  • 6. Oxygen: -2 in most compounds, -1 in peroxides (H₂O₂), +2 with F (OF₂).
  • 7. Halogens: -1 in most compounds, positive with more electronegative elements.
  • 8. Sum of ONs: In a neutral molecule = 0; In a polyatomic ion = ion's charge.

🎙️ Hydrogen usually has +1, except in metal hydrides where it's -1. Oxygen is typically -2, but watch out for peroxides and compounds with fluorine. Halogens are usually -1. Finally, the sum of oxidation numbers in a neutral compound is zero, and in a polyatomic ion, it equals the ion's charge. These rules are applied hierarchically.

1Hydrogen (+1/-1)
2Oxygen (-2, -1, +2)
3Halogens (-1)
4Sum = 0 or charge

Worked Example 1: Assigning Oxidation Numbers

  • Find the oxidation number of Cr in K₂Cr₂O₇.
  • K: +1 (Group 1), O: -2 (most compounds).
  • 2(+1) + 2(Cr) + 7(-2) = 0
  • 2 + 2Cr - 14 = 0 => 2Cr = 12 => Cr = +6.

🎙️ Let's apply the rules. In potassium dichromate, K₂Cr₂O₇, Potassium is +1 and Oxygen is -2. We set up an equation: 2 times the ON of K, plus 2 times the ON of Cr, plus 7 times the ON of O, all sum to zero because it's a neutral compound. Solving for Cr gives us +6.

Oxidation in terms of Oxidation Number

  • Oxidation is an INCREASE in oxidation number.
  • Example: S in H₂S (-2) to S in SO₂ (+4).
  • Increase from -2 to +4 indicates oxidation.
  • The species loses electrons.

🎙️ Using the oxidation number concept, oxidation is defined as an increase in the oxidation number of an element. If an atom's oxidation number goes from a lower value to a higher value, it means it has lost electrons and is therefore oxidized. For instance, from -2 to +4 is an increase, signifying oxidation.

Reduction in terms of Oxidation Number

  • Reduction is a DECREASE in oxidation number.
  • Example: N in HNO₃ (+5) to N in NO (+2).
  • Decrease from +5 to +2 indicates reduction.
  • The species gains electrons.

🎙️ Conversely, reduction is defined as a decrease in the oxidation number of an element. If an atom's oxidation number goes from a higher value to a lower value, it means it has gained electrons and is therefore reduced. A decrease from +5 to +2 clearly shows reduction.

Identifying Redox Reactions: Oxidation Number Method

  • 1. Assign ON to all atoms in reactants and products.
  • 2. Identify atoms whose ON has changed.
  • 3. If ON increases for one atom and decreases for another, it's redox.
  • Example: CuO + H₂ → Cu + H₂O

🎙️ To identify redox reactions using this method, first assign oxidation numbers to every atom in the reaction. Then, compare the oxidation numbers from reactants to products. If you find one element's oxidation number increasing (oxidation) and another's decreasing (reduction), you've identified a redox reaction.

1Assign all ONs
2Find changing ONs
3Increase = Oxidation
4Decrease = Reduction

Worked Example 2: Identifying Redox using ON

  • Reaction: CuO + H₂ → Cu + H₂O
  • ONs: Cu (+2), O (-2) in CuO; H (0) in H₂.
  • ONs: Cu (0) in Cu; H (+1), O (-2) in H₂O.
  • Cu: +2 → 0 (Decrease, Reduction). H: 0 → +1 (Increase, Oxidation).
  • This is a redox reaction.

🎙️ Let's apply this to the reaction of copper oxide with hydrogen. In CuO, Cu is +2. In H₂, H is 0. In the products, Cu metal is 0, and in water, H is +1. We see Cu's ON decreases from +2 to 0 (reduction), and H's ON increases from 0 to +1 (oxidation). Both processes occur, so it is a redox reaction.

Types of Redox Reactions: Combination & Decomposition

  • Combination: A + B → C (e.g., C + O₂ → CO₂).
  • Decomposition: C → A + B (e.g., 2KClO₃ → 2KCl + 3O₂).
  • Many, but not all, are redox.

🎙️ Redox reactions can be classified into different types. Combination reactions involve two or more substances combining to form a single product. Decomposition reactions are the reverse, where a single compound breaks down into two or more simpler substances. It's important to remember that not all combination or decomposition reactions are redox, but many are, especially if elements are involved.

Types of Redox Reactions: Displacement & Disproportionation

  • Displacement: X + YZ → XZ + Y (e.g., Zn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu).
  • Disproportionation: Same element oxidized AND reduced (e.g., 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂).
  • Displacement reactions are always redox.

🎙️ Displacement reactions involve an atom or ion displacing another from a compound. These are always redox. Disproportionation reactions are special: a single element in a single compound is simultaneously oxidized and reduced. Hydrogen peroxide decomposition is a classic example, where oxygen goes from -1 to -2 and 0.

Real-Life Example 1: Corrosion (Rusting of Iron)

  • Slow oxidation of metals by atmospheric oxygen and moisture.
  • Iron: Fe → Fe²⁺ → Fe³⁺ (oxidized).
  • Oxygen: O₂ → O²⁻ (reduced).
  • Damages structures, significant economic impact.

🎙️ A very common real-life redox reaction is corrosion, particularly the rusting of iron. Iron metal slowly oxidizes in the presence of oxygen and water, forming hydrated iron(III) oxide, which is rust. This process involves iron losing electrons and oxygen gaining them, leading to significant material degradation.

Real-Life Example 2: Respiration

Balancing Redox Reactions: Introduction

  • Crucial for stoichiometry calculations.
  • Mass balance (atoms) and Charge balance (electrons).
  • Two main methods: Ion-Electron Method and Oxidation Number Method.
  • Often performed in acidic or basic medium.

🎙️ Balancing redox reactions is essential for quantitative chemistry. Unlike simple reactions, we must balance not only the atoms but also the charges, accounting for electron transfer. There are two primary methods, and the medium (acidic or basic) often dictates specific steps.

⚖️
Equalizing Reactions
22 / 25

Method 1: Ion-Electron Method (Half-Reaction Method)

  • 1. Separate into oxidation and reduction half-reactions.
  • 2. Balance atoms (other than O, H) in each half-reaction.
  • 3. Balance O atoms using H₂O.
  • 4. Balance H atoms using H⁺ (acidic) or H₂O/OH⁻ (basic).
  • 5. Balance charge using electrons (e⁻).
  • 6. Multiply half-reactions to equalize electrons, then add.

🎙️ The ion-electron method, also called the half-reaction method, is very systematic. You first split the overall reaction into two separate half-reactions for oxidation and reduction. Then, you balance atoms and charges step-by-step, adding water and H+ or OH- as needed, and finally combining the balanced half-reactions.

1Split into half-reactions
2Balance atoms (non-O/H)
3Balance O with H₂O
4Balance H with H⁺/H₂O/OH⁻
5Balance charge with e⁻
6Equalize e⁻ & combine

Method 2: Oxidation Number Method

  • 1. Write unbalanced equation, assign ONs to all atoms.
  • 2. Identify atoms undergoing change in ON.
  • 3. Calculate total increase/decrease in ON.
  • 4. Equalize total increase/decrease by multiplying species.
  • 5. Balance remaining atoms (except O, H).
  • 6. Balance O with H₂O, then H with H⁺ (acidic) or H₂O/OH⁻ (basic).

🎙️ The oxidation number method focuses on the change in oxidation numbers. You first assign all oxidation numbers to identify the elements being oxidized and reduced. Then, you balance the total increase in oxidation number with the total decrease by using stoichiometric coefficients. The rest of the balancing follows similar steps as the ion-electron method for atoms and charge.

1Assign ONs
2Identify ON changes
3Calculate total change
4Equalize ON changes
5Balance other atoms
6Balance O with H₂O, H with H⁺/OH⁻

Recap: Key Takeaways from Redox Reactions

  • Oxidation: Loss of electrons / Increase in ON.
  • Reduction: Gain of electrons / Decrease in ON.
  • Redox reactions are simultaneous electron transfers.
  • Oxidizing agents get reduced, reducing agents get oxidized.
  • Two methods for balancing: Ion-Electron and Oxidation Number.

🎙️ To summarize, remember that oxidation is losing electrons or an increase in oxidation number, while reduction is gaining electrons or a decrease in oxidation number. These processes always occur together. Understand the roles of oxidizing and reducing agents, and practice the two main methods for balancing redox reactions.

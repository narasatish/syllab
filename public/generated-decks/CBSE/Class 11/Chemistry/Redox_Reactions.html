Classical Concept: Oxidation & Reduction

  • The earliest definitions were based on oxygen and hydrogen.
  • Helped explain many common chemical changes.
  • Still useful for understanding basic reactions.

🎙️ Historically, chemists first defined oxidation and reduction based on the gain or loss of oxygen and hydrogen. These classical definitions were pioneering for their time.

Oxidation (Classical Definition)

  • Addition of oxygen to a substance.
  • Removal of hydrogen from a substance.
  • Example: 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO (Magnesium gets oxidized)

🎙️ Classically, oxidation means either gaining oxygen or losing hydrogen. Think of rusting iron, where iron gains oxygen to form iron oxide.

1Add Oxygen
2Remove Hydrogen
4 / 25

Reduction (Classical Definition)

  • Removal of oxygen from a substance.
  • Addition of hydrogen to a substance.
  • Example: CuO + H₂ → Cu + H₂O (Copper oxide gets reduced)

🎙️ Conversely, reduction is the opposite: losing oxygen or gaining hydrogen. A common example is the reduction of metal oxides to obtain pure metals.

1Remove Oxygen
2Add Hydrogen

Limitations of Classical Concepts

  • Not all reactions involve oxygen or hydrogen.
  • Couldn't explain reactions in non-aqueous solutions.
  • A broader, more universal definition was needed.

🎙️ While useful, the classical definitions had significant limitations. Many important reactions, especially in organic chemistry or non-aqueous media, don't involve oxygen or hydrogen, necessitating a new approach.

Electron Transfer Concept: Oxidation

  • Oxidation is the loss of one or more electrons.
  • Results in an increase in positive charge or decrease in negative charge.
  • Example: Na → Na⁺ + e⁻

🎙️ The electron transfer concept provides a much broader definition. Oxidation is simply the loss of electrons, making the species more positive or less negative. Remember 'OIL RIG' - Oxidation Is Loss.

⚡️
Losing Electrons

Electron Transfer Concept: Reduction

  • Reduction is the gain of one or more electrons.
  • Results in a decrease in positive charge or increase in negative charge.
  • Example: Cl₂ + 2e⁻ → 2Cl⁻

🎙️ Conversely, reduction is the gain of electrons, making the species more negative or less positive. This is the 'RIG' part of 'OIL RIG' - Reduction Is Gain.

Gaining Electrons

Redox Reactions: Simultaneous Process

  • Oxidation and reduction always occur together.
  • Electrons lost by one species are gained by another.
  • No free electrons exist in a chemical reaction.

🎙️ It's crucial to understand that oxidation and reduction are inseparable. You cannot have one without the other. One species gives electrons, and another takes them immediately.

Redox reactions involve electron transfer between reactants. Oxidation = loss of electrons

Oxidizing Agent (Oxidant)

  • A substance that causes oxidation in another substance.
  • It itself undergoes reduction (gains electrons).
  • Example: O₂, Cl₂, KMnO₄

🎙️ An oxidizing agent is like a 'helper' for oxidation. It oxidizes another substance by accepting electrons from it, meaning the oxidizing agent itself gets reduced.

🛡️
Electron Acceptor
10 / 25

Reducing Agent (Reductant)

  • A substance that causes reduction in another substance.
  • It itself undergoes oxidation (loses electrons).
  • Example: H₂, Na, LiAlH₄

🎙️ Conversely, a reducing agent helps reduction. It reduces another substance by donating electrons to it, and in doing so, the reducing agent itself gets oxidized.

Electron Donor

Worked Example 1: Identifying Agents

  • Reaction: Zn(s) + CuSO₄(aq) → ZnSO₄(aq) + Cu(s)
  • Zn changes to Zn²⁺ (loses 2e⁻) → Oxidation
  • Cu²⁺ changes to Cu (gains 2e⁻) → Reduction

🎙️ In this reaction, zinc loses electrons, so it's oxidized and acts as the reducing agent. Copper(II) ions gain electrons, so they are reduced and CuSO₄ acts as the oxidizing agent.

1Zn is Oxidized
2Cu²⁺ is Reduced
3Zn is Reducing Agent
4CuSO₄ is Oxidizing Agent

Oxidation Number: A Key Concept

  • Hypothetical charge if electrons were completely transferred.

  • Not always the actual charge on an atom.

🎙️ The oxidation number is a very powerful tool. It's an assigned number that helps us keep track of electrons, especially in covalent compounds where actual charges aren't obvious.

Rules for Assigning Oxidation Numbers - Part 1

  • Elements in free state have oxidation number zero (e.g., O₂, Na).
  • Monoatomic ions: Oxidation number equals their charge (e.g., Na⁺ is +1).
  • Group 1 metals: +1; Group 2 metals: +2 in compounds.

🎙️ These are the foundational rules. Remember that elements in their natural, uncombined state always have an oxidation number of zero, like oxygen gas or solid sodium.

1Free elements = 0
2Monatomic ion = charge
3Group 1 = +1
4Group 2 = +2
14 / 25

Rules for Assigning Oxidation Numbers - Part 2

  • Fluorine: always -1 in compounds.
  • Hydrogen: +1 with non-metals, -1 with metals (hydrides).
  • Oxygen: usually -2; peroxides -1; superoxides -½; OF₂ is +2.

🎙️ Hydrogen and oxygen have specific rules with exceptions. Pay close attention to these exceptions, especially for oxygen in peroxides and superoxides, as they are common sources of error.

1Fluorine = -1
2Hydrogen = +1 (non-metals)
3Hydrogen = -1 (metals)
4Oxygen = -2 (common)

Worked Example 2: Assigning Oxidation Numbers

  • Find oxidation number of Mn in KMnO₄.
  • K is +1, O is -2.
  • Let Mn be x. Equation: (+1) + x + 4(-2) = 0.

🎙️ To find the oxidation number of an unknown element, use the known rules for other elements in the compound and set the sum of all oxidation numbers to zero for a neutral compound, or to the charge for an ion.

1K = +1
2O = -2
31 + x - 8 = 0
4x = +7 (for Mn)

Redox using Oxidation Numbers

  • Oxidation: Increase in oxidation number.
  • Reduction: Decrease in oxidation number.
  • This is the most general and widely used definition.

🎙️ This definition is powerful because it applies to all types of reactions, whether or not they involve oxygen, hydrogen, or obvious electron transfer. It simplifies identifying redox processes.

Oxidation (Increase ON)
Reduction (Decrease ON)

Worked Example 3: Identifying Redox

  • Reaction: 2H₂S + SO₂ → 3S + 2H₂O
  • H₂S: H=+1, S=-2. SO₂: O=-2, S=+4. S (product): 0.
  • S in H₂S (-2 to 0) is oxidized. S in SO₂ (+4 to 0) is reduced.

🎙️ In this reaction, sulfur from two different compounds undergoes both oxidation and reduction to form elemental sulfur. This is a classic example of a disproportionation reaction, which we'll discuss soon.

1S from -2 to 0 (Oxidation)
2S from +4 to 0 (Reduction)
3H₂S is Reducing Agent
4SO₂ is Oxidizing Agent

Types of Redox Reactions - Part 1

  • Combination Reactions: A + B → C (e.g., C + O₂ → CO₂).
  • Decomposition Reactions: C → A + B (e.g., 2KClO₃ → 2KCl + 3O₂).
  • Many combination and decomposition reactions are redox.

🎙️ Redox reactions can be classified into different types. Combination reactions involve two or more substances forming a single product, while decomposition reactions involve a single substance breaking down.

Types of Redox Reactions - Part 2

  • Metal Displacement: A metal displaces another metal from its salt solution.
  • Non-metal Displacement: A non-metal displaces another non-metal.
  • Example: Zn(s) + CuSO₄(aq) → ZnSO₄(aq) + Cu(s).

🎙️ Displacement reactions are very common in redox. A more reactive element will displace a less reactive element from its compound. This highlights the concept of reactivity series.

Types of Redox Reactions - Part 3

  • An element in one oxidation state is simultaneously oxidized and reduced.
  • The same element acts as both oxidizing and reducing agent.
  • Example: 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂ (Oxygen goes from -1 to -2 and 0).

🎙️ Disproportionation reactions are unique and important. Here, a single element shows a change in its oxidation state in two opposite directions, both increasing and decreasing, within the same reaction.

Redox in Real Life

  • Corrosion (e.g., rusting of iron): Oxidation of metals.
  • Respiration: Oxidation of glucose to produce energy.
  • Combustion: Rapid oxidation releasing heat and light.

🎙️ Redox reactions are not just theoretical concepts; they are happening all around us, from the rusting of vehicles to the very process of breathing that sustains life. They are fundamental to many natural and industrial processes.

Recap: Key Takeaways

  • Classical definitions: gain/loss of O/H.
  • Modern definitions: loss/gain of electrons; increase/decrease in oxidation number.
  • Oxidizing agents get reduced, reducing agents get oxidized.
  • Different types: combination, decomposition, displacement, disproportionation.

🎙️ To summarize, redox reactions involve simultaneous oxidation and reduction. The electron transfer and oxidation number concepts are crucial for understanding them. Mastering these concepts is key for your exams.

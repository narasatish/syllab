What is Thermodynamics?

  • Branch of science dealing with heat and other forms of energy.
  • Studies the interconversion of different forms of energy.
  • Predicts the feasibility and direction of physical and chemical changes.

🎙️ Thermodynamics is essentially the study of energy and how it moves and transforms. It helps us answer questions like: 'Will this reaction occur on its own?' and 'How much energy will be released or absorbed?'

Key Terms: System, Surroundings, Boundary

  • System: Part of the universe chosen for study (e.g., a chemical reaction in a beaker).
  • Surroundings: Everything else in the universe outside the system.
  • Boundary: The real or imaginary surface separating the system from its surroundings.

🎙️ To study energy changes precisely, we first need to define our 'system'. Imagine a reaction happening in a test tube; the chemicals inside are the system, the test tube and the air around it are the surroundings, and the test tube walls form the boundary.

Types of Systems

  • Open System: Exchanges both energy and matter with surroundings.
  • Closed System: Exchanges energy but NOT matter with surroundings.
  • Isolated System: Exchanges NEITHER energy NOR matter with surroundings.

🎙️ Understanding system types is crucial. An open cup of hot coffee is an open system. A sealed flask of hot coffee is a closed system. An ideal thermos flask is an isolated system, though truly isolated systems are difficult to achieve in practice.

Properties of a System: Extensive vs. Intensive

  • Extensive Properties: Depend on the amount of matter (e.g., mass, volume, internal energy).
  • Intensive Properties: Independent of the amount of matter (e.g., temperature, pressure, density).
  • Ratio of two extensive properties is an intensive property (e.g., density = mass/volume).

🎙️ This distinction is very important. If you take a small piece of a substance, its temperature remains the same, but its mass changes. So, temperature is intensive, and mass is extensive.

State Functions vs. Path Functions

  • State Function: Property whose value depends only on the initial and final states of the system.
  • Independent of the path taken (e.g., Pressure, Volume, Temperature, Internal Energy, Enthalpy).
  • Path Function: Property whose value depends on the path taken between initial and final states.
  • Examples include Heat (q) and Work (w).

🎙️ Imagine climbing a mountain. Your change in altitude (height) is a state function – it only depends on where you started and where you ended. However, the distance you walked to get there is a path function, as it depends on the specific trail you took.

Internal Energy (U)

  • Sum of all forms of energy associated with the atoms and molecules of a system.
  • Includes kinetic energy (translational, rotational, vibrational) and potential energy (intermolecular forces).
  • An extensive property and a state function.

🎙️ Internal energy is the total energy stored within a system. We cannot measure its absolute value, but we can measure the change in internal energy (ΔU) during a process. This change is what truly matters in thermodynamics.

🔥
Total Energy

First Law of Thermodynamics

  • Also known as the Law of Conservation of Energy.
  • Energy can neither be created nor destroyed, only transformed.
  • Mathematical form: ΔU = q + w (where ΔU is change in internal energy, q is heat, w is work).

🎙️ The First Law is a fundamental principle. It tells us that any change in the internal energy of a system must be accounted for by heat absorbed or released, and work done on or by the system. It's a simple energy balance equation.

⚖️
Energy Balance

Work (w) in Thermodynamics

  • Work is energy transferred when a force causes displacement.
  • In chemistry, we often consider pressure-volume (PV) work (expansion/compression).
  • Sign convention: w > 0 (positive) when work is done ON the system (compression).
  • w < 0 (negative) when work is done BY the system (expansion).

🎙️ The sign convention for work is crucial for calculations. Remember, if the system expands, it does work on the surroundings, so its internal energy decreases, hence 'w' is negative. If the surroundings compress the system, work is done on the system, and 'w' is positive.

⚙️
PV Work

Heat (q) in Thermodynamics

  • Heat is energy transferred due to a temperature difference.
  • Sign convention: q > 0 (positive) when heat is absorbed BY the system (endothermic).
  • q < 0 (negative) when heat is released BY the system (exothermic).

🎙️ Similar to work, heat also has a sign convention. If a reaction feels cold, it's absorbing heat from your hand (surroundings), so q is positive. If it feels hot, it's releasing heat, and q is negative. This helps us track energy flow.

Worked Example 1: First Law Application

  • Problem: A system absorbs 200 J of heat and does 150 J of work on the surroundings.
  • Calculate the change in internal energy (ΔU) of the system.
  • Solution: q = +200 J (absorbed), w = -150 J (work done BY system).
  • ΔU = q + w = (+200 J) + (-150 J) = +50 J.

🎙️ This example directly applies the First Law. Pay close attention to the sign conventions for heat and work. 'Absorbs heat' means q is positive. 'Does work on surroundings' means w is negative. A positive ΔU means the system's internal energy increased.

Enthalpy (H)

  • A thermodynamic state function, useful for constant pressure processes.
  • Defined as H = U + PV (Internal Energy + Pressure × Volume).
  • Represents the total heat content of a system at constant pressure.

🎙️ Enthalpy is extremely important in chemistry because many reactions occur at constant atmospheric pressure. It helps us directly relate heat changes to the system's energy content under these common conditions. It's also a state function, so its change depends only on initial and final states.

📈
Heat Content

Enthalpy Change (ΔH)

  • ΔH = H_products - H_reactants.
  • Represents the heat absorbed or released during a reaction at constant pressure.
  • For reactions involving gases, ΔH = ΔU + Δn_g RT (where Δn_g is change in moles of gas).

🎙️ ΔH is the most commonly measured thermodynamic quantity in chemistry. A negative ΔH indicates an exothermic reaction (heat released), and a positive ΔH indicates an endothermic reaction (heat absorbed). This is crucial for understanding reaction energetics.

Exothermic vs. Endothermic Reactions

  • Exothermic: Reactions that release heat to the surroundings (ΔH < 0).
  • Endothermic: Reactions that absorb heat from the surroundings (ΔH > 0).
  • Examples: Combustion (exothermic), Photosynthesis (endothermic).

🎙️ This is a key concept. Exothermic reactions feel hot because they're releasing energy, like burning fuel. Endothermic reactions feel cold because they're taking energy from their surroundings, like dissolving certain salts in water.

Hess's Law of Constant Heat Summation

  • States that the total enthalpy change for a reaction is the same, regardless of the path taken.
  • If a reaction can be expressed as a sum of several steps, the ΔH for the overall reaction is the sum of ΔH values for individual steps.
  • Allows calculation of ΔH for reactions that are difficult to measure directly.

🎙️ Hess's Law is incredibly powerful. Since enthalpy is a state function, the overall change only depends on the initial and final states. This means we can add or subtract known reaction enthalpies to find the enthalpy of a target reaction, even if we can't perform it directly.

Worked Example 2: Hess's Law

  • Given: C(s) + O₂(g) → CO₂(g), ΔH₁ = -393.5 kJ/mol
  • CO(g) + ½O₂(g) → CO₂(g), ΔH₂ = -283.0 kJ/mol
  • Calculate ΔH for C(s) + ½O₂(g) → CO(g).
  • Solution: Target = (Eq 1) - (Eq 2) => ΔH = ΔH₁ - ΔH₂ = (-393.5) - (-283.0) = -110.5 kJ/mol.

🎙️ In this example, we manipulate the given equations to match our target reaction. If you reverse an equation, you change the sign of its ΔH. If you multiply an equation by a factor, you multiply its ΔH by the same factor. Then, simply sum them up.

Heat Capacity (C)

  • Amount of heat required to raise the temperature of a substance by 1°C (or 1 K).
  • Units: J/K or J/°C.
  • Specific Heat Capacity (c): Heat required to raise temperature of 1 gram by 1°C (J/g·K).
  • Molar Heat Capacity (C_m): Heat required to raise temperature of 1 mole by 1°C (J/mol·K).

🎙️ Heat capacity tells us how much heat energy a substance can 'store' for a given temperature change. Water has a high specific heat capacity, meaning it takes a lot of energy to heat it up, which is why it's used as a coolant.

Spontaneity

  • A spontaneous process occurs without external intervention.
  • Doesn't imply speed; a spontaneous reaction can be very slow (e.g., rusting).
  • Non-spontaneous processes require continuous energy input.

🎙️ Spontaneity in thermodynamics simply refers to whether a process can occur on its own under given conditions. It has nothing to do with how fast it happens. A diamond converting to graphite is spontaneous but takes millions of years!

Entropy (S)

  • A measure of the disorder or randomness of a system.
  • Higher disorder means higher entropy.
  • Gases have higher entropy than liquids, which have higher entropy than solids.
  • A state function and an extensive property.

🎙️ Entropy is a concept that often confuses students, but think of it as the degree of 'messiness' or 'randomness'. When you clean your room, you decrease its entropy. When you leave it, it naturally becomes messy again, increasing its entropy.

🎲
Disorder

Second Law of Thermodynamics

  • States that for any spontaneous process, the total entropy of the universe must increase (ΔS_universe > 0).
  • ΔS_universe = ΔS_system + ΔS_surroundings.
  • It sets the direction for all natural processes.

🎙️ The Second Law is why heat flows from hot to cold, and why gas expands to fill a container. These processes increase the overall disorder of the universe. It's a powerful law that explains the directionality of time itself.

➡️
Direction of Change

Gibbs Free Energy (G)

  • A thermodynamic potential that measures the 'useful' or 'available' energy.
  • Criterion for spontaneity at constant temperature and pressure: ΔG = ΔH - TΔS.
  • If ΔG < 0: Process is spontaneous.
  • If ΔG > 0: Process is non-spontaneous.
  • If ΔG = 0: System is at equilibrium.

🎙️ Gibbs Free Energy is the most practical tool for predicting spontaneity in chemistry. It combines the enthalpy (heat) and entropy (disorder) changes. A negative ΔG means the process will occur spontaneously under those conditions.

Worked Example 3: Gibbs Free Energy

  • Problem: For a reaction, ΔH = -120 kJ/mol and ΔS = -40 J/K·mol at 298 K.
  • Calculate ΔG and predict spontaneity.
  • Solution: ΔS = -0.040 kJ/K·mol (convert J to kJ).
  • ΔG = ΔH - TΔS = -120 kJ/mol - (298 K)(-0.040 kJ/K·mol)
  • ΔG = -120 kJ/mol + 11.92 kJ/mol = -108.08 kJ/mol.
  • Since ΔG < 0, the reaction is spontaneous at 298 K.

🎙️ This example shows how to calculate ΔG. Remember to ensure consistent units (kJ or J). A negative ΔG confirms spontaneity. Notice how the negative entropy change (making it less spontaneous) is outweighed by the highly negative enthalpy change (making it more spontaneous).

Common Mistakes & Exam Tips

  • Sign Conventions: Always double-check signs for q, w, and ΔH/ΔG.
  • Units: Be careful with J vs. kJ, and ensure consistent units in calculations (especially for TΔS).
  • Definitions: Clearly understand state vs. path functions, extensive vs. intensive properties.
  • Hess's Law: Practice manipulating equations and their ΔH values correctly.

🎙️ Many errors in thermodynamics stem from incorrect sign conventions or unit conversions. Practice regularly and pay attention to detail. Understanding the underlying definitions will help you avoid conceptual mistakes.

Thermodynamics: Key Takeaways

  • Thermodynamics explains energy changes and spontaneity.
  • First Law: Energy conservation (ΔU = q + w).
  • Second Law: Entropy of universe increases for spontaneous processes (ΔS_universe > 0).
  • Gibbs Free Energy (ΔG = ΔH - TΔS) predicts spontaneity at constant T, P.

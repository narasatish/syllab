Types of Thermodynamic Systems

  • Open System: Exchanges both matter and energy (e.g., open cup of tea).
  • Closed System: Exchanges energy but not matter (e.g., closed flask).
  • Isolated System: Exchanges neither matter nor energy (e.g., thermos flask).

🎙️ These distinctions are crucial because they determine how energy and matter can move in and out of our defined system. Most chemical reactions occur in open or closed systems, making these concepts highly relevant.

State Functions vs. Path Functions

  • State Function: Depends only on initial and final states (e.g., altitude).
  • Path Function: Depends on the path taken (e.g., distance walked).
  • Internal energy, enthalpy, entropy are state functions.

🎙️ Imagine climbing a mountain. Your final altitude is a state function – it only depends on your start and end points. But the distance you walked is a path function, varying with the route chosen.

Intensive vs. Extensive Properties

  • Extensive Properties: Depend on the amount of matter (e.g., mass, volume).
  • Intensive Properties: Independent of the amount of matter (e.g., temperature, density).
  • Think of a samosa: its taste is intensive, its weight is extensive.

🎙️ This distinction helps us understand which properties change when the size of our system changes. If you cut a samosa in half, its taste (intensive) remains the same, but its mass (extensive) halves.

Internal Energy (U): The System's Total Energy

  • Sum of all forms of energy within a system.
  • Includes kinetic (molecular motion) and potential (intermolecular forces) energies.
  • A state function, its absolute value cannot be determined, only change (ΔU).

🎙️ Internal energy is like the total energy bank account of a system, encompassing everything from the jiggling of atoms to the bonds between them. We're usually interested in how this total energy changes, not its absolute value.

Work (W) and Heat (Q): Energy Transfer Modes

  • Work (W): Energy transfer when a force acts over a distance.
  • Both are path functions; their values depend on how the change occurs.

🎙️ These are the two primary ways a system can exchange energy with its surroundings. Think of a piston in an engine: heat can flow in or out, and the piston can do work or have work done on it.

First Law of Thermodynamics: Conservation of Energy

  • Energy can neither be created nor destroyed.
  • It can only be converted from one form to another.
  • Mathematical form: ΔU = Q + W (Change in internal energy = Heat + Work).

🎙️ This law is fundamental! It states that the total energy of an isolated system remains constant. For a non-isolated system, any change in its internal energy must be due to heat absorbed or work done.

Worked Example: Applying the First Law

  • Problem: A system absorbs 100 J of heat and does 50 J of work on the surroundings.
  • Step 1: Q = +100 J (absorbed), W = -50 J (work done by system).
  • Step 2: ΔU = Q + W = 100 J + (-50 J) = 50 J.
  • The internal energy of the system increases by 50 J.

🎙️ Remember the sign conventions: heat absorbed by the system is positive, heat released is negative. Work done by the system is negative, work done on the system is positive. These signs are crucial for correct calculations.

Enthalpy (H): Heat at Constant Pressure

  • Total heat content of a system at constant pressure.
  • H = U + PV (Internal energy + Pressure-Volume work).
  • Change in enthalpy (ΔH) is a state function, often measured in experiments.

🎙️ Many chemical reactions occur in open containers, meaning at constant atmospheric pressure. Enthalpy is a convenient thermodynamic property to track energy changes under these common conditions, simplifying our calculations.

Enthalpy Change (ΔH): Exothermic vs. Endothermic

  • Exothermic Reactions: Release heat, ΔH < 0 (e.g., combustion).
  • Endothermic Reactions: Absorb heat, ΔH > 0 (e.g., melting ice).
  • ΔH represents the heat exchanged at constant pressure.

🎙️ This distinction is crucial for understanding whether a reaction heats up or cools down its surroundings. Most common reactions, like burning fuel, are exothermic, releasing energy into the environment.

Hess's Law: Summing Enthalpy Changes

  • The total enthalpy change for a reaction is the same, regardless of the path taken.
  • If a reaction can be expressed as a sum of other reactions, its ΔH is the sum of their ΔH values.
  • Allows calculation of ΔH for reactions difficult to measure directly.

🎙️ Think of it like a journey: the total elevation change from your starting point to your destination is the same, no matter which route you take. Hess's Law is incredibly useful for calculating enthalpy changes for complex reactions.

Worked Example: Applying Hess's Law

  • Problem: Calculate ΔH for C(s) + 2H₂(g) → CH₄(g) given:
  • 1) C(s) + O₂(g) → CO₂(g) ΔH₁ = -393.5 kJ
  • 2) H₂(g) + ½O₂(g) → H₂O(l) ΔH₂ = -285.8 kJ
  • 3) CH₄(g) + 2O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(l) ΔH₃ = -890.3 kJ

🎙️ To solve this, you'll need to manipulate the given equations (reverse, multiply) so that they sum up to the target equation. Remember, if you reverse an equation, you change the sign of its ΔH. If you multiply, multiply ΔH too. The answer is -74.8 kJ.

Spontaneity: Why Do Reactions Happen?

  • A spontaneous process occurs without external intervention.
  • Many spontaneous processes are exothermic (ΔH < 0).
  • However, some endothermic processes are also spontaneous (e.g., ice melting at room temp).

🎙️ Spontaneity doesn't mean instantaneous; it just means the process *can* happen on its own, given the conditions. It's about the natural tendency of a system. What drives these natural tendencies beyond just heat release?

Second Law of Thermodynamics: The Universe's Tendency

  • For a spontaneous process, the total entropy of the universe (system + surroundings) increases.
  • ΔS_universe = ΔS_system + ΔS_surroundings > 0 for spontaneous processes.
  • This law explains the direction of natural processes.

🎙️ This is a profound law! It tells us that the universe is constantly moving towards greater disorder. While a system's entropy can decrease, it must be compensated by a larger increase in the surroundings' entropy.

Gibbs Free Energy (G): Predicting Spontaneity

  • Combines enthalpy (ΔH) and entropy (ΔS) to predict spontaneity at constant T and P.
  • ΔG = ΔH - TΔS
  • ΔG < 0: Spontaneous; ΔG > 0: Non-spontaneous; ΔG = 0: At equilibrium.

🎙️ Gibbs Free Energy is a powerful tool because it directly tells us if a reaction will proceed spontaneously under specific conditions, without needing to calculate the entropy change of the surroundings. It's the ultimate predictor!

Worked Example: Calculating Gibbs Free Energy

  • Problem: For a reaction, ΔH = -10 kJ, ΔS = +30 J/K at 298 K. Is it spontaneous?
  • Step 1: ΔH = -10 kJ, ΔS = +30 J/K, T = 298 K.
  • Step 2: Convert ΔS to kJ/K: +30 J/K = +0.030 kJ/K.
  • Step 3: ΔG = -10 kJ - (298 K * 0.030 kJ/K) = -10 kJ - 8.94 kJ = -18.94 kJ.
  • Step 4: Since ΔG < 0, the reaction is spontaneous.

🎙️ Pay close attention to units! Enthalpy is usually in kJ, while entropy is often in J/K. Always convert them to be consistent before applying the Gibbs equation to avoid common errors.

Common Mistakes in Thermodynamics

  • Sign Conventions: Misinterpreting Q and W (by/on system, absorbed/released).
  • Units: Inconsistent units for ΔH, ΔS, T in ΔG calculation.
  • State vs. Path Functions: Confusing what depends on the path.
  • Neglecting Surroundings: For spontaneity, remember ΔS_universe.

🎙️ Thermodynamics problems often test your attention to detail. Carefully read the problem statement, double-check your sign conventions, and always ensure your units are consistent before calculating.

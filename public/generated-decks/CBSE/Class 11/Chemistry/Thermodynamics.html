What is Thermodynamics?

  • Branch of science dealing with heat and other forms of energy.
  • Studies the interconversion of different forms of energy.
  • Predicts the feasibility and spontaneity of a process.
  • Does NOT predict the rate of a reaction.

🎙️ Thermodynamics, from Greek 'therme' (heat) and 'dynamis' (power), is all about energy. It helps us understand how heat, work, and other energy forms transform and interact in chemical and physical processes.

Key Terms: System and Surroundings

  • System: The part of the universe under thermodynamic study.
  • Surroundings: Everything else in the universe not part of the system.
  • Boundary: Separates the system from its surroundings (real or imaginary).

🎙️ To study energy changes, we first need to define our area of interest. The 'system' is what we're focusing on, like a beaker with a reaction, and everything outside it is the 'surroundings'.

Types of Systems

  • Open System: Exchanges both matter and energy with surroundings.
  • Closed System: Exchanges energy but NOT matter with surroundings.
  • Isolated System: Exchanges NEITHER matter NOR energy with surroundings.

🎙️ Understanding system types is fundamental. Think of an open beaker of boiling water, a closed flask of boiling water, and a thermos flask as examples of open, closed, and isolated systems, respectively.

State Functions vs. Path Functions

  • State Function: Depends only on initial and final states, not path taken.
  • Examples: Internal Energy (U), Enthalpy (H), Entropy (S), Gibbs Free Energy (G).
  • Path Function: Depends on the path or manner in which the change occurs.
  • Examples: Heat (q), Work (w).

🎙️ This distinction is crucial. Imagine climbing a mountain; your change in altitude is a state function, but the distance you walk depends on the path you choose, making it a path function. Same applies to energy.

Extensive vs. Intensive Properties

  • Extensive Properties: Depend on the amount of matter in the system.
  • Examples: Mass, Volume, Internal Energy, Enthalpy, Heat Capacity.
  • Intensive Properties: Independent of the amount of matter in the system.
  • Examples: Temperature, Pressure, Density, Viscosity, Molar Heat Capacity.

🎙️ Think of it this way: if you take half of a substance, its mass and volume (extensive) will halve, but its temperature and density (intensive) will remain the same. This concept helps us categorize properties.

Internal Energy (U)

  • Total energy contained within a system (kinetic + potential energy of particles).
  • A state function; its absolute value cannot be determined.
  • Change in internal energy (ΔU) is measurable.
  • ΔU = q + w (First Law of Thermodynamics).

🎙️ Internal energy is the sum of all forms of energy in a system. While we can't measure its absolute value, we can precisely determine the change in internal energy (ΔU) during a process, which is very useful.

First Law of Thermodynamics

  • Energy can neither be created nor destroyed, only transformed.
  • Also known as the Law of Conservation of Energy.
  • Mathematical statement: ΔU = q + w.
  • ΔU = change in internal energy, q = heat, w = work.

🎙️ The First Law is foundational. It tells us that any energy gained or lost by a system must come from or go to the surroundings. The total energy of the universe remains constant.

Work (w) and Heat (q) Conventions

  • Work done BY the system (expansion): w = -PΔV (negative).
  • Work done ON the system (compression): w = +PΔV (positive).
  • Heat absorbed BY the system (endothermic): q = positive.
  • Heat released BY the system (exothermic): q = negative.

🎙️ These sign conventions are critical for correctly applying the First Law. Always remember: work done by the system is energy leaving the system, hence negative; heat absorbed by the system is energy entering, hence positive.

Worked Example 1: First Law Application

  • Problem: A system absorbs 500 J of heat and does 200 J of work.
  • Calculate the change in internal energy (ΔU) of the system.
  • Solution: q = +500 J (absorbed), w = -200 J (work done BY system).
  • ΔU = q + w = 500 J + (-200 J) = 300 J.

🎙️ Let's apply the First Law. Remember the sign conventions: heat absorbed is positive, and work done by the system is negative. So, ΔU is 300 J, indicating an increase in the system's internal energy.

Enthalpy (H)

  • Total heat content of a system at constant pressure.
  • Defined as H = U + PV (Internal energy + Pressure-Volume work).
  • A state function; its absolute value cannot be determined.
  • Change in enthalpy (ΔH) is measurable and very useful for reactions.

🎙️ Enthalpy is particularly important in chemistry because most reactions occur at constant atmospheric pressure. ΔH directly gives us the heat absorbed or released under these common conditions.

🔥

Enthalpy Change (ΔH): Exothermic vs. Endothermic

  • Exothermic Reactions: Release heat to surroundings; ΔH is negative.
  • Endothermic Reactions: Absorb heat from surroundings; ΔH is positive.
  • ΔH = H_products - H_reactants.
  • At constant pressure, ΔH = q_p (heat at constant pressure).

🎙️ This is a key concept. Exothermic reactions feel hot because they release energy, while endothermic reactions feel cold as they absorb energy. Many everyday processes are exothermic, like combustion.

Standard Enthalpy of Formation (ΔfH°)

  • Enthalpy change when 1 mole of a compound is formed from its elements.
  • Elements must be in their most stable physical states (standard states).
  • Standard conditions: 298 K (25°C), 1 bar pressure.
  • ΔfH° of an element in its standard state is zero.

🎙️ Standard enthalpy of formation is a reference point. It allows us to calculate the enthalpy change of any reaction using Hess's Law, as long as we know the ΔfH° values for all reactants and products.

Standard Enthalpy of Combustion (ΔcH°)

  • Enthalpy change when 1 mole of a substance undergoes complete combustion.
  • Reacts with sufficient oxygen under standard conditions.
  • Always an exothermic process (ΔcH° is negative).
  • Used to determine calorific values of fuels.

🎙️ Combustion reactions are essential for energy generation. The standard enthalpy of combustion helps us quantify the energy released when fuels burn, which is critical for industrial and biological processes.

Hess's Law of Constant Heat Summation

  • If a reaction occurs in several steps, the total enthalpy change is the sum of enthalpy changes for each step.
  • It applies because enthalpy is a state function.
  • Allows calculation of ΔH for reactions difficult to measure directly.
  • Manipulate known reactions (reverse, multiply) to match target reaction.

🎙️ Hess's Law is a powerful tool. It means that no matter how many steps a reaction takes, the overall energy change is the same as if it happened in one step. This is incredibly useful for complex reactions.

Worked Example 2: Applying Hess's Law

  • Target: C(s) + 2H₂(g) → CH₄(g); ΔH = ?
  • Given: (1) C(s) + O₂(g) → CO₂(g); ΔH₁ = -393.5 kJ
  • (2) H₂(g) + ½O₂(g) → H₂O(l); ΔH₂ = -285.8 kJ
  • (3) CH₄(g) + 2O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(l); ΔH₃ = -890.3 kJ
  • Solution: (1) + 2*(2) + reverse(3) yields ΔH = -74.8 kJ.

🎙️ To solve this, we combine the given equations. Equation (1) is kept as is. Equation (2) is multiplied by 2. Equation (3) is reversed, changing its sign. Summing these adjusted ΔH values gives the final answer.

Spontaneity: What Drives Reactions?

  • Spontaneous Process: Occurs without continuous external intervention.
  • Non-spontaneous Process: Requires continuous external energy input.
  • Spontaneity does NOT imply fast reaction rate.
  • Factors influencing spontaneity: Enthalpy (ΔH) and Entropy (ΔS).

🎙️ A spontaneous process is one that happens naturally, like a ball rolling downhill. It doesn't mean it happens quickly; rust formation is spontaneous but slow. We need to understand what makes processes spontaneous.

Entropy (S): Measure of Disorder

  • Measure of the randomness or disorder of a system.
  • A state function; units: J/K/mol.
  • Higher disorder = Higher entropy.
  • Solid < Liquid < Gas (in terms of entropy).

🎙️ Entropy is often called the arrow of time because the universe naturally tends towards greater disorder. Understanding entropy helps us predict the direction of spontaneous change.

Second Law of Thermodynamics

  • For any spontaneous process, the total entropy of the universe increases.
  • ΔS_universe = ΔS_system + ΔS_surroundings > 0 for spontaneous processes.
  • Systems tend towards states of higher probability (greater disorder).
  • Explains why heat flows from hot to cold, and gases expand.

🎙️ The Second Law is profound. It dictates the direction of natural processes. While a system's entropy can decrease, it must be compensated by an even larger increase in the surroundings' entropy, ensuring the universe's overall entropy always rises.

Gibbs Free Energy (G): Spontaneity Criterion

  • Combines enthalpy and entropy to predict spontaneity at constant T and P.
  • Defined as G = H - TS.
  • Change in Gibbs Free Energy: ΔG = ΔH - TΔS.
  • A state function; units: J/mol or kJ/mol.

🎙️ Gibbs Free Energy is the ultimate tool for predicting spontaneity in chemical reactions under typical lab conditions. It balances the system's tendency to minimize energy (ΔH) and maximize disorder (ΔS).

Gibbs Free Energy and Spontaneity

  • If ΔG < 0: Process is spontaneous (favored).
  • If ΔG > 0: Process is non-spontaneous (unfavored, spontaneous in reverse).
  • If ΔG = 0: Process is at equilibrium.
  • The sign of ΔG depends on ΔH, ΔS, and Temperature (T).

🎙️ This table is crucial for exam questions. Remember, a negative ΔG means the reaction will proceed on its own. A positive ΔG means it won't, and you'd need to input energy for it to happen.

ΔG < 0 (Spontaneous)
ΔG > 0 (Non-spontaneous)
ΔG = 0 (Equilibrium)

Worked Example 3: Calculating ΔG

  • Problem: For a reaction, ΔH = -30 kJ/mol and ΔS = -50 J/K/mol at 298 K.
  • Calculate ΔG and determine spontaneity.
  • Solution: Convert ΔS to kJ/K/mol: -50 J/K/mol = -0.050 kJ/K/mol.
  • ΔG = ΔH - TΔS = -30 kJ/mol - (298 K)(-0.050 kJ/K/mol).
  • ΔG = -30 kJ/mol + 14.9 kJ/mol = -15.1 kJ/mol. Since ΔG < 0, the reaction is spontaneous.

🎙️ Always ensure your units for ΔH and TΔS are consistent before calculation, usually kJ/mol. Here, despite a negative ΔS, the negative ΔH is large enough to make ΔG negative, indicating spontaneity.

Common Mistakes & Exam Tips

  • Sign Conventions: Always be careful with 'q' and 'w' signs.
  • Units: Ensure consistency (e.g., J vs. kJ) for ΔG calculations.
  • State vs. Path Functions: Don't confuse them, especially for ΔU, ΔH, q, w.
  • Hess's Law: Practice manipulating equations and their ΔH values.

🎙️ Many students lose marks due to these common errors. Pay close attention to unit conversions and sign conventions. Practice numerical problems regularly to solidify your understanding.

Recap: Key Takeaways from Thermodynamics

  • Thermodynamics studies energy changes and spontaneity.
  • First Law: Energy conservation (ΔU = q + w).
  • Enthalpy (ΔH): Heat at constant pressure, indicates exo/endothermic.
  • Entropy (ΔS): Measure of disorder, universe tends to increasing ΔS.
  • Gibbs Free Energy (ΔG): Predicts spontaneity (ΔG = ΔH - TΔS).

Why Do Atoms Bond?

  • To attain stability.
  • To lower potential energy.
  • To achieve noble gas configuration (octet/duplet).

🎙️ Atoms bond primarily to achieve a more stable state, which usually means lowering their overall potential energy. This stability is often associated with completing their outermost electron shells, similar to noble gases.

The Octet Rule: A Guiding Principle

  • Atoms tend to have eight electrons in their valence shell.
  • Achieved by gaining, losing, or sharing electrons.
  • Exceptions exist for smaller atoms (duplet) and larger atoms.

🎙️ The Octet Rule states that atoms tend to react in ways that give them eight valence electrons. While very useful, remember that it's a generalization and has several important exceptions, especially for elements in periods beyond the second.

Ionic Bond: Complete Electron Transfer

  • Formed between metals and non-metals.
  • Metal loses electrons to form cations.
  • Non-metal gains electrons to form anions.
  • Electrostatic attraction holds ions together.

🎙️ An ionic bond is essentially an electrostatic force of attraction between oppositely charged ions. This occurs when one atom completely transfers its valence electrons to another, typically between elements with large electronegativity differences.

Lattice Energy: Strength of Ionic Bonds

  • Energy released when 1 mole of ionic compound forms from gaseous ions.
  • Higher lattice energy means stronger ionic bond.
  • Influenced by charge magnitude and ionic radii.

🎙️ Lattice energy is a measure of the strength of an ionic bond. Compounds with higher lattice energy are generally more stable and have higher melting points. Remember, smaller ions and higher charges lead to greater lattice energy.

Covalent Bond: Mutual Electron Sharing

  • Formed between two non-metal atoms.
  • Atoms share one or more pairs of electrons.
  • Achieves stable electron configuration for both atoms.

🎙️ Covalent bonds are formed by the mutual sharing of electrons, typically between non-metal atoms. This sharing allows both atoms to achieve a stable electron configuration, often fulfilling the octet rule.

Formal Charge: Assessing Electron Distribution

  • Hypothetical charge on an atom in a molecule.
  • Assumes equal sharing of bonding electrons.
  • Helps identify the most stable Lewis structure.

🎙️ Formal charge helps us evaluate the electron distribution within a molecule and choose the most plausible Lewis structure when multiple options exist. The structure with formal charges closest to zero on all atoms, especially the most electronegative ones, is usually preferred.

Resonance Structures: Delocalized Electrons

  • When a single Lewis structure is insufficient.
  • Multiple structures contribute to the actual molecule.
  • Electrons are delocalized, not fixed between two atoms.

🎙️ Resonance occurs when a molecule cannot be accurately represented by a single Lewis structure. The actual structure is a hybrid of all contributing resonance forms, where electrons are delocalized over several atoms, enhancing stability.

VSEPR Theory: Predicting Molecular Shapes

  • Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion Theory.
  • Electron pairs repel each other.
  • Arrangement minimizes repulsion, determining geometry.

🎙️ VSEPR theory is a powerful tool for predicting the three-dimensional shape of molecules. The core idea is that electron pairs, both bonding and non-bonding, around a central atom will arrange themselves as far apart as possible to minimize repulsion.

📐
Predicting geometry

Common VSEPR Geometries

  • Linear (2 electron domains).
  • Trigonal Planar (3 electron domains).
  • Tetrahedral (4 electron domains).
  • Trigonal Bipyramidal (5 electron domains).
  • Octahedral (6 electron domains).

🎙️ These are the fundamental geometries determined by the number of electron domains around the central atom. Remember that lone pairs also count as electron domains and influence the overall shape, even if they aren't 'visible' bonds.

Worked Example: VSEPR for NH₃ and H₂O

  • NH₃: 4 electron domains (3 bond, 1 lone pair) → Trigonal pyramidal.
  • H₂O: 4 electron domains (2 bond, 2 lone pairs) → Bent/V-shaped.
  • Lone pair repulsion is greater than bond pair repulsion.

🎙️ Let's apply VSEPR to ammonia and water. Both have four electron domains, but the number of lone pairs differs, leading to distinct molecular shapes: trigonal pyramidal for ammonia and bent for water. This highlights the importance of lone pair repulsion.

Polarity: Dipole Moment

  • Bond polarity: Unequal sharing of electrons due to electronegativity difference.
  • Molecular polarity: Overall dipole moment of a molecule.
  • Depends on bond polarities AND molecular geometry.

🎙️ Polarity is a crucial concept. A bond can be polar if there's an electronegativity difference. However, a molecule's overall polarity depends not only on its bond polarities but also on its 3D geometry, as bond dipoles can cancel out.

↕️
Charge separation

Worked Example: Polarity of CO₂ vs H₂O

  • CO₂: Linear, bond dipoles cancel out → Non-polar.
  • H₂O: Bent, bond dipoles do not cancel out → Polar.
  • Symmetry plays a key role in molecular polarity.

🎙️ This example beautifully illustrates how geometry affects polarity. Both CO2 and H2O have polar bonds, but CO2's linear symmetry leads to a net zero dipole moment, making it non-polar, unlike bent and polar water.

Valence Bond Theory (VBT)

  • Explains bond formation through overlapping atomic orbitals.
  • Greater overlap leads to stronger bonds.
  • Introduces sigma (σ) and pi (π) bonds.

🎙️ Valence Bond Theory describes covalent bond formation as the overlap of atomic orbitals. It helps us understand the directionality of bonds and introduces the concepts of sigma bonds (head-on overlap) and pi bonds (sideways overlap).

Hybridisation: Mixing Atomic Orbitals

  • The concept of mixing atomic orbitals to form new hybrid orbitals.
  • Hybrid orbitals are equivalent in energy and shape.
  • Provides suitable orbitals for bonding and explains molecular geometry.

🎙️ Hybridisation is a theoretical concept that explains how atoms can form more bonds than their simple atomic orbital configurations would suggest. It provides a basis for understanding the observed molecular geometries.

Types of Hybridisation: sp, sp², sp³

  • sp hybridisation: 1 s + 1 p orbital → 2 sp orbitals (Linear).
  • sp² hybridisation: 1 s + 2 p orbitals → 3 sp² orbitals (Trigonal Planar).
  • sp³ hybridisation: 1 s + 3 p orbitals → 4 sp³ orbitals (Tetrahedral).

🎙️ Each type of hybridisation corresponds to a specific number of hybrid orbitals and a characteristic geometry. Knowing the hybridisation allows you to predict the bond angles and shape around a central atom.

sp (Linear)
sp² (Trigonal Planar)
sp³ (Tetrahedral)

Worked Example: Hybridisation in C₂H₄ (Ethene)

  • Each carbon atom forms 3 sigma bonds (2 with H, 1 with C).
  • Requires sp² hybridisation for each carbon.
  • Remaining p orbitals form a pi bond between carbons.

🎙️ Let's determine the hybridisation of carbon in ethene. Each carbon is bonded to two hydrogens and one carbon, forming three sigma bonds. This indicates sp² hybridisation for each carbon atom, leading to a planar geometry around each carbon.

Molecular Orbital Theory (MOT)

  • Atomic orbitals combine to form molecular orbitals (MOs).
  • MOs are occupied by electrons, similar to atomic orbitals.
  • Explains bonding and anti-bonding orbitals.

🎙️ Molecular Orbital Theory offers a more advanced description of bonding, where atomic orbitals combine to form new molecular orbitals that span the entire molecule. It successfully explains properties like paramagnetism of oxygen, which VBT cannot.

MOT: Bond Order and Magnetic Properties

  • Bond Order = ½ (N_b - N_a), where N_b is bonding, N_a is anti-bonding electrons.
  • Higher bond order indicates greater bond strength and stability.
  • Presence of unpaired electrons in MOs predicts paramagnetism.

🎙️ Bond order is a direct consequence of MOT and correlates with bond strength and stability. Moreover, MOT can predict the magnetic properties of molecules, such as whether a substance is paramagnetic (attracted to a magnetic field) or diamagnetic.

The Octet Rule: Atoms Crave Stability!

  • Atoms tend to gain, lose, or share electrons.
  • Goal: Achieve 8 electrons in their outermost shell.
  • This configuration is like that of noble gases (highly stable).

🎙️ The Octet Rule is a cornerstone of chemical bonding. It states that atoms strive to have eight electrons in their valence shell, just like the noble gases, which are known for their inertness and stability. This rule helps us predict how atoms will interact with each other.

5 / 29

Exceptions to the Octet Rule

  • Some atoms can have fewer than 8 electrons (e.g., H, Li, Be, B).
  • Some can have more than 8 electrons (expanded octet, e.g., PCl5, SF6).
  • Odd-electron molecules also exist (e.g., NO, NO2).

🎙️ While the Octet Rule is very useful, it's not universally applicable. There are important exceptions, particularly for elements in the second period (due to lack of d-orbitals) and third period onwards (due to availability of d-orbitals). Be mindful of these exceptions when drawing Lewis structures.

Kossel-Lewis Approach: Visualizing Electrons

  • Introduced the concept of valence electrons.
  • Atoms represented by element symbol + dots for valence electrons.
  • Helps understand electron transfer/sharing during bond formation.

🎙️ The Kossel-Lewis approach provided a simple yet powerful way to visualize chemical bonding. By representing valence electrons as dots, we can easily track their movement and understand how atoms achieve their stable configurations. This is the basis for drawing Lewis dot structures.

Lewis Dot Structures: Step-by-Step

  • 1. Count total valence electrons.
  • 2. Identify central atom (usually least electronegative, never H).
  • 3. Form single bonds to central atom.
  • 4. Distribute remaining electrons as lone pairs to satisfy octets.

🎙️ Drawing Lewis dot structures is a fundamental skill. Follow these steps systematically to correctly represent the arrangement of electrons in a molecule. Remember to always ensure that all atoms, especially the central one, achieve their octet (or duet for hydrogen) if possible.

Worked Example: Drawing CO2 Lewis Structure

  • Total valence electrons: C (4) + O (2x6) = 16.
  • Central atom: Carbon.
  • Form C-O single bonds, then add lone pairs to O.
  • Convert lone pairs to double bonds to satisfy C's octet.

🎙️ Let's apply the steps to carbon dioxide. Initially, carbon won't have an octet with just single bonds and lone pairs on oxygen. We then need to form double bonds by shifting lone pairs from oxygen to carbon, ensuring all atoms satisfy the octet rule. This example illustrates the formation of multiple bonds.

Ionic Bonding: The 'Give and Take' Relationship

  • Complete transfer of one or more electrons.
  • Forms positively charged cations and negatively charged anions.
  • Electrostatic attraction holds ions together (strong bond).

🎙️ Ionic bonding is like a complete handover of electrons. One atom, typically a metal, loses electrons to become a cation, while another atom, usually a non-metal, gains those electrons to become an anion. The strong electrostatic forces between these oppositely charged ions form a very stable bond.

Factors Favouring Ionic Bond Formation

  • Low Ionization Enthalpy for metal (easy electron loss).
  • High Electron Gain Enthalpy for non-metal (easy electron gain).
  • High Lattice Enthalpy for the ionic compound (high stability).

🎙️ The formation of an ionic bond is energetically favorable when these three conditions are met. A metal that readily loses electrons and a non-metal that readily accepts them will form a stable ionic compound, further stabilised by a high lattice enthalpy, which we'll discuss next.

Lattice Enthalpy: Strength of Ionic Bonds

  • Energy released when 1 mole of ionic compound forms from gaseous ions.
  • Measures the strength of electrostatic attraction in a crystal lattice.
  • Higher lattice enthalpy means a more stable ionic compound.

🎙️ Lattice enthalpy is a crucial concept for understanding the stability of ionic compounds. It quantifies the energy required to break apart one mole of an ionic solid into its constituent gaseous ions, or conversely, the energy released during its formation. Higher values indicate stronger bonds and greater stability.

Covalent Bonding: The 'Sharing is Caring' Principle

  • Mutual sharing of electrons between two atoms.
  • Atoms achieve stable octet/duet by sharing.
  • Forms molecules, typically between non-metals.

🎙️ In contrast to ionic bonding, covalent bonding involves the sharing of electrons. This 'sharing is caring' approach allows both participating atoms to achieve a stable electron configuration, often an octet, without completely transferring electrons. This type of bonding is predominant in organic chemistry and between non-metal elements.

Types of Covalent Bonds: Single, Double, Triple

  • Single bond: One shared pair of electrons (e.g., H-H).
  • Double bond: Two shared pairs of electrons (e.g., O=O).
  • Triple bond: Three shared pairs of electrons (e.g., N≡N).

🎙️ Covalent bonds can vary in their strength and number of shared electron pairs. A single bond is the weakest and longest, while a triple bond is the strongest and shortest due to increased electron density between the nuclei. Understanding these types is essential for drawing accurate Lewis structures and predicting molecular properties.

Formal Charge: Assigning Electron Ownership

  • Hypothetical charge on an atom in a molecule/ion.
  • Helps in selecting the most stable Lewis structure.
  • Formula: (Valence e-) - (Non-bonding e-) - (1/2 Bonding e-).

🎙️ Formal charge is a useful tool for evaluating the 'best' or most probable Lewis structure when multiple valid structures can be drawn. The most stable structure typically has formal charges closest to zero on all atoms, and any negative formal charges reside on the more electronegative atoms. It's a theoretical charge, not the actual charge on the atom.

Bond Parameters (Part 1): Length and Angle

  • Bond Length: Equilibrium distance between nuclei of two bonded atoms.
  • Bond Angle: Angle between orbitals containing bonding electron pairs.
  • Both influence molecular shape and properties.

🎙️ Bond parameters provide quantitative information about chemical bonds. Bond length is influenced by factors like atomic size and bond order (triple bonds are shorter than single bonds). Bond angle, on the other hand, is crucial for determining the overall geometry of a molecule, which dictates many of its physical and chemical properties.

Bond Parameters (Part 2): Enthalpy and Order

  • Bond Enthalpy: Energy required to break one mole of a specific bond.
  • Higher bond enthalpy means stronger bond.
  • Bond Order: Number of bonds between two atoms (single=1, double=2, triple=3).

🎙️ Bond enthalpy is a direct measure of bond strength; the more energy needed to break a bond, the stronger it is. Bond order is a simple count that correlates strongly with bond length and enthalpy. Higher bond order generally means shorter bond length and higher bond enthalpy, signifying a stronger and more stable connection between atoms.

Resonance Structures: Multiple Representations

  • When a single Lewis structure cannot describe a molecule accurately.
  • Actual structure is a hybrid of two or more contributing structures.
  • Example: Carbonate ion (CO3^2-), Benzene (C6H6).

🎙️ Resonance occurs when electrons are delocalized over several atoms, rather than being confined to a single bond. This delocalization leads to increased stability. Remember, resonance structures are not rapidly interconverting forms; the actual molecule is a single, averaged hybrid of all contributing forms, none of which perfectly represents it alone.

Polarity of Bonds: Uneven Electron Sharing

  • Arises from differences in electronegativity between bonded atoms.
  • Polar covalent bonds have partial positive (δ+) and partial negative (δ-) charges.
  • Dipole moment (μ) measures overall molecular polarity.

🎙️ When atoms with different electronegativities form a covalent bond, the electrons are not shared equally. This creates a dipole, making the bond polar. The overall polarity of a molecule, measured by its dipole moment, depends not only on bond polarity but also on the molecule's geometry, as bond dipoles can cancel each other out.

VSEPR Theory: Predicting Molecular Shapes

  • Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion Theory.
  • Electron pairs (bonding and lone) repel each other.
  • They arrange themselves to minimize repulsion, determining geometry.

🎙️ VSEPR theory is a powerful and simple model for predicting the three-dimensional shapes of molecules. It's based on the idea that electron pairs, being negatively charged, will position themselves as far apart as possible around a central atom to minimize repulsive forces. This arrangement dictates the molecule's geometry and its properties.

VSEPR Shapes: No Lone Pairs on Central Atom

  • 2 electron pairs: Linear (180°, e.g., BeCl2).
  • 3 electron pairs: Trigonal Planar (120°, e.g., BF3).
  • 4 electron pairs: Tetrahedral (109.5°, e.g., CH4).

🎙️ When only bonding pairs are present around the central atom, the geometry is straightforward. The electron pairs spread out maximally. For example, with four bonding pairs, they point to the corners of a tetrahedron, giving the familiar tetrahedral geometry of methane. These are ideal geometries.

VSEPR Shapes: With Lone Pairs on Central Atom

  • Lone pair-lone pair repulsion > lone pair-bond pair > bond pair-bond pair.
  • Lone pairs distort ideal geometries, reducing bond angles.
  • Example: NH3 (Pyramidal), H2O (Bent).

🎙️ The presence of lone pairs significantly impacts molecular geometry. Lone pairs occupy more space than bonding pairs and exert greater repulsive forces, compressing the bond angles. This leads to deviations from the ideal geometries, resulting in shapes like pyramidal for ammonia and bent for water, even though both have four electron domains.

Valence Bond Theory (VBT): Orbital Overlap

  • Chemical bonds form by overlapping atomic orbitals.
  • Electrons in overlapping orbitals pair up with opposite spins.
  • Greater overlap leads to stronger bonds.

🎙️ Valence Bond Theory provides a quantum mechanical explanation for covalent bond formation. It postulates that a covalent bond forms when atomic orbitals of two atoms overlap, allowing their electrons to share the same region of space. The extent of this overlap directly correlates with the strength of the resulting bond.

Atomic orbitals have characteristic shapes based on their angular momentum quantum number.

Types of Overlap: Sigma (σ) and Pi (π) Bonds

  • Sigma (σ) bond: Formed by head-on (axial) overlap of orbitals.
  • Pi (π) bond: Formed by sideways (lateral) overlap of p-orbitals.
  • Single bonds are always σ; double/triple bonds contain 1σ + 1/2π.

🎙️ These are the two fundamental types of covalent bonds based on the geometry of orbital overlap. Sigma bonds are stronger due to direct overlap, while pi bonds are weaker but crucial for forming multiple bonds. Understanding their formation is key to explaining molecular rigidity and reactivity.

Hybridisation: Mixing Atomic Orbitals

  • Concept of mixing atomic orbitals to form new hybrid orbitals.
  • Hybrid orbitals are equivalent in energy and shape.
  • Explains observed molecular geometries that VBT alone can't.

🎙️ Hybridisation is a theoretical concept that helps explain the observed geometries of molecules, especially when simple atomic orbital overlap doesn't fit. By mixing atomic orbitals like s and p, new hybrid orbitals are formed that are identical and oriented in space to minimize repulsion, thus dictating the molecular shape.

Common Mistakes in Chemical Bonding

  • Incorrectly counting valence electrons for Lewis structures.
  • Forgetting lone pairs when applying VSEPR theory.
  • Confusing electron geometry with molecular geometry.
  • Not recognizing resonance structures when applicable.

🎙️ Many students make common errors in this chapter. Always double-check your valence electron count. Remember that lone pairs are just as important as bonding pairs in VSEPR theory and significantly influence the final molecular shape. Practice identifying when resonance is necessary to accurately represent a molecule.

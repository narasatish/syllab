Why Do Atoms Combine?

  • Atoms combine to achieve stability.
  • Lowering of energy is the driving force.
  • Achieve noble gas configuration (octet or duplet).
  • Forming bonds releases energy, making the system more stable.

🎙️ Atoms in nature are not always stable on their own. They combine with other atoms primarily to attain a state of lower energy and greater stability. This usually means achieving the electron configuration of the nearest noble gas, either by gaining, losing, or sharing electrons.

The Octet Rule

  • Atoms tend to have eight electrons in their outermost shell.
  • Achieved by gaining, losing, or sharing electrons.
  • Applies mainly to main group elements.
  • A stable electron configuration, like noble gases.

🎙️ The octet rule is a foundational concept in chemical bonding. It states that atoms strive to have eight electrons in their valence shell, making them chemically stable. This can be achieved through various types of bonding, which we will explore next.

Ionic Bond Formation

  • Formed by complete transfer of electrons.
  • Occurs between a metal and a non-metal.
  • Results in formation of oppositely charged ions.
  • Electrostatic attraction holds ions together.

🎙️ An ionic bond is a strong bond formed when one atom completely transfers one or more electrons to another atom. Typically, a metal atom loses electrons to become a positively charged cation, and a non-metal atom gains electrons to become a negatively charged anion. These oppositely charged ions then attract each other strongly.

Factors Affecting Ionic Bond Formation

  • Low Ionisation Enthalpy for metal (easy electron loss).
  • High Electron Gain Enthalpy for non-metal (easy electron gain).
  • High Lattice Enthalpy of the ionic compound (strong attraction).
  • These factors contribute to the overall stability of the ionic compound.

🎙️ The formation of a stable ionic bond is favored by several energetic factors. A metal that readily loses electrons (low ionization enthalpy) and a non-metal that readily accepts electrons (high electron gain enthalpy) are ideal. Most importantly, the lattice enthalpy, which is the energy released when ions form a crystal lattice, must be high, indicating strong attractive forces.

Properties of Ionic Compounds

  • High melting and boiling points due to strong electrostatic forces.
  • Generally hard and brittle solids.
  • Soluble in polar solvents (e.g., water).
  • Conduct electricity in molten state or aqueous solution, not solid state.

🎙️ Ionic compounds exhibit distinct properties stemming from their strong ionic bonds. Their high melting points are a direct consequence of the immense energy required to overcome the strong electrostatic forces holding the ions in the crystal lattice. They are also good conductors when their ions are free to move, either in a molten state or when dissolved in water.

Covalent Bond Formation

  • Formed by mutual sharing of electrons.
  • Occurs between two non-metal atoms.
  • Atoms achieve octet/duplet by sharing electron pairs.
  • Can be single, double, or triple bonds.

🎙️ Unlike ionic bonds, covalent bonds involve the sharing of electrons between atoms. This typically happens between two non-metal atoms where neither atom has a strong enough tendency to completely lose or gain electrons. By sharing, both atoms can achieve a stable electron configuration.

Lewis Dot Structures: Drawing Molecules

  • Represent valence electrons as dots around the atomic symbol.
  • Shared pairs form bonds, unshared pairs are lone pairs.
  • Follow the octet rule for most atoms.
  • Crucial for visualizing electron distribution.

🎙️ Lewis structures are a simple yet powerful tool to represent the bonding in molecules and polyatomic ions. The steps involve counting total valence electrons, arranging atoms, forming single bonds, and then distributing remaining electrons to satisfy the octet rule. Practice is key to mastering this.

1Count total valence e-
2Arrange atoms, bond with single lines
3Complete octets for outer atoms
4Place remaining e- on central atom
5Form multiple bonds if needed

Worked Example: Drawing Lewis Structure of CO₂

  • Step 1: Count total valence electrons (C=4, O=6x2=12; Total = 16).
  • Step 2: Place C as central atom, bond to two O atoms (O-C-O). (4 e- used, 12 remaining).
  • Step 3: Distribute 12 e- to outer O atoms (6 on each O). Both O atoms have octets.
  • Step 4: Central C has only 4 e-. Move two lone pairs from O atoms to form double bonds. (O=C=O).

🎙️ Let's walk through CO₂. After forming single bonds and satisfying the outer atoms' octets, the central carbon still lacks an octet. This is where you convert lone pairs from the adjacent oxygen atoms into double bonds, ensuring all atoms achieve their octet. This leads to the O=C=O structure.

Formal Charge

  • Hypothetical charge on an atom in a molecule/ion.
  • Assumes equal sharing of bonding electrons.
  • Helps in selecting the most stable Lewis structure.
  • Formula: Valence e⁻ - Non-bonding e⁻ - ½ Bonding e⁻.

🎙️ Formal charge helps us assess the electron distribution within a molecule and choose the most plausible Lewis structure, especially when multiple structures are possible. The most stable structure typically has formal charges closest to zero on all atoms, and any negative formal charge resides on the more electronegative atom.

Limitations of the Octet Rule

  • Incomplete Octet: Central atom has fewer than 8 electrons (e.g., BF₃).
  • Expanded Octet: Central atom has more than 8 electrons (e.g., PCl₅, SF₆).
  • Odd-Electron Molecules: Molecules with an odd number of valence electrons (e.g., NO, NO₂).
  • Does not explain shapes or relative bond strengths.

🎙️ While the octet rule is very useful, it's not universally applicable. There are significant exceptions like molecules with an incomplete octet, expanded octets (common for elements in Period 3 and beyond), and odd-electron species. These limitations highlight the need for more advanced theories to explain bonding.

VSEPR Theory: Predicting Molecular Shapes

  • Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion Theory.
  • Electron pairs (bonding & lone) repel each other.
  • Electron pairs arrange themselves to minimize repulsion.
  • This arrangement determines the molecular geometry.

🎙️ VSEPR theory is a powerful tool for predicting the three-dimensional shape of molecules. The core idea is that electron pairs, whether shared in bonds or existing as lone pairs, repel each other and will arrange themselves as far apart as possible around the central atom to minimize these repulsions. This leads to specific molecular geometries.

VSEPR Theory: Common Geometries

  • 2 electron pairs: Linear (e.g., BeCl₂)
  • 3 electron pairs: Trigonal Planar (e.g., BF₃)
  • 4 electron pairs: Tetrahedral (e.g., CH₄)
  • Lone pairs distort ideal geometries (e.g., NH₃, H₂O).

🎙️ To apply VSEPR, first count the total number of electron pairs around the central atom. This gives the electron geometry. Then, consider how many of these are lone pairs. Lone pairs exert more repulsion than bonding pairs, leading to distortions and different molecular geometries even if the electron geometry is the same.

1Count electron pairs
2Determine electron geometry
3Identify lone pairs
4Predict molecular geometry
14 / 27

Worked Example: VSEPR for Ammonia (NH₃)

  • Central atom: N (5 valence e-). H (1 valence e-). Total = 5 + 3 = 8 e-.
  • N forms 3 single bonds with H atoms (6 e- used).
  • Remaining electrons: 8 - 6 = 2 e- (1 lone pair on N).
  • Total electron pairs around N = 3 bonding + 1 lone pair = 4 electron pairs.
  • Electron geometry: Tetrahedral. Molecular geometry: Trigonal Pyramidal (due to lone pair).

🎙️ For ammonia, the nitrogen atom has three bonding pairs and one lone pair. These four electron pairs arrange tetrahedrally. However, because one position is occupied by a lone pair, the molecular shape, which describes the arrangement of atoms, becomes trigonal pyramidal. This is a common point of confusion, so be careful to distinguish between electron geometry and molecular geometry.

Valence Bond Theory (VBT): Orbital Overlap

  • Explains bond formation as overlap of atomic orbitals.
  • Greater overlap leads to stronger bonds.
  • Electrons in overlapping orbitals must have opposite spins.
  • Explains directionality of bonds and molecular shapes.

🎙️ Valence Bond Theory provides a quantum mechanical explanation for covalent bonding. It postulates that a covalent bond forms when atomic orbitals of two atoms overlap, allowing electrons with opposite spins to be shared in the overlapping region. The extent of this overlap directly correlates with bond strength.

Atomic orbitals have characteristic shapes based on their angular momentum quantum number.

Types of Covalent Bonds: Sigma (σ) and Pi (π)

  • Sigma (σ) bond: Formed by head-on (axial) overlap of orbitals.
  • Pi (π) bond: Formed by sideways (lateral) overlap of unhybridized p-orbitals.
  • Single bonds are always sigma bonds.
  • Double bonds have one σ and one π bond; Triple bonds have one σ and two π bonds.

🎙️ Sigma bonds are the strongest type of covalent bond due to their direct, head-on overlap. Pi bonds are weaker and are always formed in conjunction with a sigma bond. Understanding the distinction between sigma and pi bonds is crucial for describing multiple bonds and molecular structure.

Hybridization: Mixing Atomic Orbitals

  • Process of intermixing of atomic orbitals of slightly different energies.
  • Forms new set of equivalent hybrid orbitals.
  • Hybrid orbitals have same energy and shape.
  • Explains observed molecular geometries that VBT alone cannot.

🎙️ Hybridization is a theoretical concept introduced to explain the observed geometries of molecules. It involves the mixing of atomic orbitals on a central atom to form new hybrid orbitals that are identical in energy and shape. These hybrid orbitals then form more effective bonds, leading to specific molecular shapes.

Orbital Blending

Types of Hybridization: sp, sp², sp³

  • sp hybridization: 1 s + 1 p orbital → 2 sp hybrid orbitals (Linear, 180°). E.g., BeCl₂.
  • sp² hybridization: 1 s + 2 p orbitals → 3 sp² hybrid orbitals (Trigonal Planar, 120°). E.g., BF₃.
  • sp³ hybridization: 1 s + 3 p orbitals → 4 sp³ hybrid orbitals (Tetrahedral, 109.5°). E.g., CH₄.
  • The number of hybrid orbitals equals the number of mixed atomic orbitals.

🎙️ These are the most common types of hybridization. Notice how the number of hybrid orbitals corresponds to the number of regions of electron density around the central atom, and each type dictates a specific ideal geometry. Remember that lone pairs also occupy hybrid orbitals.

1sp: Linear
2sp²: Trigonal Planar
3sp³: Tetrahedral
20 / 27

Types of Hybridization: sp³d, sp³d²

  • sp³d hybridization: 1 s + 3 p + 1 d → 5 sp³d hybrid orbitals (Trigonal Bipyramidal). E.g., PCl₅.
  • sp³d² hybridization: 1 s + 3 p + 2 d → 6 sp³d² hybrid orbitals (Octahedral). E.g., SF₆.
  • Involves the participation of d-orbitals from elements in Period 3 and beyond.
  • These expanded octets are explained by d-orbital involvement.

🎙️ For elements in the third period and beyond, d-orbitals can participate in hybridization, leading to expanded octets and more complex geometries. sp³d hybridization leads to a trigonal bipyramidal arrangement, while sp³d² hybridization results in an octahedral arrangement. These are important for understanding molecules like PCl₅ and SF₆.

Worked Example: Hybridization in SF₆

  • Central atom: S (Group 16, 6 valence e-). F (Group 17, 7 valence e-).
  • S needs to form 6 bonds with F atoms.
  • Ground state S: [Ne] 3s² 3p⁴.
  • Excited state S: One 3s e- and two 3p e- promoted to 3d orbitals.
  • Hybridization: 1s + 3p + 2d orbitals intermix to form 6 sp³d² hybrid orbitals.
  • Geometry: Octahedral.

🎙️ In SF₆, sulfur needs to form six bonds. This requires six unpaired electrons, which is achieved by promoting electrons to the empty 3d orbitals. The subsequent mixing of one s, three p, and two d orbitals yields six equivalent sp³d² hybrid orbitals, resulting in an octahedral geometry. This is a classic example of expanded octet and d-orbital hybridization.

Molecular Orbital Theory (MOT): A Deeper Look

  • Atomic orbitals combine to form molecular orbitals (MOs).
  • Electrons occupy MOs as per Aufbau, Pauli, and Hund's rules.
  • Explains magnetic properties and bond order more accurately.
  • LCAO (Linear Combination of Atomic Orbitals) method.

🎙️ Molecular Orbital Theory is a more advanced approach to chemical bonding. Instead of localized bonds, it proposes that atomic orbitals combine to form new, delocalized molecular orbitals that span the entire molecule. Electrons then fill these MOs, providing a more accurate explanation for phenomena like paramagnetism and bond order.

MOT: Bonding and Anti-bonding Orbitals

  • Constructive interference of AOs forms Bonding MOs (lower energy, stable).
  • Destructive interference of AOs forms Anti-bonding MOs (higher energy, unstable).
  • Electrons in bonding MOs stabilize the molecule.
  • Electrons in anti-bonding MOs destabilize the molecule.

🎙️ When atomic orbitals combine, they form two types of molecular orbitals: bonding MOs, which are lower in energy and stabilize the molecule, and anti-bonding MOs, which are higher in energy and destabilize it. The filling of these orbitals dictates the stability and properties of the molecule.

MOT: Bond Order & Magnetic Properties

  • Bond Order = ½ (Number of electrons in Bonding MOs - Number of electrons in Anti-bonding MOs).
  • Higher bond order indicates greater bond strength and stability.
  • Paramagnetic: Contains unpaired electrons (attracted to magnetic field).
  • Diamagnetic: All electrons are paired (repelled by magnetic field).

🎙️ MOT allows us to calculate bond order, which is a direct measure of bond strength. It also accurately predicts magnetic properties. If a molecule has unpaired electrons in its molecular orbitals, it is paramagnetic; if all electrons are paired, it is diamagnetic. This explains why O₂ is paramagnetic, which VBT cannot.

Hydrogen Bonding: An Intermolecular Force

  • Special type of dipole-dipole interaction.
  • Occurs when H is bonded to highly electronegative atoms (F, O, N).
  • Hydrogen atom acts as a bridge between two electronegative atoms.
  • Responsible for high boiling points of H₂O, NH₃, HF.

🎙️ Hydrogen bonding is a particularly strong type of intermolecular force, not a true chemical bond within a molecule. It plays a critical role in the properties of many substances, especially water, influencing its high boiling point, surface tension, and solvent properties. It's an important concept for understanding biological molecules too.

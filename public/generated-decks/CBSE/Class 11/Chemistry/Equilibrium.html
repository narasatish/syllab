What is Chemical Equilibrium?

  • A state where opposing processes occur at equal rates.
  • No net change in observable properties.
  • Dynamic, not static, process.

🎙️ Imagine a tug-of-war where both teams are pulling with equal force – the rope doesn't move, but the teams are still actively pulling. That's equilibrium! It's a state of balance where forward and reverse reactions happen at the same speed, leading to no visible change.

Reversible vs. Irreversible Reactions

  • Reversible: Products can reform reactants.
  • Irreversible: Products cannot revert to reactants.
  • Equilibrium only achieved in reversible reactions.

🎙️ This distinction is vital. Reversible reactions are like a two-way street, allowing movement in both directions. Irreversible reactions are one-way. Equilibrium is exclusively observed in reversible processes because it requires both forward and reverse reactions to occur.

Key Characteristics of Equilibrium

  • Closed system required.
  • Constant macroscopic properties.
  • Dynamic nature at microscopic level.
  • Can be approached from either direction.

🎙️ For equilibrium to be established, the system must be closed, preventing matter exchange. While macroscopic properties like concentration and temperature appear constant, reactions are still occurring actively at the molecular level – hence, 'dynamic'. You can reach equilibrium whether you start with reactants or products.

Solid-Liquid Equilibrium (Melting/Freezing)

  • At melting point: rate of melting = rate of freezing.
  • Example: Ice ⇌ Water at 0°C.
  • Concentration of solid and liquid remains constant.

🎙️ Consider ice and water at 0°C and 1 atm pressure. Ice melts into water, and water freezes into ice simultaneously. At equilibrium, the amount of ice and water remains constant, although molecules are continuously interconverting between states.

Liquid-Gas Equilibrium (Evaporation/Condensation)

  • At boiling point: rate of evaporation = rate of condensation.
  • Example: Water ⇌ Water Vapour at 100°C.
  • Vapour pressure becomes constant.

🎙️ In a closed container, water evaporates, and its vapour condenses back into liquid. When the rate of evaporation equals the rate of condensation, a constant vapour pressure is established, signifying liquid-gas equilibrium. This is crucial for understanding boiling points.

Solid-Gas Equilibrium (Sublimation)

  • Rate of sublimation = rate of deposition.
  • Example: Iodine (s) ⇌ Iodine (g).
  • Common for substances like dry ice, camphor.

🎙️ Some solids, like iodine or dry ice, can directly convert into gas without passing through a liquid phase, a process called sublimation. In a closed container, the gas can also deposit back into solid. Equilibrium is reached when these rates are equal.

The Law of Mass Action

  • Proposed by Guldberg and Waage.
  • Rate of reaction is proportional to product of molar concentrations.
  • Each concentration term raised to its stoichiometric coefficient.

🎙️ This fundamental law helps us quantify reaction rates. It states that the speed of a chemical reaction is directly related to the concentrations of the reacting species. This principle is key to deriving the equilibrium constant expression.

Defining the Equilibrium Constant (Kc)

  • Ratio of product concentrations to reactant concentrations.
  • Each raised to their stoichiometric coefficients.
  • Constant at a given temperature.

🎙️ Kc is a powerful tool. For a general reaction aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD, Kc = [C]^c [D]^d / [A]^a [B]^b. Remember, it only includes species whose concentrations can change, usually gases and aqueous solutions. Solids and pure liquids are omitted as their 'active mass' is considered constant.

Equilibrium Constant in Terms of Pressure (Kp)

  • Used for reactions involving gases.
  • Partial pressures replace molar concentrations.
  • Kp = (P_C)^c (P_D)^d / (P_A)^a (P_B)^b.

🎙️ For reactions entirely in the gaseous phase, it's often more convenient to express equilibrium in terms of partial pressures. Kp follows the same form as Kc, but uses partial pressures instead of molar concentrations. Understanding this is crucial for gas-phase equilibria.

Relationship between Kp and Kc

  • Kp = Kc (RT)^Δn_g.
  • Δn_g = (moles of gaseous products) - (moles of gaseous reactants).
  • R is gas constant, T is absolute temperature.

🎙️ This formula allows you to convert between Kp and Kc. Make sure to correctly calculate Δn_g, considering only gaseous species. A common mistake is including solids or liquids in the Δn_g calculation. Remember R must be in appropriate units, usually 0.0821 L atm mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ or 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.

Worked Example: Calculating Kc

  • Problem: N₂ (g) + 3H₂ (g) ⇌ 2NH₃ (g). At equilibrium, [N₂]=0.1M, [H₂]=0.3M, [NH₃]=0.2M. Calculate Kc.
  • Solution: Kc = [NH₃]² / ([N₂][H₂]³)
  • Kc = (0.2)² / (0.1)(0.3)³ = 0.04 / (0.1 * 0.027) = 0.04 / 0.0027 ≈ 14.81.

🎙️ Always start by writing the correct Kc expression. Then, substitute the equilibrium concentrations carefully. Pay close attention to the stoichiometry as exponents. Practice these calculations to avoid common arithmetic errors.

16 / 25

Worked Example: Calculating Kp

  • Problem: For PCl₅ (g) ⇌ PCl₃ (g) + Cl₂ (g), at 250°C, partial pressures are P(PCl₅)=0.2 atm, P(PCl₃)=1.0 atm, P(Cl₂)=1.0 atm. Calculate Kp.
  • Solution: Kp = (P_PCl₃)(P_Cl₂) / P_PCl₅
  • Kp = (1.0)(1.0) / 0.2 = 1 / 0.2 = 5.0.

🎙️ Similar to Kc, write the Kp expression correctly using partial pressures. Substitute the given values. Remember that Kp, like Kc, is dimensionless if you use the standard state convention, but often units are reported based on the pressure units used (e.g., atm).

Le Chatelier's Principle: Introduction

  • Predicts the direction a system shifts to re-establish equilibrium.
  • When a stress is applied, the system adjusts to relieve that stress.
  • Key factors: Concentration, Pressure, Temperature.

🎙️ Le Chatelier's Principle is incredibly powerful for predicting how equilibrium systems respond to changes. It's like the system tries to 'fight back' against any disturbance. Understanding this principle is crucial for optimizing industrial processes.

Effect of Concentration Change

  • Adding reactant: Equilibrium shifts forward (products increase).
  • Removing reactant: Equilibrium shifts backward (reactants increase).
  • Adding product: Equilibrium shifts backward (reactants increase).

🎙️ Think of it as the system trying to consume what's added or replace what's removed. If you add more reactants, the system will try to use them up by making more products. If you remove products, the system will make more to compensate.

Effect of Pressure Change

  • Only affects reactions involving gases.
  • Increase pressure: Shifts to side with fewer moles of gas.
  • Decrease pressure: Shifts to side with more moles of gas.

🎙️ Pressure changes mainly impact gaseous systems. The system tries to reduce the stress. If pressure increases, it moves to the side with fewer gas molecules to lessen the collisions. If pressure decreases, it moves to the side with more gas molecules to increase collisions.

Effect of Temperature Change

  • Increase T: Shifts in endothermic direction (absorbs heat).
  • Decrease T: Shifts in exothermic direction (releases heat).
  • Temperature also changes the value of K.

🎙️ Temperature is unique because it's the only factor that changes the value of the equilibrium constant (K). Treat heat as a reactant in endothermic reactions and a product in exothermic reactions. Increasing temperature favors the heat-absorbing (endothermic) direction.

Effect of Catalyst on Equilibrium

  • Increases rates of both forward and reverse reactions equally.
  • Does NOT change the position of equilibrium.
  • Helps equilibrium be attained faster.

🎙️ A catalyst is like a shortcut. It speeds up the journey to equilibrium but doesn't change the destination. It lowers the activation energy for both forward and reverse reactions identically, meaning the final concentrations of reactants and products remain the same.

Real-World Applications of Equilibrium

  • Haber-Bosch Process: N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g) (Ammonia synthesis).
  • Contact Process: 2SO₂(g) + O₂(g) ⇌ 2SO₃(g) (Sulfuric acid production).
  • Biological systems: pH regulation in blood.

🎙️ Equilibrium principles are not just theoretical; they are vital in industry. Processes like Haber-Bosch and Contact are optimized using Le Chatelier's principle to maximize product yield. Even our own bodies rely on equilibrium for maintaining critical balances.

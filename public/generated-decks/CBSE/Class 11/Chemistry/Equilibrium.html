What is Chemical Equilibrium?

  • A state where opposing processes occur at equal rates.
  • No net change in macroscopic properties.
  • A dynamic state, not static.

🎙️ Imagine a tug-of-war where both teams are pulling with equal force; the rope doesn't move, but the effort is still there. That's equilibrium – a balance between opposing forces or reactions.

Physical vs. Chemical Processes

Dynamic Nature of Chemical Equilibrium

  • Forward reaction rate = Reverse reaction rate.
  • Reactions don't stop, they balance out.
  • Constant concentrations of reactants and products.

🎙️ This is a crucial point: chemical equilibrium is not a standstill. Imagine two runners running towards each other and then turning back; at equilibrium, they might cross the midpoint at the same rate, maintaining a constant 'population' on each side.

Macroscopic Properties at Equilibrium

  • Concentration of reactants and products become constant.
  • Temperature and pressure remain constant.
  • Observable properties like color, density, pH don't change.

🎙️ While microscopic changes continue, from an observer's perspective, everything appears stable. The system has reached a steady state where all measurable properties are unchanging.

The Law of Mass Action

  • Proposed by Guldberg and Waage.
  • Rate of a reaction is proportional to the product of molar concentrations of reactants.
  • Each concentration term raised to its stoichiometric coefficient.

🎙️ This law is fundamental to understanding equilibrium. It quantitatively links the rate of a reaction to the concentrations of the reacting species, paving the way for the equilibrium constant.

Reaction Rates

The Equilibrium Constant (Kc)

  • Ratio of products to reactants at equilibrium.
  • Expressed in terms of molar concentrations.
  • Kc = [C]^c [D]^d / [A]^a [B]^b for aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD.

🎙️ Kc is a powerful tool. It tells us the extent to which a reaction proceeds towards products at a given temperature. A large Kc means more products at equilibrium, a small Kc means more reactants.

🧮
Calculation

Units of Equilibrium Constant (Kc)

  • Units depend on the stoichiometry of the reaction.
  • Calculated as (mol L⁻¹)^(Δn).
  • Δn = (sum of product coeffs) - (sum of reactant coeffs).

🎙️ Unlike many constants, Kc often has units. It's important to derive them correctly based on the net change in moles of gaseous or aqueous species in the balanced equation.

Equilibrium Constant for Gaseous Reactions (Kp)

  • Used when reactants and products are gases.
  • Expressed in terms of partial pressures.
  • Kp = (P_C)^c (P_D)^d / (P_A)^a (P_B)^b.

🎙️ For reactions involving gases, partial pressures are often more convenient than molar concentrations. Kp serves the same purpose as Kc but uses partial pressures instead.

Relationship between Kp and Kc

  • Kp = Kc (RT)^Δn.
  • R is the gas constant, T is absolute temperature.
  • Δn = (moles of gaseous products) - (moles of gaseous reactants).

🎙️ This equation is vital for interconverting between Kp and Kc. Remember to use the correct value of R (0.0821 L atm mol⁻¹ K⁻¹) and ensure temperature is in Kelvin.

Worked Example: Calculating Kc

  • Problem: N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g). At equilibrium, [N₂]=0.5M, [H₂]=1.5M, [NH₃]=0.2M. Calculate Kc.
  • Solution: Kc = [NH₃]² / ([N₂][H₂]³).
  • Kc = (0.2)² / (0.5 * 1.5³) = 0.04 / (0.5 * 3.375) = 0.04 / 1.6875 ≈ 0.0237 M⁻².

🎙️ Always start by writing the correct equilibrium expression. Then, substitute the equilibrium concentrations and perform the calculation carefully. Pay attention to units!

Factors Affecting Equilibrium: Le Chatelier's Principle

  • System at equilibrium resists changes.
  • Shift to counteract the imposed change.
  • Concentration, Pressure, Temperature are key factors.

🎙️ Le Chatelier's Principle is one of the most important concepts in equilibrium. It helps us predict how an equilibrium system will respond to a disturbance, much like a seesaw rebalancing itself.

Effect of Concentration Change

  • Adding reactant: Equilibrium shifts to product side.
  • Adding product: Equilibrium shifts to reactant side.
  • Removing reactant/product: Shifts to replenish it.

🎙️ If you add more of a reactant, the system tries to consume it by making more products. Conversely, removing a product makes the system produce more of it to compensate.

Effect of Pressure Change

  • Increasing pressure: Shifts to side with fewer gas moles.
  • Decreasing pressure: Shifts to side with more gas moles.
  • Only affects reactions with gaseous components and Δn ≠ 0.

🎙️ Pressure changes directly relate to volume changes. The system tries to reduce or increase the total number of gas molecules to relieve the stress, favouring the side with fewer or more moles, respectively.

⚙️
Volume Adjustment

Effect of Temperature Change

  • Increasing temperature: Shifts in endothermic direction.
  • Decreasing temperature: Shifts in exothermic direction.
  • Temperature is the only factor that changes the value of K.

🎙️ Treat heat as a reactant in endothermic reactions and a product in exothermic reactions. Changing temperature will shift the equilibrium to absorb or release that heat, and uniquely, it alters the value of K.

🔥
Heat Exchange

Effect of Catalyst

  • Catalyst speeds up both forward and reverse reactions equally.
  • Does not change the position of equilibrium.
  • Helps equilibrium to be attained faster.

🎙️ A catalyst is like a shortcut. It helps the reaction reach equilibrium more quickly, but it doesn't change where that equilibrium lies. The final ratio of products to reactants remains the same.

Worked Example: Applying Le Chatelier's Principle

  • Problem: N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g) + Heat. What happens if (a) H₂ is added, (b) pressure is increased, (c) temperature is increased?
  • (a) Shifts right (more NH₃).
  • (b) Shifts right (2 moles vs 4 moles gas).
  • (c) Shifts left (endothermic direction to absorb heat).

🎙️ For each change, identify the stress on the system. Then, determine how the system will try to relieve that stress according to Le Chatelier's Principle. This is a common exam question.

Recap: Key Takeaways on Equilibrium

Equilibrium: A Chemical Tug-of-War

  • Imagine two teams pulling a rope with equal and opposite force.
  • The rope doesn't move, but both teams are still actively pulling!
  • This balance of opposing forces is the essence of equilibrium.

🎙️ Just like in a tug-of-war where the net movement is zero but activity is ongoing, equilibrium in chemistry means forward and reverse processes occur at equal rates.

Defining Equilibrium: The State of Dynamic Balance

  • A state where the rate of the forward process equals the rate of the reverse process.
  • Macroscopic properties (concentration, pressure, temperature) remain constant.
  • It is dynamic, meaning processes continue at the molecular level.

🎙️ Crucially, equilibrium is not static. Molecules are still reacting, but for every molecule reacting in the forward direction, another reacts in the reverse, creating an apparent standstill.

Two Main Arenas: Physical vs. Chemical Equilibrium

  • Physical Equilibrium: Involves changes in physical states or dissolution.
  • Chemical Equilibrium: Involves chemical reactions where reactants form products and vice-versa.

🎙️ We'll delve into both these types. Physical equilibrium deals with phase changes like melting or boiling, while chemical equilibrium involves the formation of new substances.

Physical Equilibrium: Solid-Liquid (Melting Ice)

  • At 0°C and 1 atm, ice and water coexist.
  • Rate of melting = Rate of freezing.
  • Example: Ice (s) ⇌ Water (l)

🎙️ Imagine ice cubes in a glass of water exactly at 0°C. Some ice melts, but an equal amount of water freezes, maintaining the quantity of ice and water. This is a dynamic balance.

Physical Equilibrium: Liquid-Gas (Boiling Water)

  • In a closed container, at boiling point, rate of vaporization = rate of condensation.
  • Example: Water (l) ⇌ Water (g)
  • This leads to a constant vapor pressure.

🎙️ When water boils in a closed system, not all water turns to steam. A balance is struck where liquid water evaporates at the same rate as water vapour condenses back into liquid, creating a constant pressure.

Physical Equilibrium: Dissolution of Solids (Sugar in Tea)

  • When sugar dissolves in tea, sugar molecules move into solution.
  • At saturation, rate of dissolution = rate of crystallization.
  • Example: Sugar (s) ⇌ Sugar (aq)

🎙️ You keep adding sugar to your tea, and eventually, it stops dissolving. That's because the solution has become saturated, and a dynamic equilibrium is established between dissolved and undissolved sugar.

Physical Equilibrium: Gases in Liquids (Henry's Law)

  • The solubility of a gas in a liquid is directly proportional to its partial pressure above the liquid.
  • Example: CO₂ (g) ⇌ CO₂ (aq) in soda.
  • Higher pressure means more gas dissolves.

🎙️ This is exactly what happens in aerated drinks. Under high pressure, more CO2 dissolves. When you open the bottle, pressure drops, and CO2 escapes, causing fizz.

Chemical Equilibrium: The Reversible Path

  • Many chemical reactions are reversible, meaning products can react to reform reactants.
  • Represented by a double arrow (⇌).
  • Example: N₂ (g) + 3H₂ (g) ⇌ 2NH₃ (g) (Haber Process)

🎙️ Unlike irreversible reactions that go to completion, reversible reactions establish a balance where both forward and reverse reactions are continuously occurring at equal rates.

Hallmarks of Chemical Equilibrium

  • Occurs only in a closed system at constant temperature.
  • Concentrations of reactants and products become constant (not necessarily equal).
  • It is dynamic in nature.
  • Can be approached from either direction (reactants or products).

🎙️ These are crucial points. A closed system prevents matter exchange, and constant temperature ensures reaction rates aren't changing due to external heat, allowing equilibrium to establish stably.

Law of Mass Action & Equilibrium Constant (Kc)

  • For a reaction aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD, Kc = [C]ᶜ[D]ᵈ / [A]ᵃ[B]ᵇ.
  • Kc expresses the ratio of product concentrations to reactant concentrations at equilibrium.
  • Kc is constant at a given temperature.

🎙️ This law, proposed by Guldberg and Waage, is foundational. It quantifies the relative amounts of reactants and products at equilibrium. Remember, square brackets denote molar concentrations.

Equilibrium Constant (Kp) & Kc Relationship

  • For gaseous reactions, Kp uses partial pressures: Kp = (P_C)ᶜ(P_D)ᵈ / (P_A)ᵃ(P_B)ᵇ.
  • Relationship: Kp = Kc(RT)Δn_g.
  • Δn_g = (moles of gaseous products) - (moles of gaseous reactants).

🎙️ Kp is used when dealing with gases, as pressure is often more convenient than concentration. The relationship between Kp and Kc is vital for calculations and understanding gas-phase equilibria.

Worked Example 1: Calculating Kc

  • Problem: For H₂ (g) + I₂ (g) ⇌ 2HI (g), at 717K, equilibrium concentrations are [H₂]=0.08 M, [I₂]=0.08 M, [HI]=0.49 M.
  • Step 1: Write the Kc expression. Kc = [HI]² / ([H₂][I₂]).
  • Step 2: Substitute values. Kc = (0.49)² / (0.08 × 0.08) = 0.2401 / 0.0064.
  • Step 3: Calculate Kc. Kc = 37.51.

🎙️ Follow these steps carefully. Always ensure you use equilibrium concentrations and raise them to the power of their stoichiometric coefficients from the balanced chemical equation.

Le Chatelier's Principle: When Equilibrium is Disturbed

  • If a system at equilibrium is subjected to a change, it will readjust itself to counteract the effect of the change.
  • The system tries to minimize the disturbance.
  • It's like a seesaw trying to rebalance.

🎙️ This principle is incredibly powerful. It allows us to predict how a chemical system will respond to changes in conditions, which is vital in industrial processes to optimize product yield.

Le Chatelier's: Effect of Concentration Change

  • Adding reactant: Equilibrium shifts to the right (forward direction) to consume added reactant.
  • Removing reactant: Equilibrium shifts to the left (reverse direction) to replenish reactant.
  • Opposite for products.

🎙️ Think of it as the system trying to 'dilute' the added substance or 'make up for' the removed substance. This is a common way to drive reactions to completion in chemistry labs and industry.

Le Chatelier's: Effect of Pressure Change

  • Only affects reactions involving gases with Δn_g ≠ 0.
  • Increasing pressure: Shifts towards fewer moles of gas.
  • Decreasing pressure: Shifts towards more moles of gas.
  • No effect if Δn_g = 0.

🎙️ Pressure changes are significant for gaseous systems. The system tries to reduce or increase the number of gas molecules to counteract the applied pressure change and relieve the stress.

Le Chatelier's: Effect of Temperature Change

  • Increasing temperature: Favours the endothermic reaction (absorbs heat).
  • Decreasing temperature: Favours the exothermic reaction (releases heat).
  • Temperature changes affect the value of Kc and Kp.

🎙️ This is the only factor that changes the actual value of the equilibrium constant. Endothermic reactions shift right with heat, exothermic reactions shift left to release heat.

Worked Example 2: Applying Le Chatelier's Principle

  • Reaction: N₂ (g) + 3H₂ (g) ⇌ 2NH₃ (g) + Heat (Exothermic)
  • 1. Increase N₂ concentration: Shifts right (more NH₃).
  • 2. Decrease pressure: Shifts left (4 moles gas vs 2 moles gas).
  • 3. Increase temperature: Shifts left (favours endothermic reverse reaction).

🎙️ For the Haber process, increasing N2 pushes it to products. Decreasing pressure means the system tries to make more gas, so it goes left. Increasing temperature works against the exothermic forward reaction.

Ionic Equilibrium: Electrolytes & Non-electrolytes

  • Electrolytes: Substances that conduct electricity in molten state or aqueous solution (due to ions).
  • Non-electrolytes: Substances that do not conduct electricity (e.g., glucose, urea).
  • Strong electrolytes dissociate completely; Weak electrolytes dissociate partially.

🎙️ Ionic equilibrium focuses on the equilibrium involving ions in solution. The ability of a substance to conduct electricity is directly linked to the presence of free ions in the solution.

Acids & Bases: Different Perspectives

  • Arrhenius: Acids produce H⁺, Bases produce OH⁻ in water.
  • Brønsted-Lowry: Acids are proton (H⁺) donors, Bases are proton acceptors.
  • Lewis: Acids are electron pair acceptors, Bases are electron pair donors.

🎙️ Each definition expands our understanding of acids and bases. Brønsted-Lowry is most commonly used in ionic equilibrium, introducing conjugate acid-base pairs.

pH scale (0-14) measures acidity and alkalinity. Acids < 7 (H+ ions), bases > 7 (OH− ions)

Ionization Constants (Ka, Kb) and the pH Scale

  • Ka: Acid dissociation constant. Kb: Base dissociation constant.
  • Kw = Ka × Kb (for conjugate pairs) = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ at 25°C.
  • pH = -log[H⁺]. pOH = -log[OH⁻]. pH + pOH = 14.

🎙️ Ka and Kb quantify the strength of weak acids and bases. The pH scale provides a convenient way to express the acidity or basicity of a solution, from 0 (very acidic) to 14 (very basic).

Buffer Solutions: Resisting pH Changes

  • Solutions that resist changes in pH upon addition of small amounts of acid or base.
  • Composed of a weak acid and its conjugate base, or a weak base and its conjugate acid.
  • Crucial in biological systems (e.g., blood) and chemical processes.

🎙️ Buffers are like a pH shield. They contain components that can neutralize both added H+ and OH- ions, maintaining a stable pH, which is essential for many reactions.

Solubility Product (Ksp): Sparingly Soluble Salts

  • For sparingly soluble salt like AgCl (s) ⇌ Ag⁺ (aq) + Cl⁻ (aq), Ksp = [Ag⁺][Cl⁻].
  • Ksp is the product of the concentrations of its ions in a saturated solution.
  • Used to predict precipitation and dissolution.

🎙️ Ksp helps us understand when a precipitate will form or dissolve. It's an equilibrium constant for the dissolution of ionic solids, indicating their extent of solubility.

Common Mistakes & Exam Tips for Equilibrium

  • Mistakes: Not distinguishing Kc vs Kp, incorrect Δn_g, misapplying Le Chatelier's (e.g., catalyst effect on equilibrium).
  • Tips: Practice derivations of Kc/Kp, work numerical problems step-by-step, understand conceptual questions on Le Chatelier's.
  • Pay attention to units and significant figures.

🎙️ Many students confuse catalysts with affecting equilibrium position. Remember, catalysts only speed up reaching equilibrium, not change its position. Practice is key to mastering these concepts!

Early Attempts: Dobereiner's Triads (1829)

  • First significant attempt to classify elements.
  • Elements grouped in 'triads' (groups of three).
  • Based on similar chemical properties.

🎙️ Think of it like forming small groups of three friends who share common interests. Dobereiner noticed that certain sets of three elements had very similar characteristics, prompting him to group them.

Group of Three

The Triad Rule

  • In a triad, the atomic mass of the middle element was approximately the average of the other two.
  • All three elements in a triad showed similar chemical properties.

🎙️ For example, in the Lithium, Sodium, Potassium triad, the atomic mass of Sodium (23) is roughly the average of Lithium (7) and Potassium (39). This pattern was exciting but limited.

1Li (7), Na (23), K (39)
2Ca (40), Sr (88), Ba (137)
3Cl (35.5), Br (80), I (127)

Limitations of Dobereiner's Triads

  • Could identify only a few triads.
  • Many known elements could not be grouped into triads.
  • Not universally applicable to all elements.

🎙️ While a clever start, Dobereiner's Triads were more of a curiosity than a comprehensive classification system. It couldn't account for the vast majority of elements discovered at the time, indicating its incompleteness.

Newlands' Law of Octaves (1865)

  • Arranged elements in increasing order of atomic masses.
  • Noticed a repetition of properties every eighth element.
  • Compared it to musical octaves (Sa, Re, Ga, Ma, Pa, Dha, Ni, Sa).

🎙️ Imagine a musical scale where the eighth note repeats the first. Newlands observed a similar pattern in elements, suggesting a 'Law of Octaves' where properties recurred after every seven elements.

🎶
Musical Harmony
8 / 30

Newlands' Octaves - Concept

  • The properties of the eighth element were similar to the first.
  • For example, Li and Na (8th from Li) had similar properties.

🎙️ This was a significant step as it highlighted a periodic relationship. He placed elements like Lithium, Beryllium, Boron, Carbon, Nitrogen, Oxygen, Fluorine, and then Sodium, where Sodium showed properties similar to Lithium.

Limitations of Newlands' Octaves

  • Applicable only up to Calcium (lighter elements).
  • Did not account for new elements or noble gases.
  • Forced elements with dissimilar properties into the same group.

🎙️ Newlands' law quickly broke down for heavier elements. When noble gases were discovered, they didn't fit into his scheme, and he sometimes placed elements like Cobalt and Nickel with Halogens, which have very different properties.

Mendeleev: The Game Changer (1869)

  • Dmitri Mendeleev published his Periodic Table.
  • Considered the 'Father of the Periodic Table'.
  • A more comprehensive and predictive classification.

🎙️ Mendeleev's approach was revolutionary. He not only classified existing elements but also left gaps for undiscovered ones, predicting their properties with remarkable accuracy, much like knowing where to place a missing brick in a house.

Mendeleev's Periodic Law

  • The properties of elements are a periodic function of their atomic masses.
  • Arranged elements in increasing order of atomic masses.
  • Similar properties appeared at regular intervals.

🎙️ This law formed the cornerstone of his periodic table. He carefully observed chemical properties, especially reactions with oxygen and hydrogen, to place elements in groups and periods, noting the recurring patterns based on their masses.

⚖️
Atomic Mass Basis
12 / 30

Achievements of Mendeleev's PT

  • Prediction of undiscovered elements (e.g., Eka-Aluminium, Eka-Silicon).
  • Correction of atomic masses of some elements (e.g., Beryllium, Indium).
  • Accommodation of noble gases without disturbing the existing table.

🎙️ Mendeleev's greatest triumph was his bold prediction of elements like Scandium, Gallium, and Germanium, which were later discovered and matched his predictions perfectly. This validated his entire system and earned him widespread acclaim.

🏆
Predictive Power
13 / 30

Limitations of Mendeleev's PT

  • Position of Hydrogen was uncertain.
  • Anomalous pairs (e.g., Ar and K, Co and Ni).
  • Isotopes posed a challenge (same element, different atomic masses).
  • No clear explanation for the cause of periodicity.

🎙️ Despite its successes, Mendeleev's table had its share of problems. The placement of hydrogen, the existence of isotopes, and certain inversions in atomic mass order were issues that needed resolution for a truly perfect system.

The Modern Periodic Law (Henry Moseley, 1913)

  • Henry Moseley studied X-ray spectra of elements.
  • Found a more fundamental property: Atomic Number (Z).
  • Each element has a unique 'ID card' – its atomic number.

🎙️ Moseley's work was groundbreaking. He discovered that the square root of the frequency of X-rays emitted by an element was proportional to its atomic number, not its atomic mass. This provided the true basis for periodicity.

🆔
Unique Identity

Basis: Atomic Number

  • The properties of elements are a periodic function of their atomic numbers.
  • Atomic number (number of protons) defines an element.
  • Resolves limitations of Mendeleev's table.

🎙️ This modern law perfectly explains why periodicity occurs. It correctly places elements like Argon and Potassium, and Cobalt and Nickel, based on their atomic numbers, not masses, thus solving Mendeleev's anomalous pairs.

⚛️

Structure of the Modern Periodic Table

  • Elements arranged in 7 horizontal rows called Periods.
  • Elements arranged in 18 vertical columns called Groups.
  • Elements in the same group have similar outermost electronic configurations.

🎙️ The modern periodic table is a highly organized grid. Periods represent the number of electron shells, while groups represent elements with similar valence electron configurations, leading to similar chemical properties.

Nomenclature of Elements with Z > 100

  • IUPAC (International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry) system.
  • Systematic naming for elements with atomic number greater than 100.
  • Uses specific prefixes for digits 0-9.

🎙️ Since elements with high atomic numbers are often synthesized in labs and their discovery might be disputed, IUPAC established a temporary naming system. This ensures a consistent way to refer to them until a permanent name is agreed upon.

10: nil
21: un
32: bi

Worked Example: Naming Z = 104

  • Digit 1: 'un'
  • Digit 0: 'nil'
  • Digit 4: 'quad'
  • Combined: Unnilquadium (Symbol: Unq)

🎙️ Let's apply the rules. For Z=104, we combine 'un' for 1, 'nil' for 0, and 'quad' for 4, adding the suffix '-ium' to form 'Unnilquadium'. This systematic approach prevents confusion in the scientific community.

Blocks of the Periodic Table

  • Based on the orbital where the last electron enters.
  • Divides the table into s, p, d, and f blocks.
  • Each block has characteristic properties.

🎙️ Think of the periodic table as a city, and the blocks as different neighborhoods, each with its own unique characteristics. The last electron's orbital determines which 'neighborhood' an element belongs to.

The s-Block: Group 1 & 2

  • Last electron enters the s-orbital.
  • Comprises Alkali Metals (Group 1) and Alkaline Earth Metals (Group 2).
  • Highly reactive metals, low ionization enthalpy.

🎙️ These are the most electropositive elements. Group 1 elements have ns¹ configuration, and Group 2 elements have ns² configuration. Their readiness to lose electrons makes them very reactive.

The p-Block: Group 13-18

  • Last electron enters the p-orbital.
  • Extremely diverse: includes metals, non-metals, and metalloids.
  • Contains halogens (Group 17) and noble gases (Group 18).

🎙️ The p-block is a melting pot of elements with varying properties, from highly reactive non-metals to unreactive noble gases. Their valence shell configuration ranges from ns²np¹ to ns²np⁶.

Diverse Properties

The d-Block: Transition Metals

  • Last electron enters the d-orbital (n-1)d.
  • Located between s and p blocks (Groups 3-12).
  • Exhibit variable valency, form coloured compounds, good catalysts.

🎙️ These elements are often called transition metals because their properties are intermediate between highly reactive s-block elements and less reactive p-block elements. Their partially filled d-orbitals lead to their unique characteristics.

Versatile Metals

The f-Block: Inner Transition Elements

  • Last electron enters the f-orbital (n-2)f.
  • Comprises Lanthanoids (4f series) and Actinoids (5f series).
  • Located separately at the bottom of the periodic table, many are radioactive.

🎙️ These elements are also known as inner transition elements. Lanthanoids are generally stable, while actinoids are mostly radioactive and synthetic. They are crucial in various advanced technologies, including nuclear applications.

  • Explained by the recurrence of similar electronic configurations.

🎙️ Just like seasons repeat in a cycle, many properties of elements show a recurring pattern as we move across periods and down groups. This periodicity is directly linked to the repeating valence electron configurations.

Atomic Radius

  • Distance from the center of the nucleus to the outermost electron shell.
  • Cannot be measured precisely due to electron cloud nature.
  • Defined as Covalent, Metallic, or van der Waals radius.

🎙️ Atomic radius is a measure of the size of an atom. Since the electron cloud doesn't have a sharp boundary, we define it based on how atoms bond or interact. Covalent radius is half the distance between two bonded identical atoms.

📏
Measuring Size
28 / 30

Atomic Radius Trends

  • Across a Period (left to right): Atomic radius generally decreases.
  • Down a Group (top to bottom): Atomic radius generally increases.

🎙️ Across a period, effective nuclear charge increases, pulling electrons closer to the nucleus. Down a group, new electron shells are added, increasing the distance of the outermost electrons from the nucleus, despite increased nuclear charge.

Ionic Radius

  • The effective distance from the nucleus of an ion to its outermost shell.
  • Cation (positive ion) is smaller than its parent atom.
  • Anion (negative ion) is larger than its parent atom.

🎙️ When an atom loses electrons to form a cation, it loses its outermost shell and the remaining electrons are pulled more strongly. When an atom gains electrons to form an anion, electron-electron repulsion increases, expanding the electron cloud.

➕
Cation (smaller)
30 / 30

Isoelectronic Species

  • Atoms or ions having the same number of electrons.
  • Their size depends on the nuclear charge (number of protons).
  • Higher nuclear charge means smaller ionic radius for isoelectronic species.

🎙️ For example, O²⁻, F⁻, Na⁺, Mg²⁺ all have 10 electrons. However, Mg²⁺ (12 protons) is smaller than Na⁺ (11 protons), which is smaller than F⁻ (9 protons), and so on. The increasing nuclear pull shrinks the electron cloud.

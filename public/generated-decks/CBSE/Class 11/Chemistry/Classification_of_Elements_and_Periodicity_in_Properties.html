Why Do We Classify Elements?

  • Over 118 known elements
  • Difficult to study individually
  • Systematize properties and predict behavior

🎙️ Imagine trying to remember the properties of 118 unique individuals! It's overwhelming. Classification helps us group elements with similar characteristics, making their study much more manageable and allowing us to predict their reactions.

Early Attempts: Dobereiner's Triads (1829)

  • Grouped elements in threes (triads)
  • Middle element's atomic mass ≈ average of other two
  • Example: Li (7), Na (23), K (39) -> (7+39)/2 = 23

🎙️ Dobereiner made one of the first attempts to classify elements, observing a pattern in atomic masses within groups of three. This was a significant step, showing that there were underlying relationships between elements.

Limitations of Dobereiner's Triads

  • Could identify only a few triads
  • Did not apply to all known elements
  • Failed to classify all elements

🎙️ While insightful, Dobereiner's system was not comprehensive. It could only account for a small fraction of the known elements, which meant a more universal classification method was still needed.

Newlands' Law of Octaves (1865)

  • Arranged elements by increasing atomic mass
  • Every eighth element had similar properties
  • Like musical octaves (Sa, Re, Ga...)

🎙️ Newlands introduced the idea of periodicity, noting a repetition of properties every eighth element, similar to musical notes. This was a crucial observation, linking element properties to a numerical sequence.

🎶
Musical Pattern
6 / 25

Limitations of Newlands' Law of Octaves

  • Only applicable up to Calcium
  • Failed for heavier elements
  • Placed dissimilar elements together (e.g., Co, Ni with F, Cl, Br)

🎙️ Unfortunately, Newlands' law broke down for heavier elements and sometimes forced dissimilar elements into the same group. This showed that atomic mass alone wasn't a perfect basis for classification.

🚧
Roadblock

Mendeleev's Periodic Law (1869)

  • Properties of elements are a periodic function of their atomic masses
  • Arranged elements in horizontal rows (periods) and vertical columns (groups)
  • Considered the true pioneer of the Periodic Table

🎙️ Mendeleev's work was a groundbreaking achievement. He not only arranged existing elements but also boldly predicted the existence and properties of undiscovered ones, giving his table immense predictive power.

📜
The Law

Features of Mendeleev's Periodic Table

  • Elements arranged in increasing atomic mass
  • Vertical columns are Groups (similar properties)
  • Horizontal rows are Periods

🎙️ Mendeleev's table had a clear structure with groups and periods, which helped organize the known elements systematically. This arrangement reflected the periodicity of their chemical properties.

📊
Organized Grid

Merits of Mendeleev's Periodic Table

  • Predicted existence and properties of undiscovered elements (e.g., Eka-Aluminium/Gallium)
  • Left gaps for these elements
  • Corrected atomic masses of some elements (e.g., Be)

🎙️ The greatest strength of Mendeleev's table was its predictive ability. The discovery of elements like gallium and germanium, with properties matching his predictions, solidified his work's importance.

🔮🔮🔮
Predictions Came True
10 / 25

Demerits of Mendeleev's Periodic Table

  • Position of isotopes was not clear
  • Anomalous pairs (e.g., Ar before K, Co before Ni)
  • Position of Hydrogen was not fixed

🎙️ Despite its successes, Mendeleev's table had some inconsistencies. The concept of isotopes was unknown then, and the strict adherence to atomic mass led to some elements being placed out of order based on their properties.

⚠️
Unresolved Issues
11 / 25

Modern Periodic Law (Moseley, 1913)

  • Properties of elements are a periodic function of their atomic numbers
  • Atomic number (Z) is a more fundamental property than atomic mass
  • Basis for the Modern Periodic Table

🎙️ Moseley's experiments with X-ray spectra revealed that atomic number, not atomic mass, was the true fundamental property determining an element's characteristics. This led to the Modern Periodic Law, resolving many of Mendeleev's anomalies.

⚛️
Atomic Number Rule

Features of the Modern Periodic Table

  • 18 Vertical columns (Groups)
  • 7 Horizontal rows (Periods)
  • Elements arranged by increasing atomic number

🎙️ The Modern Periodic Table, based on atomic number, is the one we use today. It systematically arranges elements into 18 groups and 7 periods, providing a clear map of chemical behavior.

Classification of Elements into s, p, d, f Blocks

  • Based on the orbital being filled by the last electron
  • s-block: last electron in s-orbital
  • p-block: last electron in p-orbital
  • d-block: last electron in d-orbital
  • f-block: last electron in f-orbital

🎙️ The modern table is further divided into blocks based on the type of atomic orbital that receives the last electron during an atom's Aufbau process. This electronic configuration is key to understanding their chemical properties.

s-Block Elements (Groups 1 & 2)

  • Alkali metals (Group 1) and Alkaline earth metals (Group 2)
  • Highly reactive metals
  • Form ionic compounds

🎙️ These are highly electropositive metals, eager to lose their outermost s-electrons to form stable cations. Their reactivity increases down the group, and they primarily form ionic bonds.

Reactive Metals

p-Block Elements (Groups 13-18)

  • Includes metals, non-metals, and metalloids
  • Exhibits a wide range of properties
  • Group 18 are Noble Gases (inert)

🎙️ The p-block is a very diverse group, showcasing a gradual transition from metallic to non-metallic character. It includes essential elements like carbon, nitrogen, oxygen, and the unreactive noble gases.

🌈
Diverse Properties

d-Block Elements (Groups 3-12)

  • Known as Transition Elements
  • Exhibit variable oxidation states
  • Form coloured compounds

🎙️ The d-block elements are the transition metals, characterized by partially filled d-orbitals. This allows them to exhibit multiple oxidation states and form vibrant, colored compounds, often acting as catalysts.

Colorful Compounds

f-Block Elements (Lanthanoids & Actinoids)

  • Inner-transition elements
  • Lanthanoids (4f series) and Actinoids (5f series)
  • Often radioactive (Actinoids)

🎙️ These are the inner-transition elements, typically placed separately at the bottom of the periodic table. The lanthanoids are known for their similar properties, while the actinoids are mostly synthetic and radioactive.

Periodicity in Properties

  • Regular gradation in properties across a period
  • Repetition of similar properties after certain regular intervals
  • Explained by recurring electronic configurations

🎙️ Periodicity refers to the predictable trends in properties of elements as we move across periods and down groups. This arises directly from the regular recurrence of similar outermost electronic configurations.

🔄
Repeating Patterns

Atomic Radius

  • Distance from the nucleus to the outermost electron shell
  • Trends: Decreases across a period (Zeff increases)
  • Increases down a group (new shells added)

🎙️ Atomic radius is a crucial property. Remember, across a period, effective nuclear charge increases, pulling electrons closer. Down a group, new electron shells are added, leading to a larger size.

Ionic Radius

  • Radius of an ion
  • Cation < Neutral Atom (loss of electrons)
  • Anion > Neutral Atom (gain of electrons)
  • Isoelectronic species: Higher Z, smaller radius

🎙️ Ionic radius differs from atomic radius due to electron loss or gain. Cations are smaller as they lose electrons, while anions are larger due to increased electron-electron repulsion. For isoelectronic species, more protons mean a stronger pull.

Ionization Enthalpy (IE)

  • Energy required to remove an electron from an isolated gaseous atom
  • Trends: Increases across a period, Decreases down a group
  • Exceptions due to stable configurations (e.g., Be > B, N > O)

🎙️ Ionization enthalpy measures how tightly an electron is held. It generally increases across a period due to stronger nuclear attraction and decreases down a group due to increased atomic size and shielding. Be mindful of exceptions like Group 2 vs 13 and Group 15 vs 16.

⚡
Energy to Remove

Electron Gain Enthalpy (EGE)

  • Energy released/absorbed when an electron is added to a gaseous atom
  • More negative across a period (easier to gain)
  • Less negative down a group
  • Halogens have very high negative EGE

🎙️ Electron gain enthalpy indicates an atom's tendency to accept an electron. A more negative value means a greater attraction for electrons. Halogens have very high negative EGEs because they are just one electron short of a stable noble gas configuration.

➕
Energy with Addition
23 / 25

Electronegativity

  • Tendency of an atom to attract shared electron pair in a molecule
  • Trends: Increases across a period, Decreases down a group
  • No units; relative scale (Pauling scale)

🎙️ Electronegativity describes an atom's 'pulling power' for electrons in a chemical bond. Fluorine is the most electronegative element. Understanding this trend is crucial for predicting bond polarity and chemical reactivity.

🧲

Metallic and Non-metallic Character

  • Metallic character decreases across a period, increases down a group
  • Non-metallic character increases across a period, decreases down a group
  • Explained by IE and EGE trends

🎙️ These trends are directly linked to ionization enthalpy and electron gain enthalpy. Elements that easily lose electrons are metallic, while those that readily gain electrons are non-metallic. This explains the diagonal relationship of metalloids.

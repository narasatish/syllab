Rest and Motion: All Relative!

  • An object is at rest if its position doesn't change with time relative to an observer.
  • An object is in motion if its position changes with time relative to an observer.
  • Rest and motion are relative terms; nothing is absolutely at rest or in motion.

🎙️ Think about it: you might be at rest relative to your chair, but you're in motion relative to the Sun! It all depends on your chosen reference point, which defines your frame of reference.

Path Length (Distance): The Total Journey

  • The total length of the actual path covered by an object.
  • A scalar quantity (only magnitude, no direction).
  • Always positive and never decreases.

🎙️ Imagine walking through a maze. The total length you walk from start to finish, even if you backtrack, is your distance. It only cares about the ground covered, not the final destination.

Displacement: The Direct Route

  • The shortest straight-line distance between the initial and final positions of an object.
  • A vector quantity (magnitude and direction).
  • Can be positive, negative, or zero.

🎙️ For Ramu, his displacement is the straight line from his home to his school, regardless of the path he actually took. It tells us 'how far' and 'in what direction' he ended up from where he started.

Speed: How Fast Are You Going?

  • The rate at which an object covers distance.
  • Scalar quantity: Speed = Distance / Time.
  • SI unit: metres per second (m/s).

🎙️ Your car's speedometer measures speed. It tells you how quickly you're covering ground, but not in which specific direction you are heading. It's a measure of 'how fast'.

Velocity: Speed with Direction

  • The rate of change of an object's displacement.
  • Vector quantity: Velocity = Displacement / Time.
  • SI unit: metres per second (m/s).

🎙️ Velocity is crucial for understanding the full picture of motion. A plane needs to know not just how fast it's flying, but also in what direction to reach its destination accurately.

Uniform Motion: Steady Pace

  • An object covers equal distances in equal intervals of time.
  • Velocity remains constant.
  • Acceleration is zero.

🎙️ Imagine a train moving at a constant speed of 100 km/h on a straight track. It covers the same distance every hour. This is a perfect example of uniform motion, where velocity doesn't change.

Non-Uniform Motion: Changing Pace

  • An object covers unequal distances in equal intervals of time.
  • Velocity changes (either magnitude, direction, or both).
  • Acceleration is non-zero.

🎙️ Most motion we observe in daily life, like a car accelerating or braking, is non-uniform. The speed or direction, or both, are constantly changing, leading to acceleration.

Acceleration: Changing Velocity

  • The rate of change of an object's velocity.
  • Vector quantity: Acceleration = Change in Velocity / Time.
  • SI unit: metres per second squared (m/s²).

🎙️ Acceleration is not just about speeding up; it also includes slowing down (deceleration) or changing direction, as long as the velocity vector is changing. It's a fundamental concept in dynamics.

2Final velocity (v) = 20 m/s
3Time (t) = 5 s

Kinematic Equations: Tools for Motion

  • A set of equations used to describe motion with constant acceleration.
  • Relate u, v, a, t, and s (initial velocity, final velocity, acceleration, time, displacement).
  • Applicable only for motion in a straight line with constant acceleration.

🎙️ These three equations are your best friends for solving problems involving uniformly accelerated motion. Memorise them and understand when to apply each one based on the given and required quantities.

Kinematic Equation 1: v = u + at

  • Relates final velocity (v) to initial velocity (u), acceleration (a), and time (t).
  • Directly shows how velocity changes with constant acceleration.
  • Useful when displacement is not given or required.

🎙️ This equation is fundamental. It tells you exactly what your final velocity will be if you start at a certain velocity and accelerate uniformly for a given time. It's the definition of acceleration rearranged.

🎙️ This equation helps you calculate how far an object travels when it's accelerating over a certain period. Think of a car covering more distance as it speeds up, proportional to time squared.

2Using v² = u² + 2as
30² = 20² + 2(-4)s
40 = 400 - 8s => s = 50 m

Position-Time Graphs: Visualising Position

  • Slope of x-t graph gives velocity.
  • Horizontal line: object at rest (zero velocity).
  • Straight line (non-zero slope): uniform velocity.
  • Curved line: non-uniform velocity (acceleration).

🎙️ Graphs are powerful tools to understand motion. A position-time graph directly shows you where an object is at any given moment and how its position changes, revealing its velocity.

Velocity-Time Graphs: Visualising Velocity

  • Slope of v-t graph gives acceleration.
  • Area under v-t graph gives displacement.
  • Horizontal line: uniform velocity (zero acceleration).
  • Straight line (non-zero slope): uniform acceleration.

🎙️ Velocity-time graphs are even more informative. From them, you can find both acceleration (from the slope) and displacement (from the area under the curve), providing a comprehensive view of motion.

Common Mistakes: Don't Get Trapped!

  • Confusing distance (scalar) with displacement (vector).
  • Using kinematic equations for non-constant acceleration.
  • Incorrectly applying sign conventions (e.g., for direction, acceleration/deceleration).
  • Units mismatch: Always convert to SI units before calculation.

🎙️ Many students lose marks on these common errors. Always double-check your definitions, assumptions, and units before finalising your answer. A careful approach prevents unnecessary mistakes.

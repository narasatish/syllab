What are Waves? - A disturbance that travels

  • A wave is a disturbance that propagates through a medium or space.
  • It transfers energy from one point to another without transferring matter.
  • Think of a stadium wave: people move up/down, but the wave travels around the stadium.

🎙️ Imagine a ripple on a pond; the water molecules mostly move up and down, but the ripple itself moves outwards. This is the essence of a wave: energy propagation without bulk matter transport. The stadium wave is a perfect analogy – people don't move seats, but the 'wave' of standing and sitting travels.

Types of Waves: Based on Medium Requirement

  • Mechanical waves need a material medium (solid, liquid, or gas) to propagate. E.g., sound waves, water waves.
  • Non-mechanical (Electromagnetic) waves do not need a medium and can travel through a vacuum. E.g., light waves, radio waves.
  • This distinction is crucial for understanding how different waves behave.

🎙️ The need for a medium is the primary classification. Mechanical waves rely on the vibration of particles, while non-mechanical waves are self-sustaining oscillations of electric and magnetic fields. This means light can travel through space, but sound cannot.

Transverse Waves: Particles oscillate perpendicular to propagation

  • Particles of the medium oscillate perpendicular (at 90°) to the direction of wave propagation.
  • Imagine a rope tied to a wall: wiggle one end up and down, the wave travels horizontally.
  • Crests (peaks) and Troughs (valleys) are formed in the medium.

🎙️ In a transverse wave, the energy moves forward, but the medium itself moves side-to-side or up-and-down. A common example is light, which is a transverse electromagnetic wave, or waves on the surface of water.

Anatomy of a Transverse Wave: Crest and Trough

  • Crest: The point of maximum positive displacement of a particle from its mean (equilibrium) position.
  • Trough: The point of maximum negative displacement of a particle from its mean (equilibrium) position.
  • These are the highest and lowest points of the wave shape.

🎙️ Understanding crests and troughs is fundamental to describing transverse waves. They represent the extreme positions of the medium's particles as the wave passes through. Think of mountain peaks and valleys.

Amplitude and Wavelength (λ): Key Spatial Properties

  • Amplitude (A): Maximum displacement of a particle from its mean position. It indicates the energy carried by the wave.
  • Wavelength (λ): The distance between two consecutive crests, or two consecutive troughs, or any two corresponding points in phase.
  • These define the 'size' and 'spread' of the wave in space.

🎙️ Amplitude is directly related to the intensity or loudness of a sound wave, or the brightness of a light wave. Wavelength tells us how 'long' one complete cycle of the wave is. Both are measured in units of length, typically metres.

Frequency (f) and Time Period (T): Key Temporal Properties

  • Frequency (f): Number of complete oscillations (or waves) passing a point per unit time, measured in Hertz (Hz).
  • Time Period (T): Time taken for one complete oscillation (or wave) to pass a point, measured in seconds (s).
  • They are inversely related: T = 1/f or f = 1/T.

🎙️ Frequency and time period describe the wave's behavior over time. Frequency is how often something happens, while time period is how long one event takes. These concepts are crucial for understanding wave speed and energy.

Longitudinal Waves: Particles oscillate parallel to propagation

  • Particles of the medium oscillate parallel to the direction of wave propagation.
  • Think of a Slinky spring: push one end, the compression travels along the spring.
  • Compressions (regions of high density/pressure) and Rarefactions (regions of low density/pressure) are formed.

🎙️ Sound waves are the most common example of longitudinal waves. When you speak, your vocal cords create compressions and rarefactions in the air, which travel to the listener's ear. The air molecules vibrate back and forth, parallel to the direction the sound travels.

Anatomy of a Longitudinal Wave: Compression and Rarefaction

  • Compression: Region where particles are crowded together, resulting in higher density and pressure.
  • Rarefaction: Region where particles are spread apart, resulting in lower density and pressure.
  • These correspond to the crests and troughs of transverse waves, representing maximum and minimum disturbances.

🎙️ In longitudinal waves, it's about how closely packed the particles are. Compressions are like the 'bunched up' parts of the Slinky, and rarefactions are the 'stretched out' parts. The wave carries energy through these variations in density and pressure.

Amplitude and Wavelength (λ) in Longitudinal Waves

  • Amplitude (A): The maximum change in density or pressure from the equilibrium value.
  • Wavelength (λ): The distance between two consecutive compressions or two consecutive rarefactions.
  • Similar concepts to transverse waves, but described in terms of density/pressure variations.

🎙️ While the particle motion is different, the fundamental definitions of amplitude and wavelength still apply. Amplitude here relates to how intense the compression or rarefaction is, and wavelength is still the spatial period of the wave.

Frequency (f) and Time Period (T) in Longitudinal Waves

  • Frequency (f): Number of compressions (or rarefactions) passing a point per second.
  • Time Period (T): Time taken for one complete compression-rarefaction cycle to pass a point.
  • The relationship T = 1/f remains universal for all types of waves.

🎙️ The temporal characteristics, frequency and time period, are defined identically for both transverse and longitudinal waves. They describe how often the wave pattern repeats itself in time at a given point.

Wave Speed (v): The Universal Wave Equation

  • Wave speed (v) is how fast the wave disturbance travels through the medium.
  • It is determined by the properties of the medium (e.g., elasticity, density), not the source.
  • The fundamental relation: v = fλ (speed = frequency × wavelength).

🎙️ This equation, v = fλ, is one of the most important formulae in wave physics. It links the spatial and temporal properties of a wave to its speed. Remember, the speed of sound in air is constant, but its frequency and wavelength can vary.

Principle of Superposition: Waves can overlap

  • When two or more waves overlap in a medium, the net displacement at any point is the vector sum of the individual displacements.
  • Waves pass through each other without being permanently altered.
  • Think of ripples in a pond crossing each other – they don't bounce off, they pass through.

🎙️ The principle of superposition is fundamental to understanding many wave phenomena, including interference and standing waves. It means waves don't 'collide' like solid objects; their effects simply add up at any given point in space and time.

Interference: Reinforcement or Cancellation

  • Constructive Interference: When crests meet crests (or troughs meet troughs), their amplitudes add up, resulting in a larger wave.
  • Destructive Interference: When a crest meets a trough, their amplitudes subtract, potentially cancelling each other out.
  • This is a direct consequence of the superposition principle.

🎙️ Interference is a fascinating phenomenon. It's why sometimes sounds get louder in certain spots and quieter in others, or why oil slicks show rainbow colours. It's all about how waves combine their effects.

Reflection of Waves: Bouncing Back

  • When a wave encounters a boundary or an obstacle, it bounces back into the original medium.
  • Fixed End Reflection: Wave reflects with a phase change of 180° (a crest becomes a trough upon reflection).
  • Free End Reflection: Wave reflects without a phase change (a crest remains a crest upon reflection).

🎙️ Reflection is a common wave behavior. The key difference between fixed and free end reflection lies in the phase change. This concept is vital for understanding how standing waves are formed in confined spaces like guitar strings or air columns.

Standing Waves (Stationary Waves): Formation

  • Formed when two identical waves travelling in opposite directions superpose.
  • They appear stationary, with points of zero displacement (nodes) and maximum displacement (antinodes).
  • Imagine a vibrating guitar string – it looks like it's standing still, just oscillating in place.

🎙️ Standing waves are a beautiful example of superposition and reflection. They are crucial for understanding musical instruments. The wave itself isn't moving forward; instead, energy is trapped and oscillates within a confined region.

Nodes and Antinodes: Key Features of Standing Waves

  • Nodes: Points in a standing wave where particles always remain at their mean (equilibrium) position (zero displacement).
  • Antinodes: Points in a standing wave where particles oscillate with maximum amplitude.
  • The distance between two consecutive nodes (or antinodes) is λ/2. The distance between a node and an adjacent antinode is λ/4.

🎙️ Nodes are points of permanent destructive interference, while antinodes are points of permanent constructive interference. These fixed patterns are what give standing waves their 'stationary' appearance. Remember these distances for numerical problems.

Modes of Vibration: Harmonics in a Stretched String

  • A string fixed at both ends can vibrate in different patterns, called modes or harmonics.
  • Fundamental frequency (first harmonic): One loop, λ = 2L.
  • Second harmonic (first overtone): Two loops, λ = L.
  • Third harmonic (second overtone): Three loops, λ = 2L/3.

🎙️ These different modes of vibration produce distinct musical notes, known as harmonics or overtones. The fundamental frequency is the lowest possible frequency at which the string can vibrate. Higher harmonics are integer multiples of the fundamental frequency.

  • Problem: A string of length 1 m is fixed at both ends. Its fundamental frequency is 100 Hz. What is the speed of the wave on the string?
  • Step 1: For the fundamental mode (first harmonic), the wavelength (λ) is twice the length (L) of the string.
  • Step 2: Use the wave equation v = fλ to find the speed.

🎙️ This example combines the concepts of standing waves in strings with the wave equation. Understanding the relationship between string length and wavelength for different harmonics is crucial for solving such problems.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Mixing up nodes and antinodes, or their distances (λ/4 vs λ/2).

What are Waves? The Basics

  • A disturbance that propagates through a medium or space.
  • Transfers energy without transferring matter.
  • Caused by oscillations or vibrations.
  • Examples: Water ripples, sound, light.

🎙️ At its core, a wave is a disturbance that moves. Importantly, it's the energy that travels, not the material particles of the medium. Think of a ripple in water; the water itself doesn't move across the pond, but the disturbance does.

Types of Waves: Mechanical vs. Non-Mechanical

  • Mechanical Waves: Require a material medium for propagation.
  • Non-Mechanical (Electromagnetic) Waves: Do not require a medium.
  • Key distinction based on medium requirement.

🎙️ The primary classification of waves is based on whether they need a medium to travel. Mechanical waves, like sound, must have a medium. Electromagnetic waves, such as light, can travel through a vacuum.

Mechanical Waves: Characteristics

  • Require an elastic medium (solid, liquid, gas).
  • Propagate due to elastic properties of the medium.
  • Cannot travel through vacuum.
  • Examples: Sound waves, water waves, waves on a string.

🎙️ Mechanical waves rely on the interaction of particles within a medium to transfer energy. Imagine a domino effect; each domino passes energy to the next. This is why sound cannot travel in space.

Electromagnetic Waves: Characteristics

  • Do not require a material medium for propagation.
  • Consist of oscillating electric and magnetic fields.
  • Travel at the speed of light in vacuum (c ≈ 3x10⁸ m/s).
  • Examples: Light, radio waves, X-rays, microwaves.

🎙️ Electromagnetic waves are unique because they are self-propagating disturbances of electric and magnetic fields. They don't need particles to 'push' each other, allowing them to traverse the vast emptiness of space, bringing us sunlight and radio signals.

Transverse Waves: Oscillations Perpendicular to Propagation

  • Particles of the medium oscillate perpendicular to wave direction.
  • Form crests (max upward displacement) and troughs (max downward).
  • Can be mechanical (string) or electromagnetic (light).
  • Cannot be formed in gases (no shear rigidity).

🎙️ In a transverse wave, imagine shaking a rope up and down; the wave moves horizontally, but the rope segments move vertically. This perpendicular motion is characteristic. Light waves are also transverse, with electric and magnetic fields oscillating perpendicular to the direction of propagation.

Longitudinal Waves: Oscillations Parallel to Propagation

  • Particles of the medium oscillate parallel to wave direction.
  • Form compressions (regions of high density/pressure) and rarefactions (low density/pressure).
  • Can propagate in solids, liquids, and gases.
  • Example: Sound waves.

🎙️ For longitudinal waves, think of a Slinky being pushed and pulled. The coils move back and forth along the same direction as the wave travels. Sound waves are the most common example, where air molecules oscillate back and forth, creating areas of varying pressure.

Wave Terminology (Part 1): Amplitude and Wavelength

  • Amplitude (A): Maximum displacement of a particle from its mean position.
  • Wavelength (λ): Distance between two consecutive crests/troughs (transverse) or compressions/rarefactions (longitudinal).
  • SI Unit of Amplitude: Meter (m).
  • SI Unit of Wavelength: Meter (m).

🎙️ Understanding these terms is crucial for describing waves quantitatively. Amplitude tells us about the energy or intensity of the wave, while wavelength defines its spatial extent. Always remember their SI units for calculations.

Wave Terminology (Part 2): Frequency, Period, and Speed

  • Frequency (f): Number of oscillations per unit time (Hz).
  • Period (T): Time taken for one complete oscillation (s). T = 1/f.
  • Wave Speed (v): Distance traveled by the wave per unit time (m/s).
  • Wave number (k): 2π/λ (rad/m).

🎙️ Frequency and period are inversely related and describe the temporal aspect of the wave. Wave speed tells us how fast the disturbance is propagating through the medium. The wave number is less common but important in some wave equations.

The Wave Equation: Relating Speed, Wavelength, and Frequency

  • The fundamental relationship: v = fλ.
  • Wave speed (v) depends on the properties of the medium.
  • Frequency (f) is determined by the source.
  • Wavelength (λ) adjusts based on v and f.

🎙️ This equation, v = fλ, is one of the most important formulas in wave physics. It links the speed of the wave to its frequency and wavelength. Remember that for a given medium, wave speed is constant, so if frequency changes, wavelength must change proportionally.

Worked Example 1: Calculating Wave Speed

  • Problem: A sound wave has a frequency of 200 Hz and a wavelength of 1.7 m. Calculate its speed.
  • Given: f = 200 Hz, λ = 1.7 m.
  • Formula: v = fλ.
  • Solution: v = (200 Hz) * (1.7 m) = 340 m/s.

🎙️ This example demonstrates a straightforward application of the wave equation. Always list your given values, recall the relevant formula, substitute carefully, and state your answer with correct units. This approach helps avoid common calculation errors.

Principle of Superposition: When Waves Meet

  • When two or more waves overlap, the net displacement at any point is the vector sum of individual displacements.
  • Waves pass through each other without being altered.
  • Foundation for interference and standing waves.
  • Applies to all types of waves.

🎙️ The principle of superposition is incredibly powerful. It means that when waves cross paths, their effects simply add up, and then they continue on their way as if nothing happened. This additive property leads to fascinating phenomena.

Interference of Waves: Constructive and Destructive

  • Constructive Interference: Waves combine to produce a larger resultant amplitude.
  • Destructive Interference: Waves combine to produce a smaller or zero resultant amplitude.
  • Occurs when two coherent waves overlap.
  • Path difference and phase difference are key factors.

🎙️ Interference is a direct consequence of superposition. When crests meet crests, we get constructive interference, a stronger wave. When a crest meets a trough, we get destructive interference, potentially canceling out the wave. Coherence is essential for stable interference patterns.

Reflection of Waves: Bouncing Back

  • When a wave encounters a boundary and changes direction, returning into the same medium.
  • Fixed end reflection: Phase reversal (crest reflects as trough).
  • Free end reflection: No phase reversal (crest reflects as crest).
  • Speed, frequency, and wavelength remain unchanged.

🎙️ Reflection is a common wave phenomenon. The crucial point for exam purposes is understanding the phase change at different types of boundaries. A fixed end acts like a denser medium, causing a 180-degree phase shift, while a free end behaves like a rarer medium with no phase change.

Refraction of Waves: Bending Through Media

  • Change in direction of a wave as it passes from one medium to another.
  • Caused by a change in wave speed.
  • Frequency remains constant.
  • Wavelength and speed change.

🎙️ Refraction occurs because the wave's speed changes as it enters a new medium. While the speed and wavelength change, the frequency, which is determined by the source, remains constant. This bending is responsible for phenomena like lenses focusing light.

Standing Waves (Stationary Waves): Formation

  • Formed by the superposition of two identical waves traveling in opposite directions.
  • Appear stationary, with fixed positions of zero (nodes) and maximum (antinodes) displacement.
  • No net transfer of energy.
  • Crucial for musical instruments.

🎙️ Standing waves are a special case of interference. When two identical waves, moving in opposite directions, interfere, they create a pattern where certain points are always at rest, and others oscillate with maximum amplitude. This is fundamental to how musical instruments produce sound.

Nodes and Antinodes: Key Features of Standing Waves

  • Nodes: Points of zero displacement and maximum pressure change (for sound).
  • Antinodes: Points of maximum displacement and minimum pressure change (for sound).
  • Distance between two consecutive nodes or antinodes is λ/2.
  • Distance between a node and adjacent antinode is λ/4.

🎙️ Nodes and antinodes are the defining characteristics of a standing wave. Understanding their positions and the distances between them is vital for solving problems related to standing waves in strings and pipes. Remember these relationships for calculations.

Vibrations in Strings (Part 1): Harmonics

  • A string fixed at both ends can vibrate in different modes.
  • These modes are called harmonics or overtones.
  • The fundamental frequency (first harmonic) has one antinode.
  • Higher harmonics have more antinodes and nodes.

🎙️ When a string vibrates, it doesn't just vibrate at one frequency. It can vibrate in multiple patterns, each corresponding to a different harmonic. These harmonics are integer multiples of the fundamental frequency and determine the timbre of a musical instrument.

Vibrations in Strings (Part 2): Fundamental Frequency and Overtones

  • Fundamental frequency (f₁): Lowest possible frequency of vibration (first harmonic).
  • For a string fixed at both ends, f₁ = v / (2L), where L is string length.
  • Overtones: Frequencies higher than the fundamental.
  • Harmonics are integral multiples of the fundamental frequency (f_n = n * f₁).

🎙️ The formula for the fundamental frequency is critical for calculations involving vibrating strings. Remember that 'n' represents the harmonic number, so the second harmonic is twice the fundamental, and so on. This concept explains why different instruments sound distinct even when playing the same note.

Worked Example 2: Standing Waves in a String

  • Problem: A string of length 0.5 m vibrates with a fundamental frequency of 400 Hz. Find the speed of the wave.
  • Given: L = 0.5 m, f₁ = 400 Hz.
  • Formula: f₁ = v / (2L) => v = 2L * f₁.
  • Solution: v = 2 * (0.5 m) * (400 Hz) = 400 m/s.

🎙️ This example combines the concept of fundamental frequency with the wave speed. Pay attention to the relationship between the length of the string and the wavelength for the fundamental mode (λ = 2L). This is a common exam question type.

Doppler Effect: Apparent Change in Frequency

  • Apparent change in frequency of a wave due to relative motion between source and observer.
  • Source moving towards observer: Higher apparent frequency.
  • Source moving away from observer: Lower apparent frequency.
  • Applies to both sound and light waves.

🎙️ The Doppler Effect is something you experience daily, like the changing pitch of an ambulance siren as it passes by. It's crucial to understand that the actual frequency of the source doesn't change, only its perceived frequency by the observer.

Applications of Doppler Effect

  • Radar guns for measuring vehicle speed.
  • Medical imaging (e.g., Doppler ultrasound for blood flow).
  • Astronomy (measuring speed of stars/galaxies - redshift/blueshift).
  • Weather forecasting (Doppler radar for storm movement).

🎙️ The Doppler Effect has wide-ranging practical applications in various fields. From traffic enforcement to understanding the expansion of the universe, this phenomenon is a powerful tool for measuring relative motion. Be aware of these examples for your exams.

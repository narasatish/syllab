What are Waves? The Basics

  • A disturbance that propagates through a medium or space.
  • Transfers energy from one point to another.
  • Does NOT involve the transfer of matter.

🎙️ Imagine a ripple in a pond. The water itself doesn't travel across the pond, but the disturbance, the energy, does. That's the essence of a wave.

Mechanical Waves: Requiring a Medium

  • Require a material medium (solid, liquid, or gas) for propagation.
  • Particles of the medium oscillate but do not move with the wave.
  • Examples: Sound waves, water waves, seismic waves.

🎙️ Think of a slinky. When you push one end, the compression travels, but the individual coils just move back and forth. The medium's particles are linked, transferring the disturbance.

Non-mechanical Waves: No Medium Needed

  • Do NOT require a material medium for propagation.
  • Can travel through a vacuum.
  • Examples: Light waves, radio waves, X-rays, microwaves.

🎙️ These waves are also known as Electromagnetic Waves. The sun's light reaches us through the vacuum of space, a perfect example of their ability to travel without a medium.

Transverse Waves: Perpendicular Motion

  • Particle oscillation is perpendicular to the wave propagation direction.
  • Formed by crests (peaks) and troughs (valleys).
  • Can propagate only through solids and on the surface of liquids.

🎙️ Imagine shaking a rope up and down while the wave travels horizontally. The rope segments move vertically, but the wave moves horizontally. This is the hallmark of a transverse wave.

Transverse Waves: Examples & Key Properties

  • Examples: Waves on a string, light waves (EM waves are transverse).
  • They involve shear stress in the medium.
  • Cannot be formed in gases or liquids (except surface waves).

🎙️ When you pluck a guitar string, you create a transverse wave. Light, though non-mechanical, also exhibits transverse wave properties, with oscillating electric and magnetic fields perpendicular to propagation.

Longitudinal Waves: Parallel Motion

  • Particle oscillation is parallel to the wave propagation direction.
  • Formed by compressions (regions of high density) and rarefactions (regions of low density).
  • Can propagate through solids, liquids, and gases.

🎙️ Think of a spring being pushed and pulled. The coils move back and forth in the same direction the disturbance travels. This is a longitudinal wave.

Wave Characteristics: Amplitude (A)

  • Maximum displacement of a particle from its mean position.
  • Determines the intensity or loudness of a wave.
  • Unit: meter (m).

🎙️ Amplitude is a measure of the wave's 'strength' or 'energy'. A louder sound wave has a larger amplitude, and a brighter light wave also has a larger amplitude.

Wave Characteristics: Wavelength (λ)

  • Distance between two consecutive crests or troughs (transverse).
  • Distance between two consecutive compressions or rarefactions (longitudinal).
  • Unit: meter (m).

🎙️ Wavelength is essentially the spatial period of the wave. It tells us how long one complete cycle of the wave is in space.

Wave Characteristics: Frequency (f) & Time Period (T)

  • Frequency (f): Number of oscillations per second. Unit: Hertz (Hz).
  • Time Period (T): Time taken for one complete oscillation. Unit: second (s).
  • Relationship: f = 1/T or T = 1/f.

🎙️ Frequency and Time Period are inversely related. High frequency means many oscillations per second, hence a short time period for each oscillation.

The Wave Equation: v = fλ

  • Wave speed (v) is the product of frequency (f) and wavelength (λ).
  • This fundamental equation applies to all types of progressive waves.
  • Speed depends on the properties of the medium.

🎙️ This equation is crucial for solving many wave problems. Remember that while frequency and wavelength can change when a wave enters a new medium, the speed of the wave is primarily determined by the medium itself.

Worked Example: Calculating Wave Speed

  • Problem: A sound wave has a frequency of 250 Hz and a wavelength of 1.3 m. Calculate its speed.
  • Given: f = 250 Hz, λ = 1.3 m.
  • Formula: v = fλ.
  • Solution: v = (250 Hz) * (1.3 m) = 325 m/s.

🎙️ Always start by identifying the given values and the quantity you need to find. Then, select the appropriate formula and perform the calculation, ensuring units are consistent.

Common Mistakes: Time Period vs. Frequency

  • Confusing time period (T) with frequency (f) in calculations.
  • Remember T is time for ONE cycle, f is cycles per SECOND.
  • Always check units: T in seconds, f in Hertz (1/s).

🎙️ This is a frequent source of error. If a problem gives you time for multiple cycles, convert it correctly to find the time period for a single cycle or the frequency.

Principle of Superposition of Waves

  • When two or more waves overlap, the resultant displacement at any point is the vector sum of individual displacements.
  • Applies to all types of waves.
  • Foundation for interference and standing waves.

🎙️ This principle means waves don't 'crash' into each other and stop. They pass through each other, momentarily combining their effects, and then continue as if nothing happened.

Interference of Waves: Constructive & Destructive

  • Constructive Interference: Waves combine to produce a larger amplitude.
  • Destructive Interference: Waves combine to produce a smaller or zero amplitude.
  • Occurs when waves from different sources meet.

🎙️ Interference is a direct consequence of superposition. When crest meets crest, you get a bigger crest (constructive). When crest meets trough, they can cancel out (destructive).

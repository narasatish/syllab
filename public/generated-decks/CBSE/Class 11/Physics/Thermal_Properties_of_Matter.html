Defining Heat and Temperature

  • Heat: Form of energy transferred between systems due to temperature difference (Units: Joule, Calorie).
  • Temperature: Measure of the average kinetic energy of molecules (Units: Kelvin, Celsius, Fahrenheit).
  • Heat flows from higher temperature to lower temperature.

🎙️ Formally, heat is the energy in transit, while temperature is a measure of the 'hotness' or 'coldness' of a body. Understanding their definitions and units is crucial for solving problems.

Linear Expansion

  • Increase in length of a solid when heated.
  • Formula: ΔL = L₀αΔT
  • α (coefficient of linear expansion): Property of material, unit K⁻¹ or °C⁻¹.

🎙️ Linear expansion refers to the change in one dimension, typically length. The formula helps us calculate this change, where alpha is a material-specific constant indicating how much a material expands per degree change in temperature.

📏
Length Change

Worked Example 1: Linear Expansion

  • A steel rod is 2 m long at 20°C. If α for steel is 1.2 × 10⁻⁵ °C⁻¹, what is its length at 50°C?
  • Steps: Identify given, apply formula, calculate ΔL, find final length.
  • Solution: ΔL = 2 × 1.2 × 10⁻⁵ × (50-20) = 0.00072 m. Final Length = 2 + 0.00072 = 2.00072 m.

🎙️ Let's walk through an example. Always start by listing your knowns, then apply the formula carefully. Remember to calculate the change in length first, then add it to the original length to get the final length.

Area (Superficial) Expansion

  • Increase in surface area of a solid when heated.
  • Formula: ΔA = A₀βΔT
  • β (coefficient of area expansion) ≈ 2α.

🎙️ Similar to linear expansion, solids also expand in two dimensions, affecting their surface area. The coefficient of area expansion, beta, is approximately twice the linear expansion coefficient.

Volume (Cubical) Expansion

  • Increase in volume of solids, liquids, and gases when heated.
  • Formula: ΔV = V₀γΔT
  • γ (coefficient of volume expansion) ≈ 3α.

🎙️ Volume expansion is the most general form, applicable to all states of matter. For isotropic solids, the volume expansion coefficient, gamma, is approximately three times the linear expansion coefficient.

Relationship Between Coefficients

  • For isotropic solids: β = 2α and γ = 3α.
  • This implies: α : β : γ = 1 : 2 : 3.
  • These relationships simplify calculations and understanding.

🎙️ These simple relationships between alpha, beta, and gamma are important for problem-solving. Knowing one coefficient allows you to deduce the others for the same material, assuming it expands uniformly in all directions.

Anomalous Expansion of Water

  • Water shows unusual behavior between 0°C and 4°C.
  • It contracts when heated from 0°C to 4°C, reaching maximum density at 4°C.
  • Above 4°C, it expands normally.
  • Crucial for aquatic life in cold regions.

🎙️ Water is an extraordinary substance. Its anomalous expansion between 0°C and 4°C is vital for the survival of aquatic life. This behavior ensures that ice floats and water at 4°C remains at the bottom of lakes, preventing them from freezing solid.

Defining Specific Heat Capacity

  • Specific Heat Capacity (c): Amount of heat required to raise the temperature of 1 unit mass of a substance by 1 degree.
  • Formula: Q = mcΔT
  • Units: J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹ or J kg⁻¹ °C⁻¹.

🎙️ Specific heat capacity is a material-dependent property. It tells us how 'stubborn' a substance is to change its temperature. A higher specific heat means more energy is needed for the same temperature change.

Molar Specific Heat Capacity

  • Molar Specific Heat (C): Amount of heat required to raise the temperature of 1 mole of a substance by 1 degree.
  • Formula: Q = nCΔT (where n is number of moles).
  • Units: J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.

🎙️ Sometimes it's more convenient to work with moles rather than mass, especially in chemistry contexts. Molar specific heat capacity is analogous to specific heat capacity but refers to one mole of the substance.

Principle of Calorimetry

  • When bodies at different temperatures are brought into thermal contact, heat flows.
  • Principle: Heat Lost by Hot Body = Heat Gained by Cold Body (assuming no heat loss to surroundings).
  • Basis for measuring specific heat capacities.

🎙️ Calorimetry is the science of measuring heat. The principle of calorimetry is a direct application of the law of conservation of energy. It's fundamental for analyzing heat exchange in various systems.

↔️
Heat Exchange

Worked Example 2: Calorimetry

  • 200g of water at 80°C is mixed with 100g of water at 20°C. Find the final temperature.
  • Steps: Heat lost by hot water = Heat gained by cold water.
  • m₁cΔT₁ = m₂cΔT₂. 200(T_final - 80) = 100(T_final - 20).
  • Solution: T_final = 60°C.

🎙️ This is a classic calorimetry problem. Notice how the specific heat capacity 'c' cancels out because it's the same substance. The key is setting up the equation correctly based on the principle of heat exchange.

Change of State (Phase Change)

  • Matter can exist in different states: solid, liquid, gas.
  • Phase change occurs at constant temperature (e.g., ice melting at 0°C, water boiling at 100°C).
  • Energy is absorbed or released during these changes.

🎙️ We all know that ice melts into water and water boils into steam. These are examples of phase changes. During these transitions, the temperature remains constant even though heat is being added or removed.

Matter exists in three states: solid, liquid, and gas. Each state has different properties

Latent Heat

  • Heat absorbed or released during a phase change at constant temperature.
  • It doesn't change temperature, but changes the state.
  • Latent Heat of Fusion (L_f): Solid to liquid / liquid to solid.
  • Latent Heat of Vaporization (L_v): Liquid to gas / gas to liquid.
  • Formula: Q = mL (where L is latent heat).

🎙️ Latent heat is 'hidden' heat because it doesn't cause a temperature change. Instead, it's used to break or form intermolecular bonds during the phase transition. It's a crucial concept for understanding processes like melting and boiling.

Worked Example 3: Latent Heat

  • Calculate the heat required to convert 10g of ice at 0°C to water at 0°C.
  • Given: Latent heat of fusion of ice = 334 J/g.
  • Steps: Identify given, apply formula Q = mL.
  • Solution: Q = 10g × 334 J/g = 3340 J.

🎙️ This example demonstrates a simple application of latent heat of fusion. Remember, during a phase change, the temperature remains constant, so we only use the latent heat formula, not specific heat.

🎙️ Conduction is how heat travels through solids. Imagine molecules vibrating and passing energy to their neighbors. Metals are excellent conductors due to free electrons, while wood is a poor conductor.

Heat Transfer: Convection

  • Heat transfer through the actual movement of fluid (liquid or gas) particles.
  • Analogy: A hot air balloon rising.
  • Common in fluids (e.g., boiling water, sea breeze).

🎙️ Convection involves the bulk movement of fluids. Hotter, less dense fluid rises, and cooler, denser fluid sinks, creating convection currents. This is how radiators heat a room or how a sea breeze occurs.

Heat Transfer: Radiation

  • Analogy: Feeling the warmth of the sun or a bonfire.

🎙️ Radiation is unique because it doesn't require a medium. This is how the sun's heat reaches Earth. Every object above absolute zero emits thermal radiation, and darker, duller surfaces are better absorbers and emitters.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Confusing Heat and Temperature: Heat is energy, temp is average KE.
  • Incorrect Units: Always check and convert units (J, Calorie, K, °C).
  • Ignoring Latent Heat: For phase changes, temperature is constant.
  • Sign Errors in Calorimetry: Heat lost is positive, heat gained is positive.
  • Assuming all materials expand equally: α, β, γ are material-specific.

🎙️ These are typical pitfalls students encounter. Pay close attention to definitions, units, and the conditions under which specific formulas apply. A careful approach can prevent many errors.

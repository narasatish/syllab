What is a Rigid Body?

  • An object with a definite shape and size.
  • Distances between constituent particles remain constant.
  • Idealization: no deformation under external forces.
  • Examples: a stone, a wheel, a wooden block.

🎙️ A rigid body is a body that does not deform under the action of external forces. In simpler terms, the distances between any two particles within the body remain unchanged, regardless of how forces are applied. While no object is perfectly rigid in reality, this concept is a powerful idealization that helps us simplify and understand complex motions.

System of Particles

  • A collection of a large number of particles.
  • These particles interact with each other.
  • Can be rigid or non-rigid (e.g., gas, liquid).
  • Our focus: rigid bodies as systems of particles.

🎙️ A system of particles refers to any collection of individual particles. These particles can be atoms, molecules, or even larger macroscopic objects. In this chapter, we primarily consider systems where the particles are bound together, forming a rigid body, allowing us to study their collective motion and interactions.

Centre of Mass (CM)

  • A point where the entire mass of a system is concentrated.
  • Its motion describes the translational motion of the entire system.
  • Crucial for analyzing complex motions.
  • May or may not lie within the physical body.

🎙️ The Centre of Mass is a very special point within a system of particles. It's the unique point where, if you apply an external force, the entire system would accelerate as if all its mass were concentrated there. Understanding the CM simplifies the analysis of a system's motion, separating its translational movement from its rotational movement.

CM for Two Particles

  • For two particles m₁ and m₂ at positions r₁ and r₂.
  • Position vector of CM: R_CM = (m₁r₁ + m₂r₂) / (m₁ + m₂).
  • If origin is at m₁, R_CM = m₂r / (m₁ + m₂).
  • CM lies closer to the heavier mass.

🎙️ Let's start with the simplest case: two particles. The formula shown helps us pinpoint the exact location of their centre of mass. Notice that if one mass is significantly larger than the other, the CM will be much closer to the heavier particle, intuitively making sense as it's the 'balance point' of the system.

CM for N Particles & Rigid Bodies

  • For N particles: R_CM = (∑ mᵢrᵢ) / (∑ mᵢ).
  • For rigid bodies (continuous mass distribution): R_CM = (∫ r dm) / (∫ dm).
  • Integration is over the entire volume/area/length of the body.
  • Symmetry often simplifies CM calculation for uniform bodies.

🎙️ Extending the concept, for a system with many discrete particles, we sum up the product of each particle's mass and position vector, then divide by the total mass. For continuous bodies like rods or spheres, we use integration, treating the body as an infinite collection of infinitesimal mass elements. Symmetry is a great shortcut here; for uniform objects, the CM is often at the geometric center.

Worked Example: CM Calculation

  • Problem: Two particles, m₁=1kg at (0,0) and m₂=2kg at (3,0). Find CM.
  • Solution: R_CM = (1*(0î+0ĵ) + 2*(3î+0ĵ)) / (1+2).
  • R_CM = (6î) / 3 = 2î.
  • Answer: Centre of Mass is at (2,0).

🎙️ Here's a straightforward example. We apply the formula for two particles directly. Notice how the CM is closer to the heavier 2kg mass, which is at (3,0). The CM is at (2,0), which is indeed closer to (3,0) than to (0,0). Always check if your answer makes physical sense!

Motion of Centre of Mass

  • Velocity of CM: V_CM = (∑ mᵢvᵢ) / M_total.
  • Acceleration of CM: A_CM = (∑ mᵢaᵢ) / M_total.
  • Newton's Second Law for a system: F_ext = M_total * A_CM.
  • CM moves as if all external forces act on it.

🎙️ The beauty of the Centre of Mass concept is evident when we study its motion. The velocity and acceleration of the CM are simply weighted averages of the individual particle velocities and accelerations. Most importantly, the total external force acting on a system equals the total mass times the acceleration of its CM. This means the CM's motion is purely translational, unaffected by internal forces.

Vector Product of Two Vectors (Cross Product)

  • A x B = |A||B| sinθ n̂, where n̂ is perpendicular to A and B.
  • Magnitude: Area of parallelogram formed by A and B.
  • Direction: Given by right-hand thumb rule.
  • Non-commutative: A x B = - (B x A).

🎙️ Before diving into rotational dynamics, we need to master the vector product, also known as the cross product. Unlike the scalar dot product, the cross product results in a vector. Its direction is crucial and determined by the right-hand rule, pointing perpendicular to both original vectors. This will be fundamental for understanding torque and angular momentum.

Angular Displacement & Velocity

  • Angular Displacement (Δθ): Angle rotated by a particle/body.
  • Angular Velocity (ω): Rate of change of angular displacement (ω = dθ/dt).
  • Vector quantity, direction along axis of rotation (Right-Hand Rule).
  • Relation to linear velocity: v = rω (for a point at radius r).

🎙️ Just as linear motion has displacement and velocity, rotational motion has angular displacement and angular velocity. Angular displacement measures how much an object has rotated. Angular velocity, the rate of this rotation, is a vector pointing along the axis of rotation using the right-hand rule. Remember the crucial link: linear velocity 'v' for a point on a rotating body is 'rω'.

Angular Acceleration (α)

  • Rate of change of angular velocity (α = dω/dt).
  • Vector quantity, direction same as Δω.
  • Relation to linear acceleration: a_t = rα (tangential acceleration).
  • Units: radians per second squared (rad/s²).

🎙️ When an object's angular velocity changes, it experiences angular acceleration. If the angular velocity increases, α is in the same direction as ω; if it decreases, α is opposite to ω. This angular acceleration directly relates to the tangential linear acceleration of a point on the rotating body, 'a_t = rα'. This is distinct from centripetal acceleration, which only changes direction.

Torque (Moment of Force)

  • Rotational analogue of force.
  • Causes or tends to cause rotational motion.
  • τ = r x F (vector product of position vector and force).
  • Magnitude: τ = rF sinθ, where θ is angle between r and F.

🎙️ Torque is the rotational equivalent of linear force. While force causes linear acceleration, torque causes angular acceleration. It depends not only on the magnitude of the force but also on where and how it's applied relative to the axis of rotation. The 'lever arm' or perpendicular distance from the axis to the line of action of the force is key here.

Torque: Real-Life Examples

  • Opening a door: pushing far from hinges creates more torque.
  • Tightening a bolt with a wrench: longer wrench, more torque.
  • Pedaling a bicycle: force on pedal creates torque on crank.
  • See-saw balance: depends on weight and distance from pivot.

🎙️ Torque is all around us! Think about opening a heavy door – it's much easier to push near the handle (far from the hinges) than near the hinges themselves. This is because pushing further away increases the 'lever arm', thus increasing the torque for the same applied force. Similarly, a longer wrench provides more torque, making it easier to tighten a stubborn bolt.

Worked Example: Calculating Torque

  • Problem: Force F = (3î + 2ĵ) N acts at r = (1î - 1ĵ) m. Find torque.
  • Solution: τ = r x F = (1î - 1ĵ) x (3î + 2ĵ).
  • τ = (1*2 - (-1)*3) k̂ = (2 + 3) k̂ = 5 k̂ Nm.
  • Answer: Torque is 5 Nm along the +z direction.

🎙️ Here, we use the determinant method for the cross product in component form. Remember that î x ĵ = k̂, ĵ x k̂ = î, k̂ x î = ĵ, and any vector crossed with itself is zero. Also, î x î = ĵ x ĵ = k̂ x k̂ = 0. The result is a vector perpendicular to both 'r' and 'F', giving the direction of rotation.

Angular Momentum (L)

  • Rotational analogue of linear momentum (p = mv).
  • For a single particle: L = r x p = r x (mv).
  • For a rigid body: L = Iω (where I is Moment of Inertia).
  • Vector quantity, direction same as ω (if I is scalar).

🎙️ Angular momentum is a measure of an object's tendency to continue rotating. For a single particle, it's the cross product of its position vector and its linear momentum. For a rigid body, it's the product of its moment of inertia and angular velocity. Like linear momentum, it's a conserved quantity under certain conditions, making it incredibly important in physics.

Conservation of Angular Momentum

  • If net external torque (τ_ext) on a system is zero, L is conserved.
  • L_initial = L_final, or I₁ω₁ = I₂ω₂.
  • Examples: ice skater spinning faster by pulling arms in.
  • Earth's rotation, planetary orbits.

🎙️ This is a cornerstone principle! If no external torque acts on a system, its total angular momentum remains constant. This means if the moment of inertia changes, the angular velocity must adjust to keep the product Iω constant. The classic example is an ice skater: as she pulls her arms in, her moment of inertia decreases, so her angular velocity increases dramatically.

Moment of Inertia (I)

  • Rotational analogue of mass.
  • Measures resistance to change in rotational motion.
  • Depends on mass distribution and axis of rotation.
  • For a system of particles: I = ∑ mᵢrᵢ².

🎙️ Moment of Inertia is to rotational motion what mass is to linear motion. It quantifies how difficult it is to change an object's state of rotation. Crucially, it depends not just on the total mass but also on how that mass is distributed relative to the axis of rotation. A heavier mass further from the axis contributes more to the moment of inertia.

Radius of Gyration (k)

  • Hypothetical distance where entire mass could be concentrated.
  • Would yield the same moment of inertia as actual body.
  • I = Mk², where M is total mass.
  • k = √(I/M), unit is meters.

🎙️ The radius of gyration is a convenient way to express the moment of inertia in terms of a single distance. Imagine all the mass of a body concentrated into a single point mass; 'k' is the distance this point mass would need to be from the axis of rotation to have the same moment of inertia as the original body. It's a useful parameter for comparing rotational properties of different bodies.

Theorems of Parallel and Perpendicular Axes

  • Parallel Axis Theorem: I = I_CM + Md².
  • Perpendicular Axis Theorem: I_z = I_x + I_y (for planar bodies).
  • These theorems simplify calculation of Moment of Inertia.
  • I_CM is moment of inertia about an axis through CM.

🎙️ These two theorems are incredibly powerful tools for calculating moments of inertia without having to perform complex integrations every time. The Parallel Axis Theorem allows us to find 'I' about any axis if we know 'I' about a parallel axis passing through the CM. The Perpendicular Axis Theorem is specific to planar objects, relating moments of inertia about three mutually perpendicular axes.

Rotational Kinetic Energy

  • Energy due to rotational motion.
  • K_rot = ½ Iω².
  • Analogous to K_linear = ½ mv².
  • Total kinetic energy = K_translational + K_rotational (for rolling).

🎙️ Just like objects moving linearly have kinetic energy, rotating objects also possess kinetic energy, but it's expressed in terms of moment of inertia and angular velocity. This rotational kinetic energy is distinct from translational kinetic energy. For objects that are both translating and rotating, like a rolling wheel, the total kinetic energy is the sum of these two forms.

Rolling Motion

  • Combination of translational and rotational motion.
  • Pure rolling: Point of contact with surface is instantaneously at rest.
  • Condition for pure rolling: v_CM = Rω.
  • Total KE = ½ Mv_CM² + ½ I_CMω².

🎙️ Rolling motion is a very common type of motion, seen in wheels, balls, and cylinders. It's a superimposition of translational motion of the center of mass and rotational motion about the center of mass. For pure rolling, there's no slipping at the point of contact, leading to the crucial condition v_CM = Rω. The total kinetic energy is the sum of its translational and rotational components.

Comparison: Linear vs. Rotational Motion

  • Mass (m) ↔ Moment of Inertia (I).
  • Linear displacement (x) ↔ Angular displacement (θ).
  • Linear velocity (v) ↔ Angular velocity (ω).
  • Force (F) ↔ Torque (τ).

🎙️ This slide summarizes the beautiful analogy between linear and rotational dynamics. For every concept in linear motion, there's a direct rotational counterpart. Understanding these parallels helps in grasping the new rotational concepts more easily. For example, Newton's second law F=ma has its rotational equivalent τ=Iα.

Quick Check & Common Mistakes

  • Is angular momentum conserved if external torque is non-zero?
  • What's the difference between torque and force?
  • Can CM be outside the body? (e.g., a ring)
  • Remember vector nature: direction matters for torque & angular momentum.

🎙️ Let's quickly test our understanding and highlight some common pitfalls. Angular momentum is conserved ONLY when the net external torque is zero. Torque is the turning effect of force, not just force itself. And yes, the CM can indeed be outside the physical boundaries of an object, like for a hollow ring or a boomerang. Always pay attention to directions with cross products!

Solved Problem: Rolling Down an Incline

  • Problem: A solid sphere rolls down an incline without slipping. Find its acceleration.
  • Forces: mg sinθ (down incline), friction (up incline).
  • Equations: mg sinθ - f = Ma (linear), fR = Iα (rotational).
  • For sphere: I = (2/5)MR², and a = Rα.
  • Solution: a = (5/7)g sinθ.

🎙️ This is a classic exam problem. We apply both Newton's second law for linear motion and its rotational equivalent for the sphere. The crucial part is relating linear acceleration 'a' to angular acceleration 'α' through the pure rolling condition. By solving these simultaneous equations, we arrive at the acceleration. Note that a hollow sphere would have a different acceleration due to different 'I'.

Chapter Recap: Key Takeaways

  • Centre of Mass: Representative point for translational motion.
  • Torque: Rotational effect of force (τ = r x F).
  • Angular Momentum: Rotational inertia in motion (L = Iω).
  • Conservation of L: If τ_ext = 0, L is constant.
  • Moment of Inertia: Rotational equivalent of mass (I = ∑mr²).
  • Rolling Motion: Combination of translation and rotation (v_CM = Rω).

🎙️ We've covered a lot today! Remember, the Centre of Mass helps us simplify complex motions. Torque is what causes rotation, and Angular Momentum is a crucial conserved quantity. Moment of Inertia tells us how mass is distributed and resists rotational changes. Finally, rolling motion elegantly combines both translational and rotational dynamics.

What is a System of Particles?

  • A collection of two or more particles.
  • Particles can be interacting with each other.
  • External forces may also act on the system.

🎙️ A 'system of particles' is simply a group of individual particles that we choose to study together. These particles can exert forces on each other, known as internal forces, and forces from outside the system, called external forces. Understanding this distinction is key to applying conservation laws effectively.

Centre of Mass (COM): The Balancing Point

  • A hypothetical point representing the average position of the mass.
  • Behaves as if the entire mass of the system is concentrated there.
  • Crucial for analyzing the motion of a system.

🎙️ The Centre of Mass is a very important concept. Imagine trying to balance an irregularly shaped object on your finger; the point where it balances perfectly is its centre of mass. For a system, the COM's motion describes the overall motion of the system, even if individual particles move complexly.

COM for Two Particles

  • For two particles m1 and m2 at positions r1 and r2.
  • COM position: R = (m1r1 + m2r2) / (m1 + m2).
  • Lies on the line joining the two particles.

🎙️ When dealing with just two particles, the formula for the centre of mass is straightforward. Remember, the COM is closer to the heavier mass. This formula is fundamental and often appears in introductory problems, so make sure you understand its application.

COM for N Particles

  • Generalizes the two-particle formula.
  • R_COM = (Σmiri) / (Σmi).
  • Can be applied in 2D or 3D coordinates (x, y, z).

🎙️ For a system with 'N' particles, we simply extend the sum. Each particle's mass is multiplied by its position vector, and these products are summed up, then divided by the total mass of the system. This general formula is powerful for any discrete system of particles.

COM for Continuous Bodies

  • For objects with continuous mass distribution (e.g., rod, disc).
  • Requires integration: R_COM = (∫r dm) / (∫dm).
  • Symmetry often simplifies calculations.

🎙️ When the mass is spread continuously, like in a solid rod or a sphere, we use integration instead of summation. If the body has a uniform density and a symmetrical shape, its centre of mass often coincides with its geometric centre. This can save a lot of calculation time in exams.

Motion of Centre of Mass

  • Velocity of COM: V_COM = (Σmivi) / (Σmi).
  • Acceleration of COM: A_COM = (Σmiai) / (Σmi).
  • F_external = M_total * A_COM (Newton's Second Law for a system).

🎙️ The most important aspect of COM is its motion. The total external force acting on a system is equal to the total mass of the system multiplied by the acceleration of its centre of mass. This means internal forces do not affect the motion of the COM, only external forces do. This principle is extremely useful in problem-solving.

Worked Example 1: COM Calculation

  • Three particles of masses 1kg, 2kg, and 3kg are placed at (0,0), (1,0), and (0,1) respectively.
  • Find the coordinates of the centre of mass.
  • Solution: X_COM = (1*0 + 2*1 + 3*0)/(1+2+3) = 2/6 = 1/3 m.
  • Y_COM = (1*0 + 2*0 + 3*1)/(1+2+3) = 3/6 = 1/2 m. So, COM is (1/3, 1/2).

🎙️ Let's apply the formula for N particles to a concrete example. We simply plug in the mass and coordinate values for each particle into the X and Y COM formulas separately. Always remember to divide by the total mass. This is a common type of question in exams.

Rigid Body: The Ideal Model

  • An ideal body that does not deform under external forces.
  • Distance between any two particles remains constant.
  • Simplifies analysis of rotational motion.

🎙️ In reality, no body is perfectly rigid, but this concept is extremely useful for simplifying complex problems. For a rigid body, the internal forces ensure that its shape and size do not change. This allows us to focus purely on its translational and rotational motion.

Translational vs. Rotational Motion

  • Translational: All particles move along parallel paths.
  • Rotational: Particles move in circles about a fixed axis.
  • Often, real-world motion is a combination (e.g., rolling).

🎙️ It's vital to distinguish between these two fundamental types of motion. In pure translation, every point on the body has the same velocity. In pure rotation, every point has the same angular velocity, but their linear velocities depend on their distance from the axis of rotation. Many objects exhibit a combination of both.

Rotational Variables: Describing Spin

  • Angular Displacement (θ): Angle rotated (radians).
  • Angular Velocity (ω): Rate of change of angular displacement (rad/s).
  • Angular Acceleration (α): Rate of change of angular velocity (rad/s²).

🎙️ Just as we have linear displacement, velocity, and acceleration, we have their rotational counterparts. These are vector quantities, and their direction is given by the right-hand rule. Understanding these variables is the first step to solving rotational kinematics problems.

Kinematics of Rotational Motion

  • Equations are analogous to linear kinematics.
  • ω = ω₀ + αt
  • θ = ω₀t + ½αt²
  • ω² = ω₀² + 2αθ

🎙️ These are the three fundamental equations for rotational motion with constant angular acceleration. Notice how they mirror the linear equations (v=u+at, s=ut+½at², v²=u²+2as). By substituting linear variables with their rotational equivalents, you can solve a wide range of problems efficiently.

2θ = ω₀t + ½αt²
3ω² = ω₀² + 2αθ

Torque: The Rotational Force

  • The rotational analogue of force.
  • Causes or tends to cause rotational motion.
  • τ = r × F = rFsinθ (vector cross product).

🎙️ Just as force causes linear acceleration, torque causes angular acceleration. It's the 'turning effect' of a force. The magnitude of torque depends not only on the force applied but also on the distance from the axis of rotation (lever arm) and the angle at which the force is applied. Max torque occurs when force is perpendicular to the lever arm.

Moment of Inertia (I): Rotational Mass

  • Rotational analogue of mass.
  • Measures resistance to change in rotational motion.
  • I = Σmiri² for discrete particles; I = ∫r²dm for continuous bodies.

🎙️ Moment of Inertia is a crucial concept. It tells us how difficult it is to get an object to rotate or to stop it from rotating. Unlike mass, Moment of Inertia depends not only on the mass but also on how that mass is distributed relative to the axis of rotation. A heavier object or one with mass further from the axis will have a larger moment of inertia.

Radius of Gyration (k)

  • A hypothetical distance from the axis of rotation.
  • If all mass were concentrated at 'k', it would have the same Moment of Inertia.
  • I = Mk², where M is the total mass.

🎙️ The radius of gyration is a convenient way to express the distribution of mass. It's not a physical radius but a mathematical construct that simplifies comparing the rotational inertia of different bodies. It's a scalar quantity and its unit is meters.

Theorems of Parallel and Perpendicular Axes

  • Parallel Axis Theorem: I = I_COM + Md² (for axis parallel to COM axis).
  • Perpendicular Axis Theorem: Iz = Ix + Iy (for planar bodies, axes in plane).

🎙️ These two theorems are incredibly powerful for calculating Moment of Inertia for various axes without complex integration. The Parallel Axis Theorem is used when shifting the axis of rotation, while the Perpendicular Axis Theorem is specific to planar objects and axes lying within its plane. Master these for exam success.

Worked Example 2: Moment of Inertia

  • A thin rod of mass M and length L. Find its MI about an axis perpendicular to the rod passing through one end.
  • Solution: MI about COM is ML²/12. Using Parallel Axis Theorem, I = I_COM + Md².
  • Here d = L/2. So, I = ML²/12 + M(L/2)² = ML²/12 + ML²/4 = ML²/12 + 3ML²/12 = 4ML²/12 = ML²/3.

🎙️ This example demonstrates the application of the Parallel Axis Theorem. Always first identify the Moment of Inertia about the centre of mass, then use the theorem to find it about any parallel axis. This method is much faster than direct integration for many standard shapes.

Angular Momentum (L): Rotational Inertia in Motion

  • Rotational analogue of linear momentum (p = mv).
  • For a particle: L = r × p = r × (mv).
  • For a rigid body: L = Iω.

🎙️ Angular momentum is a measure of the 'quantity of rotation' an object possesses. It's a vector quantity. For a rigid body rotating about a fixed axis, it's simply the product of its Moment of Inertia and its angular velocity. This concept is fundamental to understanding the stability of rotating objects.

Conservation of Angular Momentum

  • If no external torque acts on a system, its total angular momentum remains constant.
  • I₁ω₁ = I₂ω₂.
  • Implies L_initial = L_final.

🎙️ This is a powerful conservation law, similar to the conservation of linear momentum. If the net external torque on a system is zero, its angular momentum is conserved. This means if the Moment of Inertia changes, the angular velocity must change proportionally to keep the product constant. This principle explains many phenomena.

Real-Life Conservation of Angular Momentum

  • An ice skater pulling her arms in to spin faster.
  • A diver tucking their body to increase rotational speed.
  • The Earth's rotation speed slightly changing due to mass redistribution.

🎙️ The ice skater example is classic. When she pulls her arms in, her Moment of Inertia decreases. To conserve angular momentum, her angular velocity (spin speed) must increase dramatically. Similarly, a diver changes their body shape to control their rotation during a dive. These are fantastic demonstrations of this principle.

Rolling Motion: Translation + Rotation

  • A combination of translational and rotational motion.
  • Point of contact with surface is instantaneously at rest (pure rolling).
  • No slipping occurs at the point of contact.

🎙️ Rolling motion is a very common type of motion, like a wheel rolling on the ground. In pure rolling, the point of contact between the rolling body and the surface has zero velocity. This condition is crucial for solving problems related to rolling motion and understanding its dynamics.

Kinetic Energy of a Rolling Body

  • Total KE = Translational KE + Rotational KE.
  • KE_total = ½MV_COM² + ½I_COMω².
  • Can also be expressed in terms of radius of gyration: KE_total = ½MV_COM²(1 + k²/R²).

🎙️ Since rolling motion is a combination of translation and rotation, its total kinetic energy is the sum of the kinetic energy due to the translation of its centre of mass and the kinetic energy due to its rotation about its centre of mass. The second form, using the radius of gyration, is particularly useful for comparing rolling bodies.

Recap: Key Takeaways

  • Centre of Mass: average position of mass, describes system's translation.
  • Torque: causes rotational acceleration (τ = Iα).
  • Moment of Inertia: rotational resistance, depends on mass distribution.
  • Angular Momentum: conserved in absence of external torque (L = Iω).
  • Rolling Motion: combination of translation and rotation.

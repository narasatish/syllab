Centre of Mass: The Balance Point

  • It's the point where the entire mass of a system is imagined to be concentrated.
  • The CoM moves as if all external forces act on it.
  • For a system of 'n' particles: R_CoM = (Σmᵢrᵢ) / Σmᵢ

🎙️ The Centre of Mass is a crucial concept. It simplifies the analysis of complex systems by treating them as a single point mass. The formula shown helps us locate this unique point for any collection of particles, making calculations much easier.

Example: CoM of Two Masses

  • Problem: Find CoM of 2kg at (0,0) and 3kg at (4,0).
  • Step 1: Identify masses and positions.
  • Step 2: Apply CoM formula: X_CoM = (m1x1 + m2x2) / (m1 + m2).
  • Step 3: Calculate: X_CoM = (2*0 + 3*4) / (2+3) = 12/5 = 2.4 m.

🎙️ Let's apply the CoM formula to a simple case. Here, we have two masses on the x-axis. Notice how the CoM lies closer to the heavier mass, which makes intuitive sense. Always remember to clearly list your given values and use the correct formula.

CoM for Continuous Bodies

  • For objects with distributed mass (e.g., rod, disc).
  • Calculated using integration: R_CoM = (∫r dm) / ∫dm.
  • Often found using symmetry for uniform objects.

🎙️ When dealing with solid objects, we can't just sum discrete particles. We use integration to find the CoM, considering tiny mass elements. However, for uniform objects with symmetry, like a uniform rod or a sphere, the CoM is simply at its geometric center.

Motion of the Centre of Mass

  • Velocity of CoM: V_CoM = (Σmᵢvᵢ) / Σmᵢ.
  • Acceleration of CoM: A_CoM = (Σmᵢaᵢ) / Σmᵢ.
  • If no external force, V_CoM is constant.

🎙️ Just like individual particles, the Centre of Mass also has its own velocity and acceleration. An important point to remember is that if there are no external forces acting on the system, the velocity of the CoM remains constant, even if internal forces are at play.

Newton's Second Law for Systems

  • Total external force = Total mass × acceleration of CoM.
  • F_ext = M * a_CoM.
  • Internal forces do not affect the motion of the CoM.

🎙️ This is a powerful extension of Newton's second law. It tells us that the entire system behaves as if all its mass is concentrated at the CoM, and all external forces act on it. Internal forces, like those between particles within the system, cancel out and don't change the CoM's motion.

Describing Rotation: Angular Variables

  • Angular Displacement (θ): Angle rotated (radians).
  • Angular Velocity (ω): Rate of change of angular displacement (rad/s).
  • Angular Acceleration (α): Rate of change of angular velocity (rad/s²).

🎙️ Just as we have displacement, velocity, and acceleration for linear motion, we have analogous quantities for rotational motion. These angular variables allow us to precisely describe how fast and how quickly an object is spinning or changing its spin.

Rotational Kinematics Equations

  • ω = ω₀ + αt
  • θ = ω₀t + ½αt²
  • ω² = ω₀² + 2αθ

🎙️ These equations are the rotational counterparts to the linear kinematic equations you've already learned. They are incredibly useful for solving problems involving constant angular acceleration, allowing us to find unknown angular quantities like final velocity or displacement.

Example: Accelerating Wheel

  • Problem: A wheel starts from rest, α = 2 rad/s². Find ω after 5s and θ after 5s.
  • Step 1: Identify given (ω₀=0, α=2, t=5).
  • Step 2: Use ω = ω₀ + αt => ω = 0 + 2*5 = 10 rad/s.
  • Step 3: Use θ = ω₀t + ½αt² => θ = 0*5 + ½*2*5² = 25 radians.

🎙️ This example demonstrates how to use the rotational kinematic equations. Always start by listing what's given and what needs to be found. Then, choose the appropriate equation and substitute the values carefully to arrive at the correct answer.

Torque: The Turning Effect of Force

  • Torque (τ) is the rotational analogue of force.
  • It measures the effectiveness of a force in causing rotation.
  • τ = r × F = rFsinθ (vector cross product).
  • SI Unit: Newton-meter (Nm).

🎙️ Torque is not just a force; it's the 'twisting' or 'turning' effect of a force. It depends on three factors: the magnitude of the force, the distance from the axis of rotation (lever arm 'r'), and the angle at which the force is applied. Remember the vector cross product for its definition.

Moment of Inertia: Rotational Resistance

  • Rotational analogue of mass.
  • Measures an object's resistance to changes in its rotational motion.
  • For a single particle: I = mr².
  • For a system of particles: I = Σmᵢrᵢ².

🎙️ Just as mass resists linear acceleration, Moment of Inertia resists angular acceleration. It depends not only on the mass but also on how that mass is distributed relative to the axis of rotation. The further the mass is from the axis, the greater the moment of inertia.

Radius of Gyration: Equivalent Distance

  • It's the distance from the axis of rotation where, if all the mass of the body were concentrated, it would have the same moment of inertia.
  • I = Mk² (where M is total mass).
  • k = √(I/M) (SI Unit: meter).

🎙️ The radius of gyration is a conceptual distance that helps us compare the distribution of mass for different objects. It's a single value that characterizes how spread out an object's mass is around its axis of rotation, impacting its rotational inertia.

Parallel Axis Theorem

  • Relates moment of inertia about an axis to that about a parallel axis through CoM.
  • I = I_CoM + Md².
  • I_CoM: Moment of inertia about axis through CoM.
  • d: Perpendicular distance between the two parallel axes.

🎙️ This theorem is a powerful tool for calculating moments of inertia. If you know the moment of inertia about an axis passing through the Centre of Mass, you can easily find it for any parallel axis by simply adding the term Md². This saves a lot of complex calculations.

Perpendicular Axis Theorem

  • Applicable only for planar bodies (2D objects).
  • I_z = I_x + I_y.
  • I_x, I_y: Moments of inertia about two perpendicular axes in the plane.
  • I_z: Moment of inertia about an axis perpendicular to the plane, passing through their intersection.

🎙️ The Perpendicular Axis Theorem is another useful shortcut, specifically for flat, two-dimensional objects. It states that the moment of inertia about an axis perpendicular to the plane is the sum of the moments of inertia about two perpendicular axes lying within that plane.

  • Problem: Three 1kg masses at (0,1), (1,1), (1,0). Find I about y-axis.
  • Step 1: Identify masses and distances (x-coordinates) from y-axis.

Rotational Dynamics: τ = Iα

  • The net external torque on a rigid body is equal to the product of its moment of inertia and its angular acceleration.
  • Στ_ext = Iα.
  • This is the fundamental equation for rotational dynamics.

🎙️ This equation is the rotational equivalent of F=ma. It establishes a direct relationship between the cause of rotation (net external torque) and the effect (angular acceleration), with the moment of inertia acting as the rotational 'mass' or resistance.

Angular Momentum: Rotational Inertia in Motion

  • Rotational analogue of linear momentum (p = mv).
  • For a particle: L = r × p = r × (mv).
  • For a rigid body: L = Iω.
  • SI Unit: kg m²/s or J s.

🎙️ Angular momentum is a measure of an object's tendency to continue rotating. Just like linear momentum, it's a vector quantity. For a rigid body, it's directly proportional to its moment of inertia and angular velocity.

Conservation of Angular Momentum

  • If no external torque acts on a system, its total angular momentum remains constant.
  • L_initial = L_final (if Στ_ext = 0).
  • Example: Ice skater pulling arms in (I decreases, ω increases).

🎙️ This is a fundamental conservation law in physics. When an ice skater pulls their arms in, their moment of inertia decreases. To conserve angular momentum, their angular velocity must increase, causing them to spin faster. This principle has wide applications, from planetary motion to atomic physics.

Example: Spinning Platform

  • Problem: Person on rotating platform (I=100, ω=1). Jumps off, platform I reduces to 80. Find new ω.
  • Step 1: Initial angular momentum L1 = I1ω1 = 100*1 = 100.
  • Step 2: Final angular momentum L2 = I2ω2 = 80*ω2.
  • Step 3: L1 = L2 => 100 = 80ω2 => ω2 = 100/80 = 1.25 rad/s.

🎙️ This example illustrates the conservation of angular momentum. When the moment of inertia of the system changes (due to the person jumping off, reducing the platform's effective inertia), the angular velocity must adjust to keep the total angular momentum constant.

Common Mistakes to Avoid!

  • Confusing CoM with geometric center (only same for uniform, symmetric).
  • Incorrectly applying 'r' in torque (must be perpendicular distance).
  • Using linear equations for rotational motion without conversion.
  • Forgetting units or using incorrect units (e.g., rad vs. degrees).

🎙️ Students often trip up on these points. Always remember that CoM is not always the geometric center. For torque, the perpendicular distance is crucial. Also, be mindful of using the correct rotational equations and units. Practice helps in avoiding these pitfalls.

Chapter Recap: Key Takeaways!

  • Centre of Mass simplifies system motion.
  • Rotational kinematics describes spinning motion.
  • Torque causes rotation; Moment of Inertia resists it.
  • Angular Momentum is conserved in absence of external torque.

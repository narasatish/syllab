What are Fluids?

  • Substances that can flow.
  • Include Liquids and Gases.
  • Cannot sustain shear stress.

🎙️ Unlike solids, fluids do not have a fixed shape and can deform continuously under applied shear stress. This fundamental property dictates their unique mechanical behavior, which we will study in detail.

Pressure Variation with Depth

  • Formula: P = P₀ + ρgh.
  • Pressure increases with depth.
  • Independent of container shape.

🎙️ This formula is vital for understanding hydrostatic pressure. P₀ is the surface pressure, ρ is fluid density, g is acceleration due to gravity, and h is depth. Remember, pressure at the same horizontal level in a static fluid is the same.

Pascal's Law

  • Pressure applied to an enclosed fluid.
  • Transmitted undiminished to every portion.
  • And to the walls of the container.

🎙️ This principle is the backbone of many hydraulic systems. It implies that if you push on one part of a confined fluid, that pressure is felt equally throughout the fluid.

Application: Hydraulic Lift

🎙️ The hydraulic lift demonstrates Pascal's law beautifully. It allows us to lift heavy objects with relatively small effort, making it indispensable in industries and workshops. Remember the force multiplication factor.

Archimedes' Principle

  • Object partially or fully immersed in fluid.
  • Experiences an upward buoyant force.
  • Equal to the weight of fluid displaced.

🎙️ This principle explains why objects float or sink. The buoyant force, F_b = ρ_fluid * V_displaced * g, acts opposite to gravity. It's crucial for naval architecture and many other fields.

Floating and Sinking

  • If ρ_object < ρ_fluid, object floats.
  • If ρ_object > ρ_fluid, object sinks.
  • If ρ_object = ρ_fluid, object is suspended.

🎙️ The relative densities of the object and the fluid determine whether an object floats, sinks, or remains suspended. This is a direct consequence of Archimedes' principle and the balance between buoyant force and gravitational force.

Worked Example: Buoyancy

  • A block of wood (density 700 kg/m³) floats in water (1000 kg/m³).
  • Find the fraction of volume submerged.
  • Solution: V_sub / V_total = ρ_object / ρ_fluid = 0.7.

🎙️ For floating objects, the weight of the object equals the buoyant force. This means (V_object * ρ_object * g) = (V_submerged * ρ_fluid * g). Solving this gives the submerged fraction.

Fluid Flow: Streamline vs. Turbulent

  • Streamline (Laminar): Smooth, orderly, parallel layers.
  • Turbulent: Irregular, chaotic, eddies.
  • Reynolds number predicts flow type.

🎙️ Understanding the nature of fluid flow is critical. Streamline flow is ideal for many theoretical derivations, while turbulent flow is more common in high-speed scenarios and much harder to analyze.

Equation of Continuity

  • For an incompressible, non-viscous fluid in steady flow.
  • A₁v₁ = A₂v₂ = constant (Volume flow rate).
  • Area x Velocity = Constant.

🎙️ This equation is a statement of conservation of mass for fluids. It tells us that as the cross-sectional area of a pipe decreases, the fluid velocity must increase to maintain a constant volume flow rate.

Viscosity: Fluid Friction

  • Resistance to flow.
  • Caused by cohesive forces or molecular collisions.

🎙️ Viscosity is the 'thickness' or 'stickiness' of a fluid. Honey is highly viscous, while water is less so. It plays a crucial role in lubrication and pipe flow.

Coefficient of Viscosity

  • η (eta) - a measure of fluid's resistance to shear flow.
  • F = ηA(dv/dy) (Newton's Law of Viscosity).
  • SI Unit: Poiseuille (Pl) or N s/m².

🎙️ This coefficient quantifies viscosity. The velocity gradient (dv/dy) is the rate of change of velocity with distance perpendicular to the flow. Higher η means greater resistance to flow.

Stokes' Law & Terminal Velocity

  • Stokes' Law: F_viscous = 6πηrv.
  • For small spherical body moving through viscous fluid.
  • Terminal velocity: constant velocity when net force is zero.

🎙️ Stokes' law describes the viscous drag force on a sphere. When this drag force balances gravity and buoyancy, the object reaches a constant terminal velocity, seen in raindrops or falling dust particles.

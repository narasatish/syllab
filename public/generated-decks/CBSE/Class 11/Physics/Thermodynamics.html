Types of Systems: How They Interact with Surroundings

  • **Open System:** Exchanges both matter and energy (e.g., boiling water in an open pot).
  • **Closed System:** Exchanges energy but NOT matter (e.g., boiling water in a closed pot).
  • **Isolated System:** Exchanges NEITHER matter nor energy (e.g., a perfect thermos flask).

🎙️ These distinctions are crucial. An open system is like a person eating and exercising; a closed system is like a battery powering a device; and a truly isolated system is theoretical, but a thermos flask is a good approximation.

Describing a System: Thermodynamic State Variables

  • Just like you describe a person by height and weight, we describe a gas by P, V, T.
  • These are macroscopic properties that define the state of the system.
  • Pressure (P), Volume (V), Temperature (T), and Internal Energy (U) are common examples.

🎙️ These variables are like coordinates that pinpoint the exact 'state' of your system. If any of these change, the system's state changes. They are path-independent, meaning their value depends only on the current state, not how it got there.

Intensive vs. Extensive Variables: Size Matters?

  • **Intensive Variables:** Independent of the system's size (e.g., Temperature, Pressure, Density).
  • **Extensive Variables:** Depend on the system's size (e.g., Volume, Mass, Internal Energy).
  • Think of a cup of chai vs. a pot of chai: temperature is the same, volume is different.

🎙️ This distinction is important for understanding how properties scale. If you cut a system in half, intensive properties remain the same, while extensive properties are halved.

Thermodynamic Equilibrium: A State of Balance

  • Imagine perfectly balanced scales: no net change happening.
  • A system is in equilibrium when its state variables (P, V, T) are constant.
  • No spontaneous changes occur within the system or between system and surroundings.

🎙️ Equilibrium is a state of perfect balance. If a system is not in equilibrium, it will spontaneously evolve until it reaches one. We often study systems at or near equilibrium.

Types of Equilibrium: A Deeper Balance

  • **Thermal Equilibrium:** Temperature is uniform throughout the system and with surroundings.
  • **Mechanical Equilibrium:** No unbalanced forces; pressure is uniform.
  • **Chemical Equilibrium:** No net chemical reactions occurring; composition is constant.

🎙️ For a system to be in complete thermodynamic equilibrium, all three types of equilibrium must be satisfied simultaneously. This ensures a stable and unchanging state.

The Zeroth Law of Thermodynamics: Defining Temperature

  • If body A is in thermal equilibrium with body C,
  • And body B is also in thermal equilibrium with body C,
  • Then body A and body B are in thermal equilibrium with each other.

🎙️ This law might seem obvious, but it's fundamental. It allows us to define temperature as a property that is equal when two bodies are in thermal equilibrium. It's the basis for how thermometers work!

Heat (Q): Energy Transfer Due to Temperature Difference

  • Heat is energy in transit, flowing from a hotter body to a colder body.
  • It's not a property of a system; a system 'contains' internal energy, not heat.
  • Unit: Joule (J) or calorie (cal). Sign Convention: Q > 0 (heat absorbed), Q < 0 (heat released).

🎙️ Remember, heat is a form of energy transfer, not something stored. It's like the money you transfer from one account to another. The accounts have money, but the transfer itself is the 'heat'.

🎙️ Work is another way energy is exchanged. When a gas expands and pushes a piston, it's doing work. Our sign convention for work is crucial for applying the First Law correctly.

Internal Energy (U): The Total Energy Within a System

  • This is the total energy stored within a system (kinetic + potential energy of molecules).
  • It's a state function – depends only on the system's current state, not its history.
  • For an ideal gas, U depends only on temperature (U ∝ T).

🎙️ Internal energy is like the balance in your bank account. It's a property of the account itself. Heat and work are the deposits and withdrawals that change this balance.

The First Law of Thermodynamics: Conservation of Energy

  • Energy can neither be created nor destroyed, only transformed.
  • Expressed as: ΔU = Q - W (or ΔU = Q + W_on, where W_on is work done ON the system).
  • The change in internal energy (ΔU) equals heat absorbed (Q) minus work done BY the system (W).

🎙️ This is one of the most fundamental laws in physics, a restatement of the law of conservation of energy. It tells us how internal energy changes when heat and work are exchanged.

Worked Example 2: Applying the First Law

  • A system absorbs 200 J of heat and performs 50 J of work.
  • Calculate the change in internal energy (ΔU) of the system.
  • Steps: Identify Q and W with correct signs. Apply ΔU = Q - W.

🎙️ Here, heat absorbed is positive, and work done by the system is positive. So, the internal energy increases by 150 J. Always be careful with the sign conventions!

1Q = +200 J (absorbed)
2W = +50 J (done by system)
3ΔU = Q - W
4Result: ΔU = +150 J

Key Thermodynamic Processes & Their Characteristics

  • **Isothermal:** Temperature (T) constant. ΔU = 0 for ideal gas. Q = W.
  • **Adiabatic:** No heat exchange (Q = 0). ΔU = -W.
  • **Isobaric:** Pressure (P) constant. Work W = PΔV.
  • **Isochoric:** Volume (V) constant. Work W = 0. ΔU = Q.

🎙️ Memorizing these characteristics and their implications for the First Law is vital. They form the backbone of solving numerical problems related to processes.

Second Law of Thermodynamics: Direction of Processes

  • The First Law tells us energy is conserved, but not if a process is possible.
  • The Second Law defines the direction of spontaneous processes.
  • Heat naturally flows from hot to cold; engines cannot be 100% efficient.

🎙️ This law is crucial because it explains why some things happen spontaneously and others don't. It's why your chai cools down, but never heats up on its own from the surroundings.

Heat Engines: Converting Heat to Work

  • A device that converts thermal energy into mechanical work (e.g., car engine, steam turbine).
  • Works by taking heat from a hot reservoir, doing work, and rejecting waste heat to a cold reservoir.
  • Efficiency (η) = Work Output / Heat Input = 1 - (Q_cold / Q_hot).

🎙️ Heat engines are everywhere, powering our world. The Second Law tells us that some heat must always be rejected to the cold reservoir; 100% efficiency is impossible.

Refrigerators & Heat Pumps: Moving Heat Against its Flow

  • These devices move heat from a colder body to a hotter body.
  • They require external work input to achieve this non-spontaneous transfer.
  • Coefficient of Performance (COP) = Q_cold / W (for refrigerator) or Q_hot / W (for heat pump).

🎙️ Refrigerators work opposite to heat engines. They make a cold place colder by using work to pump heat out. Heat pumps use the same principle to heat homes.

Carnot Engine & Carnot Cycle: The Ideal Engine

  • A theoretical, reversible heat engine operating between two temperatures.
  • It achieves the maximum possible efficiency for any engine operating between those temperatures.
  • Carnot Efficiency (η_carnot) = 1 - (T_cold / T_hot), where temperatures are in Kelvin.

🎙️ The Carnot engine is a benchmark. No real engine can be more efficient than a Carnot engine operating between the same two temperatures. It sets the upper limit.

Common Mistakes in Thermodynamics

  • **Sign Conventions:** Confusing positive/negative for Q and W.
  • **Units:** Inconsistent use of Joules, calories, L.atm, Kelvin vs. Celsius.
  • **State vs. Path Functions:** Misunderstanding that Q and W are path functions, U is state function.
  • **Ideal Gas Assumptions:** Applying ideal gas laws to non-ideal conditions.

🎙️ These are the most frequent pitfalls. Always double-check your sign conventions, ensure unit consistency, and remember the fundamental definitions of state and path functions.

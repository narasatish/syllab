Key Terms: System, Surroundings, Boundary

  • System: Part of the universe under study
  • Surroundings: Everything else interacting with the system
  • Boundary: Separates system from surroundings

🎙️ To understand thermodynamics, we first need to define our focus. A system is what we're observing, like a gas in a cylinder. The surroundings are everything outside the system that can exchange energy or matter with it. The boundary is the imaginary or real division between them.

Types of Systems

  • Open: Exchanges both matter and energy
  • Closed: Exchanges energy but not matter
  • Isolated: No exchange of matter or energy

🎙️ Systems can be classified based on what they exchange with their surroundings. An open system, like a boiling pot without a lid, exchanges both heat and steam. A closed system, like a sealed pressure cooker, exchanges heat but not mass. An isolated system, like an ideal thermos flask, exchanges neither.

Zeroth Law of Thermodynamics

  • If A is in thermal equilibrium with B
  • And B is in thermal equilibrium with C
  • Then A is in thermal equilibrium with C

🎙️ The Zeroth Law establishes the concept of temperature. It essentially states that if two systems are each in thermal equilibrium with a third system, they are also in thermal equilibrium with each other. This allows us to use a thermometer (the third system) to measure the temperature of other systems.

First Law of Thermodynamics

  • Principle of conservation of energy
  • Energy cannot be created or destroyed
  • It can only be transformed from one form to another

🎙️ The First Law of Thermodynamics is a restatement of the law of conservation of energy. It tells us that the total energy of an isolated system remains constant. Any change in the internal energy of a system is due to the heat added to it or the work done by or on it.

Internal Energy (U)

  • Total energy contained within a system
  • Sum of kinetic and potential energies of molecules
  • Primarily depends on temperature for ideal gases

🎙️ Internal energy refers to the energy stored within a system, encompassing the kinetic energy of its molecules due to their random motion and the potential energy due to intermolecular forces. For an ideal gas, internal energy is solely a function of its temperature, meaning if temperature is constant, internal energy is constant.

Work Done (W) by/on a system

  • Energy transferred due to force acting over a distance
  • For gases, W = PΔV (area under P-V curve)
  • Sign Convention: W > 0 if work done BY system (expansion), W < 0 if work done ON system (compression)

🎙️ Work done in thermodynamics usually refers to mechanical work, especially expansion or compression work by a gas. It's the area under the P-V curve on a pressure-volume diagram. Remember the sign convention: work done *by* the system is positive, and work done *on* the system is negative according to the physics convention.

Heat (Q)

  • Energy transferred due to temperature difference
  • Flows from higher to lower temperature
  • Sign Convention: Q > 0 if heat ADDED to system, Q < 0 if heat REMOVED from system

🎙️ Heat is another form of energy transfer, driven solely by a temperature difference. It always flows spontaneously from a hotter body to a colder body. Like work, heat has a specific sign convention: heat absorbed by the system is positive, and heat released by the system is negative.

First Law Equation: ΔU = Q - W

  • ΔU = Change in internal energy
  • Q = Heat supplied to the system
  • W = Work done BY the system

🎙️ This is the mathematical statement of the First Law. It says that the change in a system's internal energy equals the heat added to it minus the work done by it. Be extremely careful with the sign conventions for Q and W, as they are crucial for correct calculations.

Worked Example 1: Applying First Law

  • A gas absorbs 200 J of heat and does 50 J of work.
  • Calculate the change in internal energy.

🎙️ Let's apply the First Law. Given Q = +200 J (absorbed) and W = +50 J (done by system). Using ΔU = Q - W, we get ΔU = 200 J - 50 J = 150 J. The internal energy of the gas increases by 150 J.

Common Mistakes: Sign Conventions

  • Confusing work done BY vs. ON the system
  • Confusing heat ADDED vs. REMOVED
  • Using chemistry convention (ΔU = Q + W) in physics context

🎙️ This is where most students lose marks. Always remember: in physics, work done *by* the system is positive. If the problem states 'work done *on* the system', you must use W = - (value). Similarly for heat: absorbed is positive, released is negative. Stick to ΔU = Q - W for physics.

Isothermal Process (T = constant)

  • Temperature remains constant (ΔT = 0)
  • For ideal gas, ΔU = 0
  • Q = W (Heat supplied equals work done)

🎙️ In an isothermal process, the system is kept at a constant temperature. This implies that for an ideal gas, its internal energy remains unchanged (ΔU=0). Therefore, any heat added to the system is entirely converted into work done by the system, or vice versa.

🧊

Adiabatic Process (Q = 0)

  • No heat exchange with surroundings (Q = 0)
  • System is thermally insulated
  • ΔU = -W (Change in internal energy equals negative of work done)

🎙️ An adiabatic process occurs without any heat transfer between the system and its surroundings. This happens either very rapidly or in a perfectly insulated system. In such a process, any work done by the system comes directly from its internal energy, causing its temperature to drop, and vice-versa.

Isobaric Process (P = constant)

  • Pressure remains constant (ΔP = 0)
  • Work done W = PΔV
  • Heat exchanged Q = ΔU + PΔV

🎙️ An isobaric process is one where the pressure of the system remains constant throughout the process. A common example is heating a gas in a cylinder with a movable piston under constant atmospheric pressure. The work done is simply P times the change in volume.

🎈
Constant pressure
18 / 25

Isochoric Process (V = constant)

  • Volume remains constant (ΔV = 0)
  • No work done (W = PΔV = 0)
  • Q = ΔU (Heat supplied equals change in internal energy)

🎙️ In an isochoric process, the volume of the system does not change. This means no work is done *by* or *on* the system. Consequently, according to the First Law, all the heat supplied to the system goes directly into changing its internal energy, which usually means increasing its temperature.

Second Law of Thermodynamics

  • Heat flows spontaneously from hot to cold
  • Not all heat can be converted into work
  • Introduces concept of entropy

🎙️ The First Law tells us about energy conservation, but not about the direction of processes. The Second Law addresses this, stating that processes tend to occur in a certain direction, like heat flowing from hot to cold. It also implies that perfect conversion of heat into work is impossible, introducing the concept of efficiency.

Heat Engines

  • Device converting heat into mechanical work
  • Operates in a cycle
  • Efficiency = (Work Output / Heat Input)

🎙️ A heat engine is a device that takes heat from a high-temperature reservoir, converts some of it into mechanical work, and rejects the remaining heat to a low-temperature reservoir. Examples include car engines and power plants. Their efficiency is always less than 100% due to the Second Law.

Refrigerators & Heat Pumps

  • Devices that transfer heat from cold to hot
  • Require external work input
  • Operate opposite to heat engines

🎙️ Refrigerators and heat pumps are essentially heat engines operating in reverse. They use external work to transfer heat from a colder region to a hotter region, which is a non-spontaneous process. A refrigerator cools a space, while a heat pump heats a space by extracting heat from a colder source.

Third Law of Thermodynamics

  • Entropy of a perfect crystal is zero at absolute zero temperature (0 K)
  • It is impossible to reach absolute zero in a finite number of steps
  • Deals with behavior near absolute zero

🎙️ The Third Law of Thermodynamics deals with the behavior of systems as they approach absolute zero temperature. It states that the entropy of a perfectly crystalline substance approaches zero as its temperature approaches absolute zero. This law has significant implications for low-temperature physics and cryogenics.

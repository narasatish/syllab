  • Physics is an experimental science.
  • Observations require quantitative descriptions.
  • To compare, predict, and verify theories.

Physical Quantities: What We Measure

  • Quantities that can be measured directly or indirectly.
  • Expressed as a number and a unit.
  • Examples: Length, Mass, Time, Force.

🎙️ A physical quantity is anything in the physical world that we can measure. It always has two parts: a numerical value and a unit. For instance, '5 meters' means a length of '5' units, where the unit is 'meter'.

Derived Quantities

  • Quantities expressed as a combination of fundamental quantities.
  • Their units are derived from base units.
  • Examples: Speed (Length/Time), Force (Mass × Length/Time²).

🎙️ Derived quantities are dependent on fundamental quantities. For example, speed is derived from length and time. Understanding how they are derived is key to understanding their units and dimensions, which is crucial for exam problems.

Systems of Units: A Historical Overview

  • CGS System: Centimetre, Gram, Second.
  • FPS System: Foot, Pound, Second (British system).
  • MKS System: Metre, Kilogram, Second.

🎙️ Historically, different systems of units emerged. The CGS system was common in scientific work, while FPS was used in engineering. The MKS system was an improvement, leading to the development of the modern SI system. Be aware of these older systems as you might encounter them.

The International System of Units (SI)

  • Modern form of the MKS system.
  • Globally accepted for scientific and commercial use.

🎙️ The SI system is the standard we use today. It's a coherent system designed for global consistency, making scientific data and trade easily understandable across borders. All your calculations and answers in exams should primarily be in SI units unless specified otherwise.

SI Base Unit: Length

  • Unit: Meter (m).
  • Original definition: 1/10,000,000 of distance from equator to North Pole.
  • Current definition: Distance light travels in vacuum in 1/299,792,458 second.

🎙️ The meter is the base unit for length. Its definition has evolved over time to become more precise and reproducible using fundamental constants of nature. Remember its symbol 'm' and its modern definition for competitive exams.

SI Base Unit: Mass

  • Unit: Kilogram (kg).
  • Original definition: Mass of a specific platinum-iridium cylinder.
  • Current definition: Defined by Planck's constant (h).

🎙️ The kilogram is the base unit for mass. Like the meter, its definition has recently been updated to be based on a fundamental constant, making it more stable and less prone to physical changes. This is a key update to be aware of.

SI Base Unit: Time

  • Unit: Second (s).
  • Definition: Duration of 9,192,631,770 periods of radiation from Cesium-133 atom.
  • Crucial for measuring rates of change.

🎙️ The second is defined with extreme precision using atomic clocks, ensuring its universality. Time is a fundamental aspect of all physical processes, and its accurate measurement is vital in physics.

SI Base Unit: Electric Current

  • Unit: Ampere (A).
  • Definition: Constant current that produces a force of 2×10⁻⁷ N/m between two parallel conductors.
  • Measures the flow of electric charge.

🎙️ The ampere is defined based on the magnetic force between two current-carrying wires. It's a fundamental unit, and you'll use it extensively when studying electricity and magnetism. Remember the 'A' symbol.

SI Base Unit: Thermodynamic Temperature

  • Unit: Kelvin (K).
  • Definition: 1/273.16 of the thermodynamic temperature of the triple point of water.
  • Measures the average kinetic energy of particles.

🎙️ Kelvin is the SI unit for temperature, representing absolute temperature. While Celsius is commonly used, Kelvin is essential for many physics formulas, especially those involving gases and thermodynamics. Note that it's 'Kelvin', not 'degrees Kelvin'.

Prefixes for SI Units

  • Used to express very large or very small quantities.
  • Powers of 10 applied to base units.
  • Examples: kilo (10³), milli (10⁻³), micro (10⁻⁶).

🎙️ Prefixes are incredibly useful for simplifying numbers. Instead of writing 0.000001 meters, we write 1 micrometer. Memorize common prefixes and their corresponding powers of 10, as they are frequently tested in conversions.

Worked Example: Unit Conversion

  • Convert 72 km/h to m/s.
  • 1 km = 1000 m, 1 h = 3600 s.
  • 72 km/h = 72 * (1000 m / 3600 s) = 20 m/s.

🎙️ Unit conversion is a fundamental skill. Always set up your conversion factors carefully, ensuring that the units you want to cancel out are in the numerator and denominator. Practice this regularly, as it appears in almost every physics problem.

Common Mistakes in Unit Conversion

  • Incorrect conversion factors (e.g., 1 min = 100 s).
  • Forgetting to convert all parts of a derived unit (e.g., area, volume).
  • Errors with powers of 10 for prefixes.

🎙️ Many students lose marks due to simple conversion errors. Double-check your conversion factors. Remember that if you convert an area, you square the length conversion factor (e.g., 1 m² = (100 cm)² = 10⁴ cm²). Be meticulous with prefixes like kilo, milli, micro.

Accuracy and Precision

  • Accuracy: How close a measurement is to the true value.
  • Precision: How close repeated measurements are to each other.
  • High precision doesn't guarantee high accuracy.

🎙️ These two terms are often confused. Imagine hitting a dartboard: accuracy is hitting the bullseye, while precision is hitting the same spot repeatedly, even if it's not the bullseye. For good experimental results, we strive for both.

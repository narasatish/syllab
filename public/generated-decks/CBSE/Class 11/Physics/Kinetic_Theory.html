Introduction to Kinetic Theory of Gases (KTG)

  • A theoretical model explaining gas properties.
  • Connects microscopic behaviour (molecules) to macroscopic properties (pressure, temperature).
  • Based on a set of fundamental assumptions about gas molecules.

🎙️ The Kinetic Theory of Gases is a powerful model. It allows us to understand why gases exert pressure, why they expand when heated, and how their internal energy is distributed, all by considering the motion of their constituent particles.

Assumptions of KTG: The Ideal Gas Model (Part 1)

  • Gases consist of a large number of identical molecules.
  • Molecules are point masses; their actual volume is negligible compared to container volume.
  • Molecules are in continuous, random, and rapid motion.

🎙️ These assumptions define an 'ideal gas'. Real gases behave like ideal gases at high temperatures and low pressures. The first assumption simplifies our model by treating molecules as tiny, identical spheres.

Assumptions of KTG: The Ideal Gas Model (Part 2)

  • Molecules exert no forces on each other, except during collisions.
  • Collisions between molecules and with container walls are perfectly elastic.
  • The time duration of a collision is negligible compared to the time between collisions.

🎙️ The absence of intermolecular forces means no potential energy between molecules, simplifying energy calculations. Perfectly elastic collisions imply conservation of kinetic energy and momentum, which is crucial for pressure derivation.

Assumptions of KTG: The Ideal Gas Model (Part 3)

  • Newton's laws of motion apply to gas molecules.
  • The effect of gravity on molecular motion is negligible.
  • The density of molecules is uniform throughout the container.

🎙️ While gravity acts on molecules, their high speeds mean its effect is minimal over short distances. Uniform density ensures that the gas properties are consistent throughout the container, making calculations simpler.

Pressure Exerted by a Gas

  • Why does gas in a cylinder push against its walls?
  • It's due to the continuous bombardment of molecules on the container walls.
  • Each collision imparts a small impulse to the wall.

🎙️ Imagine countless tiny billiard balls hitting a wall. Each hit creates a tiny force. When billions of gas molecules do this every second, the cumulative effect is a measurable, continuous force over the area, which we define as pressure.

From Collisions to Pressure: The Idea

  • Consider a single molecule colliding elastically with a wall.
  • Its momentum perpendicular to the wall changes.
  • This change in momentum is imparted to the wall.

🎙️ If a molecule hits a wall and bounces back, its velocity component perpendicular to the wall reverses. This change in momentum, by Newton's second law, means a force was exerted on the wall for the duration of the collision.

Derivation Insight: Pressure Formula

  • Pressure (P) is related to the number of molecules (N), mass (m), and average squared speed (<v²>).
  • P = (1/3) (N/V) m <v²>
  • Alternatively, P = (1/3) ρ <v²>, where ρ is the gas density.

🎙️ While a full derivation is involved, understanding this final formula is key. It shows that pressure depends on how many molecules are present, their individual mass, and crucially, how fast they are moving on average. Higher speed means more forceful and frequent collisions.

Root Mean Square (RMS) Speed

  • Gas molecules move at varying speeds.
  • RMS speed (v_rms) is a statistical measure of average speed.
  • v_rms = √<v²> = √(3RT/M) = √(3kT/m).

🎙️ Since not all molecules move at the same speed, we use the RMS speed. It's not a simple average, but the square root of the average of the squares of the speeds. It's a key parameter in KTG, directly related to temperature.

💨
Average Molecular Speed

Kinetic Interpretation of Temperature

  • Temperature is NOT just a measure of hotness or coldness.
  • It's a direct measure of the average translational kinetic energy of gas molecules.
  • Average K.E. per molecule = (3/2) kT.

🎙️ This is a profound insight from KTG! When we heat a gas, we're essentially increasing the average speed and thus the average translational kinetic energy of its molecules. Absolute zero temperature implies zero average kinetic energy.

Degrees of Freedom (f)

  • The number of independent ways a molecule can possess energy.
  • These ways can be translational, rotational, or vibrational.
  • Each degree of freedom contributes to the internal energy of the gas.

🎙️ Think of it as the number of coordinates needed to fully describe the motion and orientation of a molecule. A molecule can move in X, Y, Z directions (translational), rotate about axes (rotational), and even vibrate (vibrational).

↕️
Motion Possibilities
18 / 32

Degrees of Freedom: Monatomic Gas

  • Examples: Helium (He), Neon (Ne), Argon (Ar).
  • These are single atoms, treated as point masses.
  • They only have translational motion along X, Y, and Z axes.
  • Degrees of Freedom (f) = 3 (all translational).

🎙️ For a monatomic gas, like an atom of Helium, there's no rotation or vibration possible as it's just a single point. So, all its energy is associated with its movement in three dimensions.

Degrees of Freedom: Diatomic Gas

  • Examples: Oxygen (O₂), Nitrogen (N₂), Hydrogen (H₂).
  • These molecules have two atoms, like a dumbbell.
  • They have 3 translational + 2 rotational degrees of freedom.
  • Degrees of Freedom (f) = 5 (at moderate temperatures).

🎙️ A diatomic molecule can translate in 3 directions, and it can rotate about two axes perpendicular to the line joining the atoms. Rotation about the inter-atomic axis is usually neglected due to negligible moment of inertia.

Degrees of Freedom: Polyatomic Gas

  • Examples: Water (H₂O), Carbon Dioxide (CO₂), Methane (CH₄).
  • Linear polyatomic (e.g., CO₂): 3 translational + 2 rotational = 5.
  • Non-linear polyatomic (e.g., H₂O, CH₄): 3 translational + 3 rotational = 6.
  • Vibrational degrees of freedom become active at high temperatures.

🎙️ Polyatomic molecules are more complex. Linear ones are like extended dumbbells. Non-linear ones are 3D shapes. Vibrational modes, where atoms oscillate relative to each other, contribute additional degrees of freedom, especially at higher temperatures.

Law of Equipartition of Energy

  • For a system in thermal equilibrium, total energy is equally distributed.
  • Each degree of freedom has an average energy of (1/2) kT.
  • Total internal energy (U) for N molecules = N * f * (1/2) kT.

🎙️ This law is a cornerstone of KTG. It states that energy doesn't favour one type of motion over another. Each independent way a molecule can store energy gets an equal share, directly proportional to the absolute temperature.

⚖️
Energy Balance

Specific Heat Capacities of Gases

  • Molar specific heat at constant volume (Cᵥ) and constant pressure (Cₚ).
  • Cᵥ = (dU/dT)ᵥ and Cₚ = (dH/dT)ₚ.
  • Relation: Cₚ - Cᵥ = R (Mayer's relation).

🎙️ Specific heat capacities tell us how much energy is needed to raise the temperature of a substance. For gases, we distinguish between constant volume (where all heat goes into internal energy) and constant pressure (where some heat does work).

Specific Heat for Monatomic Gas

  • f = 3 (translational only).
  • Internal Energy U = N * 3 * (1/2) kT = (3/2) nRT.
  • Cᵥ = (dU/dT)ᵥ = (3/2) R.
  • Cₚ = Cᵥ + R = (5/2) R. Ratio γ = Cₚ/Cᵥ = 5/3 = 1.67.

🎙️ Using the Law of Equipartition, we can derive the specific heats. For monatomic gases, the calculations are straightforward due to only translational degrees of freedom. This gives us a theoretical value for γ.

🌡️
Monatomic Heat

Specific Heat for Diatomic Gas

  • f = 5 (3 translational + 2 rotational, at moderate T).
  • Internal Energy U = N * 5 * (1/2) kT = (5/2) nRT.
  • Cᵥ = (5/2) R.
  • Cₚ = Cᵥ + R = (7/2) R. Ratio γ = Cₚ/Cᵥ = 7/5 = 1.4.

🎙️ For diatomic gases, the two rotational degrees of freedom contribute an additional R to Cᵥ. This means more energy is required to raise the temperature by the same amount compared to a monatomic gas, as some energy goes into rotation.

Specific Heat for Polyatomic Gas

  • For non-linear polyatomic: f = 6 (3 trans + 3 rot, at moderate T).
  • Internal Energy U = N * 6 * (1/2) kT = 3nRT.
  • Cᵥ = 3R.
  • Cₚ = Cᵥ + R = 4R. Ratio γ = Cₚ/Cᵥ = 4/3 = 1.33.
  • Vibrational modes add 2 degrees of freedom (potential + kinetic) per mode at high T.

🎙️ Polyatomic gases have even more ways to store energy. The general formula for Cᵥ is (f/2)R. Remember that vibrational contributions are temperature-dependent and usually considered at higher temperatures.

Mean Free Path (λ)

  • The average distance a molecule travels between two successive collisions.
  • Depends on molecular diameter (d) and number density (n/V).
  • λ = 1 / (√2 π d² n/V).
  • At higher pressure/density, mean free path decreases.

🎙️ Molecules don't travel in straight lines indefinitely; they constantly collide. The mean free path is an important concept for understanding transport phenomena like diffusion and viscosity in gases. It tells us how 'crowded' the gas is.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Confusing average speed with RMS speed.
  • Forgetting to convert temperature to Kelvin in formulas.
  • Miscounting degrees of freedom (especially for polyatomic molecules).
  • Applying KTG assumptions to real gases at extreme conditions (low T, high P).

🎙️ These are typical pitfalls that students encounter. Always double-check your units and ensure you're using the correct formula and values for degrees of freedom. Remember KTG is for ideal gases.

🎙️ Motion isn't always linear or rotational. Many things in nature move back and forth, or repeat their path. We'll categorize these into periodic, oscillatory, and vibratory motions, which are often used interchangeably but have distinct nuances.

Periodic Motion: Repeating its Path

  • Motion that repeats itself after a fixed interval of time
  • Example: Earth's revolution around the Sun
  • Not necessarily 'to and fro' motion

🎙️ Periodic motion is the broadest category. Anything that repeats its path in a consistent time frame falls under this. Think of the hands of a clock or the orbit of a planet – they always return to their initial state after a specific duration.

Oscillatory Motion: To and Fro!

  • Special type of periodic motion
  • Object moves back and forth about a fixed equilibrium position
  • Example: Swing, simple pendulum

🎙️ Now, let's narrow it down. Oscillatory motion is a subset of periodic motion where the movement is specifically 'to and fro' around a central, stable equilibrium point. A child on a swing or a pendulum are classic examples.

Key Terms: Displacement and Amplitude

  • Displacement (x): Position from equilibrium at any instant
  • Amplitude (A): Maximum displacement from equilibrium
  • Units: meters (m)

🎙️ To describe oscillations precisely, we need specific terms. Displacement tells us where the oscillating object is relative to its rest position, while amplitude defines the maximum extent of its swing on either side. These are crucial for understanding the 'size' of the oscillation.

Key Terms: Time Period and Frequency

  • Time Period (T): Time taken for one complete oscillation (s)
  • Frequency (ν or f): Number of oscillations per second (Hz)
  • Relationship: ν = 1/T

🎙️ How fast or slow an oscillation is, is described by its time period and frequency. The time period is the duration of one full cycle, while frequency tells us how many cycles occur in one second. They are inversely related, a concept you've encountered in wave mechanics.

Key Terms: Angular Frequency (ω)

  • Related to frequency in circular motion context
  • ω = 2πν = 2π/T
  • Units: radians per second (rad/s)

🎙️ Angular frequency, denoted by omega (ω), is another way to express the rate of oscillation, particularly useful when relating SHM to uniform circular motion. It's directly proportional to frequency and inversely proportional to the time period, incorporating the 2π factor from a full circle.

Simple Harmonic Motion (SHM): The Ideal Oscillation

  • Simplest form of oscillatory motion
  • Restoring force directly proportional to displacement
  • Force always directed towards equilibrium position (F = -kx)

🎙️ Simple Harmonic Motion, or SHM, is a very important type of oscillation. It's characterized by a restoring force that always pulls the object back to its equilibrium position and is directly proportional to how far it's displaced. This linear relationship makes SHM mathematically elegant and widely applicable.

SHM is periodic motion where restoring force is proportional to displacement. Displacement

SHM Equation: Displacement x(t)

  • Position of particle at time t: x(t) = A sin(ωt + φ)
  • A: Amplitude, ω: Angular frequency
  • φ: Initial phase or phase constant

🎙️ The displacement of a particle undergoing SHM can be described by a sinusoidal function. This equation, x(t) = A sin(ωt + φ), allows us to predict the particle's position at any given time, provided we know its amplitude, angular frequency, and initial phase.

SHM: Velocity and Acceleration

  • Velocity: v(t) = Aω cos(ωt + φ)
  • Acceleration: a(t) = -Aω² sin(ωt + φ) = -ω²x
  • Velocity max at equilibrium, acceleration max at extremes

🎙️ By differentiating the displacement equation with respect to time, we can find the velocity and acceleration. Notice that acceleration is directly proportional to displacement and opposite in direction, which is consistent with the restoring force definition of SHM. This also shows where velocity and acceleration are maximum or zero.

Phase and Phase Difference

  • Phase (ωt + φ): Describes state of oscillation (position & direction)
  • Phase difference (Δφ): Difference in phases of two oscillating particles
  • Determines how 'in sync' two oscillations are

🎙️ The phase of an oscillation tells us exactly where an object is in its cycle and in which direction it's moving. When comparing two oscillations, their phase difference is crucial – it tells us if they are moving together, opposite to each other, or somewhere in between.

Energy in SHM: Kinetic Energy (KE)

  • KE = ½ mv²
  • KE = ½ m A²ω² cos²(ωt + φ)
  • Maximum at equilibrium (x=0), zero at extreme positions (x=±A)

🎙️ As the particle oscillates, its energy continuously transforms. Kinetic energy, the energy of motion, is maximum when the particle passes through its equilibrium position, where its speed is highest. It becomes zero at the extreme ends of its path, where it momentarily stops before reversing direction.

⚡
Energy of Motion

Energy in SHM: Potential Energy (PE)

  • PE = ½ kx² = ½ mω²x²
  • PE = ½ m A²ω² sin²(ωt + φ)
  • Maximum at extreme positions (x=±A), zero at equilibrium (x=0)

🎙️ Potential energy in SHM is the stored energy due to the displacement from equilibrium. It's maximum at the extreme positions, where the restoring force has done the most work to displace the particle. At the equilibrium position, where the displacement is zero, the potential energy is also zero.

Stored Energy

Energy in SHM: Total Energy (E)

  • Total Energy E = KE + PE = ½ m A²ω²
  • Total energy is constant and proportional to A²
  • Energy continuously transforms between KE and PE

🎙️ A key principle in SHM is the conservation of total mechanical energy. The sum of kinetic and potential energy remains constant throughout the oscillation, assuming no damping. This energy continuously swaps between its kinetic and potential forms, but the total amount stays fixed, proportional to the square of the amplitude.

Simple Pendulum: A Classic SHM Example

  • Point mass suspended by a massless, inextensible string
  • Oscillates under gravity for small angles
  • Restoring force component: -mg sinθ

🎙️ The simple pendulum is a textbook example of SHM, provided its oscillations are small. It consists of a bob suspended by a string, and gravity acts as the restoring force. For small angular displacements, the motion closely approximates SHM, allowing for simpler analysis.

Simple Pendulum: Time Period for Small Angles

  • For small θ, sinθ ≈ θ
  • Restoring force F ≈ -mgθ = -mg(x/L)
  • T = 2π√(L/g) (independent of mass and amplitude)

🎙️ By using the small angle approximation (sinθ ≈ θ), we can show that the restoring force becomes proportional to displacement, thus confirming SHM. The resulting formula for the time period, T = 2π√(L/g), is remarkable because it shows that the period depends only on the length of the pendulum and gravity, not on the mass of the bob or the amplitude of swing.

1Force F = -mg sinθ
2Small angle approx. sinθ ≈ θ
3F = -(mg/L)x
4Compare to F = -kx, k = mg/L
5T = 2π√(m/k) = 2π√(L/g)

Spring-Mass System: Horizontal Oscillation

  • Mass attached to a spring on a frictionless surface
  • Restoring force F = -kx (Hooke's Law)
  • Angular frequency ω = √(k/m), Period T = 2π√(m/k)

🎙️ Another classic SHM system is a mass attached to a spring. In a horizontal setup, the spring's restoring force, given by Hooke's Law, directly provides the SHM condition. The time period depends on the mass and the spring constant, highlighting their roles in determining the oscillation's speed.

Spring-Mass System: Vertical Oscillation

  • Mass hangs vertically, spring extends by ΔL due to gravity
  • Equilibrium is at new extended position
  • ω = √(k/m), Period T = 2π√(m/k) (same as horizontal)

🎙️ When a spring-mass system oscillates vertically, gravity causes an initial extension. However, the oscillation still occurs about this new equilibrium position, and surprisingly, the angular frequency and time period remain the same as in the horizontal case, determined solely by the spring constant and mass.

Worked Example 1: SHM Parameters

  • A particle executes SHM with amplitude 5 cm and frequency 2 Hz. Calculate its angular frequency, time period, and maximum velocity.
  • Solution: ω = 2πf = 4π rad/s, T = 1/f = 0.5 s
  • v_max = Aω = 0.05 m * 4π rad/s = 0.2π m/s

🎙️ Let's apply what we've learned. Here, we are given amplitude and frequency, and we need to find other key parameters. Remember to convert units to SI if necessary. The maximum velocity occurs at the equilibrium position and is a product of amplitude and angular frequency.

Worked Example 2: Pendulum Length

  • A simple pendulum has a time period of 2 s. What is its length? (Assume g = 9.8 m/s²)
  • Solution: T = 2π√(L/g) => T² = 4π²L/g
  • L = T²g / (4π²) = (2² * 9.8) / (4 * (3.14)²) ≈ 0.993 m ≈ 1 m

🎙️ This problem tests your understanding of the simple pendulum's time period formula. By rearranging the equation, we can solve for the unknown length. Notice how the calculation shows that a pendulum with a 2-second period, often called a 'seconds pendulum', has a length very close to 1 meter.

Common Mistakes in Oscillations

  • Confusing periodic with oscillatory motion
  • Incorrectly applying small angle approximation for pendulum
  • Forgetting negative sign in F = -kx or a = -ω²x
  • Unit errors (cm vs m, Hz vs rad/s)

🎙️ Physics problems often have common pitfalls. Make sure you understand the subtle differences between periodic and oscillatory motion. Always check if the small angle approximation is valid for pendulum problems, and don't forget the negative sign which indicates the restoring nature of the force. And always, always double-check your units!

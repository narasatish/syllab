Understanding Periodic Motion

  • Motion that repeats itself after a fixed time interval
  • Example: Earth revolving around the Sun
  • Example: Hands of a clock

🎙️ Periodic motion is any motion that repeats itself at regular intervals. Think about the daily rotation of the Earth or the rhythmic ticking of a clock. The key is the fixed time interval after which the motion pattern starts over.

What is Oscillatory Motion?

  • A type of periodic motion
  • Body moves back and forth about a fixed equilibrium position
  • Example: A swing, a vibrating string

🎙️ Oscillatory motion is a specific kind of periodic motion where an object moves repeatedly back and forth around a central, stable equilibrium position. All oscillatory motions are periodic, but not all periodic motions are oscillatory. This 'to and fro' nature is its defining characteristic.

Key Terms: Period and Frequency

  • Period (T): Time taken for one complete oscillation (seconds)
  • Frequency (f or ν): Number of oscillations per unit time (Hertz)
  • Relationship: f = 1/T

🎙️ Period (T) is the duration of one full cycle of oscillation, measured in seconds. Frequency (f or ν) tells us how many cycles occur in one second, measured in Hertz. These two quantities are inversely related, which is a fundamental concept for solving problems.

Key Terms: Displacement and Amplitude

  • Displacement (x): Position from equilibrium at any instant
  • Amplitude (A): Maximum displacement from equilibrium position
  • Amplitude is always positive

🎙️ Displacement is the object's position relative to its equilibrium point. Amplitude is the maximum displacement from equilibrium, indicating the 'size' of the oscillation. It's a scalar quantity and always positive, representing the peak extent of the motion.

Simple Harmonic Motion (SHM)

  • Simplest form of oscillatory motion
  • Restoring force is directly proportional to displacement
  • Force acts opposite to displacement, towards equilibrium

🎙️ Simple Harmonic Motion, or SHM, is the most fundamental type of oscillation. It occurs when the net restoring force acting on an object is directly proportional to its displacement from equilibrium and always directed towards that equilibrium position. This proportional relationship is key.

Restoring Force in SHM: Hooke's Law

  • F = -kx (Hooke's Law)
  • k is the force constant or spring constant (N/m)
  • Negative sign indicates force opposes displacement

🎙️ The restoring force in SHM is mathematically described by Hooke's Law: F = -kx. Here, 'k' is the force constant, a measure of the stiffness of the system. The crucial negative sign signifies that the force always acts to bring the object back to its equilibrium position, opposing the direction of displacement.

Differential Equation of SHM

  • From F = ma and F = -kx, we get ma = -kx
  • d²x/dt² + (k/m)x = 0
  • Let ω² = k/m, so d²x/dt² + ω²x = 0

🎙️ Combining Newton's second law (F=ma) with Hooke's Law (F=-kx) leads us to the differential equation of SHM. This equation, d²x/dt² + ω²x = 0, is the mathematical fingerprint of Simple Harmonic Motion. Here, ω is the angular frequency, which we'll discuss shortly.

Displacement Equation of SHM

  • General solution: x(t) = A sin(ωt + φ)
  • A: Amplitude
  • ω: Angular frequency (rad/s), ω = 2πf = 2π/T
  • φ: Initial phase or phase constant (radians)

🎙️ The solution to the differential equation of SHM is a sinusoidal function: x(t) = A sin(ωt + φ). This equation describes the position of the oscillating particle at any given time 't'. Understand each term: A for amplitude, ω for angular frequency, and φ for the initial phase, which tells us the starting point of the oscillation.

Velocity in SHM

  • Velocity is the time derivative of displacement: v(t) = dx/dt
  • v(t) = Aω cos(ωt + φ)
  • Maximum velocity: v_max = Aω (at equilibrium x=0)
  • Minimum velocity: v_min = -Aω

🎙️ To find the velocity of the oscillating particle, we differentiate the displacement equation with respect to time. The result is v(t) = Aω cos(ωt + φ). Notice that the velocity is maximum when the particle passes through the equilibrium position, and zero at the extreme positions where it momentarily stops.

Acceleration in SHM

  • Acceleration is the time derivative of velocity: a(t) = dv/dt
  • a(t) = -Aω² sin(ωt + φ) = -ω²x(t)
  • Maximum acceleration: a_max = Aω² (at extreme positions x=±A)
  • Minimum acceleration: a_min = -Aω²

🎙️ Differentiating velocity gives us acceleration. The acceleration in SHM is a(t) = -Aω² sin(ωt + φ), which simplifies to a = -ω²x. This directly shows that acceleration is proportional to displacement and opposite in direction, which is the defining condition of SHM. Acceleration is maximum at the extreme points and zero at equilibrium.

Phase and Phase Constant (φ)

  • Phase (ωt + φ): Describes the state of oscillation at any instant
  • Includes both displacement and direction of motion
  • Phase Constant (φ): Initial phase at t=0
  • Determined by initial conditions of motion

🎙️ The term (ωt + φ) is called the phase of the oscillation, giving us a complete picture of the particle's state. The phase constant, φ, is crucial as it determines the initial position and direction of motion at t=0. It helps distinguish between oscillations that start at different points in their cycle.

Energy in SHM: Potential Energy

  • Stored energy due to position or configuration
  • U = ½ kx² = ½ mω²x²
  • Maximum at extreme positions (x=±A)
  • Minimum (zero) at equilibrium (x=0)

🎙️ In SHM, potential energy is stored in the system due to its displacement from equilibrium. It is proportional to the square of the displacement. This means potential energy is maximum at the amplitude positions, where the object momentarily stops, and zero at the equilibrium position.

Energy in SHM: Kinetic Energy

  • Energy due to motion
  • K = ½ mv² = ½ mω²(A² - x²)
  • Maximum at equilibrium position (x=0)
  • Minimum (zero) at extreme positions (x=±A)

🎙️ Kinetic energy, as you know, is the energy of motion. In SHM, kinetic energy is maximum when the object passes through the equilibrium position, as its speed is highest there. Conversely, it's zero at the extreme positions where the object momentarily comes to rest before reversing direction.

Total Mechanical Energy in SHM

  • Total Energy (E) = Kinetic Energy (K) + Potential Energy (U)
  • E = ½ mω²A² = ½ kA²
  • Total mechanical energy remains constant (conservation of energy)
  • Energy continuously transforms between K and U

🎙️ The total mechanical energy in SHM is the sum of kinetic and potential energies. A remarkable feature of SHM is that this total energy remains constant, assuming no dissipative forces. Energy continuously interconverts between kinetic and potential forms, but their sum is always equal to ½ kA², which depends only on the amplitude and spring constant.

The Simple Pendulum

  • Mass (bob) suspended by a light inextensible string
  • Undergoes SHM for small angular displacements (θ < 10°)
  • Time period T = 2π√(L/g)
  • L: length of the pendulum, g: acceleration due to gravity

🎙️ The simple pendulum is a classic example of SHM, provided its oscillations are small. The restoring force is a component of gravity, which for small angles becomes proportional to displacement. The time period depends only on the length of the string and the acceleration due to gravity, not on the mass of the bob.

The Spring-Mass System

  • Mass attached to a spring, undergoing oscillations
  • Horizontal: T = 2π√(m/k)
  • Vertical: Same formula, as gravity only shifts equilibrium position
  • m: mass, k: spring constant

🎙️ Another fundamental system demonstrating SHM is the spring-mass system. The time period depends on the mass attached and the spring's stiffness, represented by the spring constant 'k'. Interestingly, the period formula remains the same whether the system oscillates horizontally or vertically, as gravity only shifts the equilibrium point in the vertical case.

Damped Oscillations

  • Oscillations where amplitude decreases over time
  • Due to dissipative forces like air resistance or friction
  • Energy is lost from the system as heat
  • Amplitude decays exponentially

🎙️ In real-world scenarios, oscillations don't continue indefinitely. Damped oscillations occur when energy is gradually lost from the system due to resistive forces. This leads to a continuous decrease in the amplitude of oscillation over time, often following an exponential decay pattern.

Forced Oscillations and Resonance

  • Forced Oscillations: System driven by an external periodic force
  • Resonance: Phenomenon where amplitude becomes very large
  • Occurs when driving frequency matches natural frequency
  • Examples: Musical instruments, breaking glass with sound

🎙️ When an external periodic force acts on an oscillating system, it leads to forced oscillations. The most exciting aspect is resonance, where the system's amplitude dramatically increases if the driving frequency matches its natural frequency. This principle is vital in engineering and music, but can also be destructive, like bridge collapses.

Worked Example 1: SHM Equation

  • Problem: A particle executes SHM with amplitude 5 cm and period 2 s. If it starts from x = +5 cm at t=0, write its displacement equation.
  • Solution Steps: Find ω, determine φ from initial conditions.
  • ω = 2π/T = 2π/2 = π rad/s. At t=0, x=+A, so φ = π/2.
  • Equation: x(t) = 5 sin(πt + π/2) = 5 cos(πt) cm.

🎙️ Let's apply what we've learned to a practical problem. The key here is to correctly determine the angular frequency (ω) and, most importantly, the phase constant (φ) based on the given initial conditions. Remember, if it starts at maximum positive displacement, the phase constant is π/2 for a sine function, or it's a cosine function.

Worked Example 2: Energy in SHM

  • Problem: A 0.2 kg mass oscillates with k=20 N/m. Amplitude A=0.1 m. Find total energy and max speed.
  • Solution Steps: Use E = ½ kA² and E = ½ mv_max².
  • E = ½ (20 N/m)(0.1 m)² = 0.1 J.
  • 0.1 J = ½ (0.2 kg) v_max², so v_max = 1 m/s.

🎙️ This example demonstrates how to calculate the total energy and maximum speed in an SHM system. Remember that the total energy is conserved and can be expressed in terms of amplitude and spring constant. The maximum speed occurs when all energy is kinetic, at the equilibrium position.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Incorrectly determining phase constant (φ)
  • Confusing period (T) with frequency (f) or angular frequency (ω)
  • Forgetting negative signs in force/acceleration equations
  • Not using SI units consistently

🎙️ Be vigilant about these common pitfalls. The phase constant requires careful consideration of initial conditions. Always double-check your units and ensure consistency. And never forget the negative sign in F=-kx and a=-ω²x, as it's fundamental to the nature of restoring forces in SHM.

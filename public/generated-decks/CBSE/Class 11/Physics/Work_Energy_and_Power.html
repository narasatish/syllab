What is Work? (Physics Definition)

  • Work is done when a force causes displacement.
  • Force and displacement must have a component along the same direction.
  • It is a scalar quantity.

🎙️ In physics, 'work' has a very specific meaning, different from its everyday usage. Work is only done if a force applied to an object actually moves it over a distance. If you push against a wall and it doesn't move, no work is done in the physics sense.

Work Done: Formula and Units

  • Formula: W = F ⋅ d = Fd cosθ
  • θ is the angle between force (F) and displacement (d).
  • SI Unit: Joule (J); 1 J = 1 N·m.

🎙️ The dot product in the formula emphasizes that only the component of force parallel to the displacement contributes to work. If the force is perpendicular to displacement, no work is done. The Joule is the standard unit, named after James Prescott Joule.

Positive, Negative, and Zero Work

  • Positive Work: Force and displacement in same direction (0° ≤ θ < 90°).
  • Negative Work: Force and displacement in opposite direction (90° < θ ≤ 180°).
  • Zero Work: Force perpendicular to displacement (θ = 90°) or no displacement.

🎙️ Understanding these types is crucial. For example, lifting a box involves positive work by you, but gravity does negative work. When a satellite orbits Earth, gravity does zero work because the force is perpendicular to the displacement.

Example: Calculating Work Done

  • A force of 10 N pulls a box 5 m along a floor.
  • The force is applied at an angle of 30° to the horizontal.
  • Calculate the work done.

🎙️ In this example, we directly apply the formula. Remember to use the correct angle between the force and the displacement. Often, students forget to use the cosine term or use the wrong angle.

1Identify F, d, θ
2Apply W = Fd cosθ
3Calculate (10)(5)cos30°
4Result: 43.3 J

Work Done by a Variable Force

  • If force varies with position, W = ∫ F ⋅ dr.
  • Graphically, work is the area under the Force-Displacement curve.
  • Important for springs (Hooke's Law: F = -kx).

🎙️ Many real-world forces, like spring forces, are not constant. For such cases, we use integration to find the total work done. This involves calculating the area under the force-displacement graph.

Energy: The Capacity to Do Work

  • Energy is the ability or capacity of a body to do work.
  • It is a scalar quantity.
  • SI Unit: Joule (J), same as work.

🎙️ Energy is intimately linked to work. If an object possesses energy, it has the potential to perform work. Energy can exist in various forms and can be transferred or transformed, but never created or destroyed.

Kinetic Energy: Energy of Motion

  • Energy possessed by an object due to its motion.
  • Formula: KE = ½ mv² (where m = mass, v = velocity).
  • Scalar quantity, always positive.

🎙️ Any object that is moving has kinetic energy. The faster an object moves or the greater its mass, the more kinetic energy it possesses. This energy can then be used to do work on another object, for example, a moving hammer hitting a nail.

Potential Energy: Stored Energy

  • Energy stored in an object due to its position or configuration.
  • Gravitational Potential Energy (GPE): PE = mgh.
  • Elastic Potential Energy (EPE): PE = ½ kx² (for a spring).

🎙️ Potential energy is 'stored' energy that has the potential to be converted into other forms, like kinetic energy. A book held high above the ground has gravitational potential energy; a stretched spring has elastic potential energy.

Conservative vs. Non-Conservative Forces

  • Conservative: Work done is path-independent; depends only on initial and final positions (e.g., gravity, spring force).
  • Non-Conservative: Work done is path-dependent; dissipates energy (e.g., friction, air resistance).
  • Potential energy can only be defined for conservative forces.

🎙️ This distinction is very important for understanding energy conservation. For conservative forces, if you move an object in a closed loop, the net work done is zero. This is not true for non-conservative forces like friction, which always do negative work.

The Work-Energy Theorem

  • The net work done on an object equals the change in its kinetic energy.
  • W_net = ΔKE = KE_f - KE_i = ½ mv_f² - ½ mv_i².
  • Applies to all types of forces (conservative and non-conservative).

🎙️ This powerful theorem provides a direct link between work and energy. It means that if you do positive net work on an object, its kinetic energy increases, and if you do negative net work, its kinetic energy decreases.

Law of Conservation of Mechanical Energy

  • In the absence of non-conservative forces, total mechanical energy (KE + PE) remains constant.
  • KE_i + PE_i = KE_f + PE_f = Constant.
  • Applies when only conservative forces do work.

🎙️ This is a cornerstone of physics. If friction or air resistance are negligible, then energy simply transforms between kinetic and potential forms, but the total amount stays the same. Think of a roller coaster, where height is traded for speed.

  • Find its speed just before hitting the ground (ignoring air resistance).

🎙️ At the top, the ball has only potential energy (mgh). Just before hitting the ground, it has only kinetic energy (1/2 mv²). By equating these, we can easily find the final velocity, demonstrating the power of this law.

2Final State (Ground)
3Apply KE_i+PE_i = KE_f+PE_f

Power: Rate of Doing Work

  • Power is the rate at which work is done or energy is transferred.
  • Average Power: P_avg = W/t = ΔE/t.
  • Instantaneous Power: P = dW/dt = F ⋅ v.

🎙️ While work tells us how much energy is transferred, power tells us how quickly that transfer happens. A powerful engine can do a large amount of work in a short time, whereas a less powerful one takes longer to do the same work.

Units of Power: Watt and Horsepower

  • SI Unit: Watt (W); 1 Watt = 1 Joule/second.
  • Commercial unit for electrical energy: Kilowatt-hour (kWh).
  • Other common unit: Horsepower (hp); 1 hp ≈ 746 W.

🎙️ The Watt is the standard unit. Kilowatt-hour is a unit of energy, not power, representing the energy consumed by a 1 kW device for 1 hour. Horsepower is an older, non-SI unit still used in some industries.

Example: Calculating Power

  • A motor lifts a 50 kg mass to a height of 10 m in 5 seconds.
  • Calculate the power developed by the motor.

🎙️ Here, the work done against gravity is calculated as mgh. Then, this work is divided by the time taken to find the power. This is a straightforward application of the average power formula.

1Calculate Work Done (mgh)
2Identify Time (t)
3Apply P = W/t
4Result: 980 W

Collisions: Elastic and Inelastic

  • Elastic Collisions: Both momentum and kinetic energy are conserved.
  • Inelastic Collisions: Momentum is conserved, but kinetic energy is not.
  • Perfectly Inelastic: Objects stick together after collision (maximum KE loss).

🎙️ Collisions are interactions where objects exert forces on each other for a short duration. The conservation laws of momentum and energy are critical for analyzing these events. Most real-world collisions are inelastic.

Avoid These Common Mistakes!

  • Confusing work in physics with everyday work.
  • Forgetting 'cosθ' in work formula or using wrong angle.
  • Misinterpreting conservation of energy (e.g., ignoring friction).

🎙️ Students often make errors in applying the angle in the work formula. Also, remember that mechanical energy is conserved only if non-conservative forces are absent or negligible. Always identify the forces acting on the system.

What is Work?

  • In physics, work has a precise meaning.
  • It involves force causing displacement.
  • Not all effort is 'work' in physics.

🎙️ In everyday language, 'work' refers to any mental or physical effort. However, in physics, work is specifically defined. It requires a force to act on an object and cause a displacement in the direction of the force.

Work Done by a Constant Force

  • Force must be constant in magnitude and direction.
  • Displacement must occur in the direction of force.
  • Work (W) = Force (F) × Displacement (d).

🎙️ When a constant force acts on an object, and the object moves a certain distance, work is said to be done. The simplest case is when the force and displacement are in the same direction, making the calculation straightforward.

Work Done: The Dot Product

  • Work is the scalar dot product of force and displacement vectors.
  • W = Fd cosθ, where θ is the angle between F and d.
  • Only the component of force along displacement does work.

🎙️ This is the most general definition of work. The cosine term accounts for situations where the force is not perfectly aligned with the displacement. Understanding this angle is crucial for solving problems accurately.

Units of Work

  • SI unit of work is Joule (J).
  • 1 Joule = 1 Newton × 1 meter.
  • CGS unit is Erg; 1 Joule = 10⁷ Erg.

🎙️ The Joule is the standard unit for work and energy. It's important to be familiar with both SI and CGS units, as problems might use either. Remember the conversion factor between Joule and Erg.

Types of Work Done

  • Positive Work: Force and displacement in same direction (θ < 90°).
  • Negative Work: Force opposes displacement (θ > 90°).
  • Zero Work: Force perpendicular to displacement (θ = 90°) or no displacement.

🎙️ This distinction is very important. For example, lifting an object involves positive work, while friction does negative work. Holding a heavy bag still does zero work in physics terms, despite the effort.

Work by a Variable Force

  • Force changes with position or time.
  • Work is calculated by integrating F.dx.
  • Graphically, work is the area under the Force-Displacement curve.

🎙️ When a force is not constant, we cannot simply use W=Fd. We must use calculus (integration) to sum up the work done over infinitesimally small displacements. The graphical method provides a good visual understanding.

What is Energy?

  • Energy is the capacity to do work.
  • It exists in various forms: mechanical, heat, light, chemical, etc.
  • Energy is conserved; it can be transformed but not created or destroyed.

🎙️ Energy is a fundamental concept in physics, closely linked to work. If an object possesses energy, it has the potential to do work. We will focus primarily on mechanical energy in this chapter.

Kinetic Energy

  • Energy possessed by an object due to its motion.
  • Depends on mass and speed of the object.
  • A moving car, a flying bird, a rolling ball all have kinetic energy.

🎙️ Any object in motion has kinetic energy. The faster an object moves and the more massive it is, the greater its kinetic energy. This energy is a scalar quantity.

Deriving Kinetic Energy

  • Start with Work-Energy Theorem (W = ΔKE).
  • Consider a constant force F causing displacement d.
  • From Newton's 2nd Law and kinematics, derive KE = 1/2 mv².

🎙️ This derivation is important for understanding the relationship between work, force, and kinetic energy. It connects concepts from kinematics and dynamics, leading to the fundamental formula for kinetic energy.

1Apply F=ma
2Use v²=u²+2as
3Substitute into W=Fd
4Result: KE = ½mv²
12 / 25

Work-Energy Theorem

  • The net work done on an object equals the change in its kinetic energy.
  • W_net = ΔKE = KE_f - KE_i.
  • Applies to both constant and variable forces.

🎙️ This is a powerful theorem that simplifies many problems. Instead of calculating work directly, you can find the change in kinetic energy, or vice versa. It's a statement of energy conservation.

↔️
Work ⇌ Energy
13 / 25

Worked Example: Work-Energy

  • A 5 kg block initially at rest is pushed by a 10 N force for 2 m.
  • Find its final kinetic energy and speed.
  • Solution: W = Fd = 10 N * 2 m = 20 J.
  • By Work-Energy Theorem, ΔKE = W = 20 J. Since initial KE = 0, final KE = 20 J. 1/2 mv² = 20 J => 1/2 * 5 * v² = 20 => v² = 8 => v = 2√2 m/s.

🎙️ This example demonstrates the direct application of the Work-Energy Theorem. Notice how we connect the work done by the force to the resulting change in the block's motion and its final kinetic energy.

Potential Energy

  • Energy stored in an object due to its position or configuration.
  • Gravitational PE: energy due to height in a gravitational field.
  • Elastic PE: energy stored in a stretched/compressed spring.

🎙️ Potential energy is 'stored' energy, ready to be converted into kinetic energy or do work. Gravitational potential energy is common, but remember elastic potential energy for springs, which we'll briefly touch upon.

Deriving Gravitational PE

  • Work done against gravity to lift an object to height h.
  • Force required = weight (mg).
  • Work done = Force × displacement = mg × h.

🎙️ This derivation shows that the potential energy gained is equal to the work done against the gravitational force. It highlights how energy can be stored by changing an object's position.

1Force = mg (upwards)
2Displacement = h (upwards)
3Work = F x h
4PE = mgh

Forces: Conservative & Non-Conservative

  • Conservative: Work done is path-independent, depends only on initial/final positions (e.g., gravity, elastic force).
  • Non-Conservative: Work done is path-dependent, dissipates energy (e.g., friction, air resistance).
  • Mechanical energy is conserved only with conservative forces.

🎙️ Understanding this distinction is crucial for applying the principle of conservation of mechanical energy. Friction, for example, converts mechanical energy into heat, making it non-conservative.

Conservation of Mechanical Energy

  • Mechanical Energy (E) = Kinetic Energy (KE) + Potential Energy (PE).
  • In the absence of non-conservative forces, E remains constant.
  • KE_i + PE_i = KE_f + PE_f.

🎙️ This is one of the most fundamental principles in physics. If only conservative forces are doing work, the total mechanical energy of a system is conserved. This principle is extremely useful for solving problems involving motion.

🔒
Energy Balance

Worked Example: Energy Conservation

  • A ball of mass 0.5 kg is dropped from a height of 10 m.
  • Find its speed just before hitting the ground (g=10 m/s²).
  • Solution: Initial PE = mgh = 0.5 * 10 * 10 = 50 J. Initial KE = 0.
  • Final PE = 0 (at ground). Final KE = 1/2 mv². By Conservation of Energy: Initial E = Final E => 50 J = 1/2 * 0.5 * v² => v² = 200 => v = 10√2 m/s.

🎙️ This example illustrates how to apply the conservation of mechanical energy. We equate the total energy at the initial point to the total energy at the final point, simplifying the calculation of final velocity.

What is Power?

  • Power is the rate at which work is done or energy is transferred.
  • It tells us how fast work is being performed.
  • A powerful machine does work quickly.

🎙️ While work tells us the total energy transferred, power tells us how quickly that transfer happens. A powerful engine can accelerate a car faster than a less powerful one, even if both eventually do the same amount of work.

Power Formulas

  • Average Power (P_avg) = Total Work (W) / Total Time (t).
  • Instantaneous Power (P) = dW/dt = F.v (Force × Velocity).
  • These formulas connect work, time, force, and velocity.

🎙️ The two main formulas for power are important. P = W/t is for average power, while P = F.v is for instantaneous power, useful when force or velocity are changing.

⏱️
Work per Time

Units of Power

  • SI unit of power is Watt (W).
  • 1 Watt = 1 Joule / 1 second.
  • Another common unit is Horsepower (hp); 1 hp ≈ 746 Watts.

🎙️ The Watt is the standard unit for power. Horsepower is an older, non-SI unit still used in some contexts, particularly for engines. Know the conversion factor.

Collisions: Energy Perspective

  • Collision: interaction between objects causing impulse forces.
  • Elastic Collision: Kinetic energy and momentum are conserved.
  • Inelastic Collision: Only momentum is conserved; kinetic energy is NOT conserved (some converted to heat, sound).

🎙️ Collisions are a special case of interaction where forces act for a short duration. The key distinction lies in whether kinetic energy is conserved. Understanding this is vital for solving collision problems.

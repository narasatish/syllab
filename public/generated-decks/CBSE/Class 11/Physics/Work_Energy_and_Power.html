What is 'Work' in Physics?

  • You study for hours, but is it 'work' in physics?
  • You push a wall, but it doesn't move. Is work done?
  • Physics has a specific, quantitative definition for 'work'.

🎙️ In everyday language, 'work' often means effort. However, in physics, work has a very precise meaning that requires both a force and a displacement in the direction of that force.

Work Done by a Constant Force

  • Work (W) is done when a force (F) causes a displacement (d).
  • The force must have a component along the direction of displacement.
  • Formula: W = F ⋅ d = Fd cosθ (where θ is angle between F and d).

🎙️ For work to be done, two conditions must be met: a force must act on an object, and the object must undergo displacement. The angle θ between the force and displacement is critical.

Units and Dimensions of Work

  • SI Unit: Joule (J). 1 Joule = 1 Newton-metre (N·m).
  • CGS Unit: Erg. 1 Joule = 10⁷ Erg.
  • Dimensional Formula: [ML²T⁻²].

🎙️ The Joule is named after James Prescott Joule. Understanding the dimensions helps in checking the consistency of equations and converting between different unit systems, which is a common exam skill.

Positive, Negative, and Zero Work

  • Positive Work: Force and displacement in same direction (0° ≤ θ < 90°).
  • Negative Work: Force and displacement in opposite directions (90° < θ ≤ 180°).
  • Zero Work: Force perpendicular to displacement (θ = 90°) OR no displacement (d=0).

🎙️ These types are crucial for understanding energy changes. Pushing a box forward is positive work. Friction does negative work. A coolie carrying luggage horizontally does zero work on the luggage, as force is vertical and displacement is horizontal.

Example: Calculating Work Done

  • Q: A boy pulls a trolley with a force of 50 N at an angle of 30° to the horizontal. If the trolley moves 10 m, calculate the work done by the boy.
  • Given: F = 50 N, d = 10 m, θ = 30°.
  • Formula: W = Fd cosθ.
  • Calculation: W = 50 × 10 × cos(30°) = 500 × (√3/2) ≈ 433 J.

🎙️ Always break down the problem. Identify the given values and the angle between the force and displacement. Remember to use the correct trigonometric value for the angle for accurate results.

What is 'Energy'?

  • The capacity or ability to do work.
  • It exists in various forms: mechanical, heat, light, sound, electrical, chemical, nuclear.
  • Energy is conserved, meaning it can transform but never be created or destroyed.

🎙️ Energy is the most fundamental concept in physics. Without energy, no work can be done, no change can occur. It's the driving force behind all phenomena we observe.

Energy of Motion: Kinetic Energy (KE)

  • Energy possessed by an object due to its motion.
  • A moving car, a flying bird, flowing water all possess kinetic energy.
  • Greater mass or speed means greater kinetic energy.

🎙️ Kinetic energy is directly related to the movement of an object. Imagine a cricket ball: the faster it moves, the more damage it can cause, indicating higher kinetic energy.

💨
Energy in Motion
12 / 30

Deriving Kinetic Energy (KE)

  • Consider a body of mass 'm' accelerated from rest (u=0) to velocity 'v' by force 'F' over distance 's'.
  • Work done W = Fs and from Newton's 2nd Law F = ma.
  • Using kinematic equation: v² = u² + 2as => v² = 2as => s = v²/2a.
  • Substituting: W = (ma)(v²/2a) = ½ mv². Since W = ΔKE, KE = ½ mv².

🎙️ This derivation is important for understanding the relationship between work and kinetic energy. It shows how the work done on an object results in a change in its kinetic energy.

Example: Kinetic Energy of a Car

  • Q: A car of mass 1200 kg is moving with a velocity of 72 km/h. Calculate its kinetic energy.
  • Given: m = 1200 kg, v = 72 km/h. Convert v: 72 × (5/18) = 20 m/s.
  • Formula: KE = ½ mv².
  • Calculation: KE = ½ × 1200 × (20)² = 600 × 400 = 240,000 J = 240 kJ.

🎙️ Always remember to convert units to SI units (m/s for velocity) before calculation. A common mistake is forgetting this conversion, leading to incorrect answers in exams.

Stored Energy: Potential Energy (PE)

  • Energy possessed by an object due to its position or configuration.
  • Examples: A stone held high, a stretched rubber band, a compressed spring.
  • It's 'potential' because it has the capacity to do work when released.

🎙️ Potential energy is stored energy, ready to be converted into other forms, usually kinetic energy. It depends on the relative position of an object within a force field or its internal state.

Gravitational Potential Energy (GPE)

  • Energy stored due to an object's position in a gravitational field.
  • Reference level is crucial (often the ground, where GPE = 0).
  • Formula: GPE = mgh (where g is acceleration due to gravity, h is height).

🎙️ The higher an object is, the more gravitational potential energy it possesses. This energy is relative to a chosen reference point, so always specify your reference level in problems.

Elastic Potential Energy (EPE)

  • Energy stored in a deformed elastic object (e.g., stretched spring, compressed rubber band).
  • Depends on the spring constant (k) and the deformation (x).
  • Formula: EPE = ½ kx².

🎙️ Elastic potential energy is crucial in understanding springs, rubber bands, and even muscles. The 'k' value represents the stiffness of the spring; a higher 'k' means a stiffer spring.

The Work-Energy Theorem

  • States that the net work done on an object equals the change in its kinetic energy.
  • W_net = ΔKE = KE_final - KE_initial = ½ mv_f² - ½ mv_i².
  • A powerful tool relating force, displacement, and changes in motion.

🎙️ This theorem is a fundamental principle. It tells us that any net work done on an object directly translates into a change in its speed, and thus its kinetic energy. This is a crucial concept for exams.

↔️

Example: Work-Energy Theorem (Car Braking)

  • Q: A 1000 kg car moving at 15 m/s applies brakes and stops in 25 m. Calculate the average braking force.
  • Given: m = 1000 kg, v_i = 15 m/s, v_f = 0 m/s, d = 25 m.
  • ΔKE = KE_f - KE_i = ½ × 1000 × (0)² - ½ × 1000 × (15)² = -112,500 J.
  • W_net = F_braking × d. So, F_braking = W_net / d = -112,500 / 25 = -4500 N.

🎙️ The negative sign indicates the braking force acts opposite to the direction of motion, which is physically correct. This theorem simplifies problems that would be complex using kinematics alone.

2Set W_net = ΔKE
3Solve for Force

Law of Conservation of Energy

  • Energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transformed from one form to another.
  • Total energy of an isolated system (where only conservative forces act) remains constant.
  • Mechanical Energy (KE + PE) = Constant (for conservative forces).

🎙️ This is one of the most important laws in all of physics. It means that while energy might change its appearance, the total amount in a closed system never changes. This is a powerful problem-solving tool.

Example: Conservation of Energy (Ball Drop)

  • Q: A ball of mass 0.5 kg is dropped from a height of 20 m. What is its speed just before hitting the ground? (Use g = 10 m/s²).
  • Initial energy (at 20m): PE = mgh = 0.5 × 10 × 20 = 100 J, KE = 0 J. Total E_initial = 100 J.
  • Final energy (at ground): PE = 0 J, KE = ½ mv². Total E_final = ½ mv².
  • By Conservation: 100 J = ½ × 0.5 × v². So, v² = 400, v = 20 m/s.

🎙️ This method avoids using complex kinematic equations and directly applies energy principles. It's often simpler and more elegant for problems involving height and speed changes under gravity.

2Set Equal to Final Total Energy
3Solve for Unknown

How Fast is Work Done? Power!

  • Power is the rate at which work is done or energy is transferred.
  • It measures how quickly energy is used or produced.
  • A powerful engine does work faster than a less powerful one.

🎙️ While work tells us *how much* effort was expended, power tells us *how quickly* that effort was expended. Two people might do the same work, but the one who finishes faster has more power.

🚀
Rate of Work

Power: Formula and Units

  • Formula: P = W/t (Work done per unit time).
  • Also, P = F ⋅ v (Force times velocity, for constant velocity).
  • SI Unit: Watt (W). 1 Watt = 1 Joule/second (J/s).
  • Other Unit: Horsepower (hp). 1 hp ≈ 746 W.

🎙️ The Watt is named after James Watt, known for his improvements to the steam engine. The P = F ⋅ v formula is particularly useful when dealing with objects moving at a constant speed against a resistance.

Example: Calculating Power

  • Q: A man lifts a 20 kg mass to a height of 5 m in 4 seconds. Calculate the power exerted by him. (g = 10 m/s²).
  • Work done (W) = mgh = 20 × 10 × 5 = 1000 J.
  • Time (t) = 4 s.
  • Power (P) = W/t = 1000 J / 4 s = 250 W.

🎙️ First calculate the work done against gravity (which is the potential energy gained), then divide by the time taken. This is a common type of problem to test understanding of both work and power.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Forgetting the angle (cosθ) in Work: Always consider the angle between Force and Displacement.
  • Incorrect Unit Conversions: e.g., km/h to m/s, hp to W. Double-check!
  • Confusing Work and Power: Work is total energy transfer, Power is the rate of transfer.
  • Neglecting Friction/Air Resistance: Unless stated, assume ideal conditions, but be aware of their role.

🎙️ These are typical pitfalls students fall into. Paying attention to these details can save crucial marks in exams. Always read the question carefully and understand what it's asking for.

Recap: Work, Energy, Power

  • Work: Force × Displacement (component along force). W = Fd cosθ.
  • Energy: Capacity to do work. Forms include KE (½ mv²) and PE (mgh, ½ kx²).
  • Power: Rate of doing work. P = W/t = Fv.
  • Work-Energy Theorem: Net work done equals change in kinetic energy (W_net = ΔKE).
  • Conservation of Energy: Total energy of an isolated system remains constant.

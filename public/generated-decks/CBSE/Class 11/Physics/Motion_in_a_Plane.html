Scalars vs. Vectors: A Quick Review

  • Scalars: Magnitude only (e.g., distance, speed, mass).
  • Vectors: Magnitude AND direction (e.g., displacement, velocity, force).
  • Vector notation: Arrow over symbol (e.g., →v).

🎙️ Before diving into 2D motion, let's quickly recap scalars and vectors. Understanding the difference is fundamental, as motion in a plane inherently involves vector quantities like displacement, velocity, and acceleration. Remember, direction is key for vectors.

Vector Addition: Triangle Law

  • If two vectors are represented by two sides of a triangle in order.
  • Their resultant is represented by the third side in opposite order.
  • Head-to-tail method for adding vectors.

🎙️ The Triangle Law is a geometric method for adding two vectors. You place the tail of the second vector at the head of the first. The resultant vector then connects the tail of the first to the head of the second, completing the triangle.

Vector Addition: Parallelogram Law

  • If two vectors are represented by two adjacent sides of a parallelogram.
  • Their resultant is given by the diagonal passing through their common point.
  • Applicable for co-initial vectors (starting from same point).

🎙️ The Parallelogram Law is another geometric method, particularly useful when two vectors originate from the same point. The resultant is the diagonal of the parallelogram formed by these two vectors, starting from their common origin.

Vector Addition: Analytical Method

  • Resolve vectors into rectangular components (x and y).
  • Add x-components and y-components separately.
  • Resultant magnitude: R = √(Rx² + Ry²); Direction: tanθ = Ry/Rx.

🎙️ For more complex problems, the analytical method using components is preferred. By breaking vectors into their x and y components, we can add them algebraically. This method is precise and universally applicable for any number of vectors.

Vector Subtraction

  • Subtracting vector B from A is equivalent to adding -B to A.
  • -B has the same magnitude as B but opposite direction.
  • A - B = A + (-B).

🎙️ Vector subtraction isn't simply about reducing magnitudes. It's defined as adding the negative of the vector being subtracted. This means reversing the direction of the vector to be subtracted and then performing standard vector addition.

Resolution of Vectors

  • The process of splitting a vector into two or more components.
  • Usually resolved into two perpendicular (rectangular) components.
  • Ax = A cosθ, Ay = A sinθ, where θ is angle with x-axis.

🎙️ Resolution of vectors is the inverse of vector addition. It's a powerful technique where a single vector is broken down into its effective parts along specific directions. This simplifies analysis, especially in 2D motion problems, by converting a single vector into two independent components.

Worked Example: Vector Addition

  • Problem: Two forces 3N and 4N act at right angles to each other. Find the magnitude and direction of their resultant.
  • Solution: R = √(3² + 4²) = 5N. tanθ = 4/3, so θ = 53.13° with the 3N force.
  • Common mistake: Simply adding magnitudes (3+4=7N) which is incorrect.

🎙️ This example demonstrates how to find the resultant of two perpendicular vectors using the analytical method. Remember to use Pythagoras theorem for magnitude and trigonometry for direction. Never just add magnitudes for vectors acting at an angle.

Position and Displacement Vectors

  • Position vector (r): Describes object's location from origin.
  • Displacement vector (Δr): Change in position vector (Δr = r₂ - r₁).
  • Displacement is the shortest distance between initial and final points.

🎙️ In 2D, an object's position is given by a position vector from the origin to its location. Displacement, then, is the vector difference between the final and initial position vectors. It's important to distinguish displacement from the actual path length travelled.

Velocity Vector

  • Average velocity: Δr/Δt (total displacement / total time).
  • Instantaneous velocity: dr/dt (rate of change of position).
  • Direction of instantaneous velocity is tangent to the path.

🎙️ Velocity in 2D is a vector quantity, possessing both magnitude (speed) and direction. The instantaneous velocity vector is always tangential to the path of motion at any given point. This means its direction continuously changes in curved paths.

Acceleration Vector

  • Average acceleration: Δv/Δt (change in velocity / time).
  • Instantaneous acceleration: dv/dt (rate of change of velocity).
  • Acceleration can change speed, direction, or both.

🎙️ Acceleration in 2D describes how the velocity vector changes over time. This change can be in the magnitude of velocity (speeding up or slowing down), its direction (turning), or both. It's a crucial concept for understanding forces and motion.

Motion in a Plane with Constant Acceleration

  • Kinematic equations are applied component-wise (x and y).
  • vx = ux + axt, vy = uy + ayt.
  • rx = ux t + ½ axt², ry = uy t + ½ ayt².

🎙️ When acceleration is constant, we can apply the familiar 1D kinematic equations independently to the x and y components of motion. This simplifies complex 2D problems into two separate 1D problems, connected by time.

Projectile Motion: A Special Case

  • Motion of an object thrown into space and moving under gravity alone.
  • Assumes negligible air resistance.
  • Path traced is a parabola.

🎙️ Projectile motion is a classic example of motion in a plane. When an object is launched into the air and only gravity acts upon it, it follows a characteristic parabolic path. This motion is analyzed by separating it into independent horizontal and vertical components.

Projectile Motion: Assumptions & Trajectory

  • Air resistance is negligible.
  • Acceleration due to gravity (g) is constant and vertically downwards.
  • Horizontal velocity component is constant; Vertical motion is under constant 'g'.

🎙️ To simplify analysis, we make certain ideal assumptions for projectile motion. The key insight is that the horizontal and vertical motions are independent, with time connecting them. The horizontal motion is uniform, while the vertical motion is uniformly accelerated.

Projectile Motion: Time of Flight (T) & Max Height (H)

  • Time of Flight (T): Total time the projectile is in the air. T = (2u sinθ)/g.
  • Maximum Height (H): Highest point reached above projection point. H = (u² sin²θ)/(2g).
  • Derived from vertical motion equations.

🎙️ These are two important parameters describing projectile motion. The time of flight depends on the initial vertical velocity component, while the maximum height reached also depends on this component and the acceleration due to gravity. Remember these formulas for problem-solving.

Projectile Motion: Horizontal Range (R)

  • Horizontal Range (R): Total horizontal distance covered. R = (u² sin2θ)/g.
  • Maximum range occurs at a projection angle of 45°.
  • For a given speed, two complementary angles (θ and 90°-θ) give the same range.

🎙️ The horizontal range is the distance covered along the ground. It depends on both the initial speed and the projection angle. Knowing that maximum range is achieved at 45 degrees and that complementary angles yield the same range are important exam points.

Worked Example: Projectile Motion

  • Problem: A projectile is fired with an initial velocity of 20 m/s at 30° above the horizontal. Find its time of flight and maximum height (g=10 m/s²).
  • Solution: T = (2 * 20 * sin30°)/10 = 2s. H = (20² * sin²30°)/(2*10) = 5m.
  • Remember to use components for initial velocity: ux = u cosθ, uy = u sinθ.

🎙️ This example shows the direct application of the formulas for time of flight and maximum height. Make sure to correctly substitute the values and use the sine component for vertical motion. Pay attention to units and significant figures.

Uniform Circular Motion (UCM)

  • Motion of an object along a circular path at a constant speed.
  • Speed is constant, but velocity is continuously changing (due to changing direction).
  • Therefore, UCM is an accelerated motion.

🎙️ Uniform Circular Motion is another important type of 2D motion. Even though the object's speed is constant, its direction is continuously changing, which means its velocity is changing. Any change in velocity implies acceleration, making UCM an accelerated motion.

Circular motion occurs when an object moves in a circular path at constant speed. Velocity

Centripetal Acceleration & Force

  • Acceleration in UCM is always directed towards the center of the circle (centripetal acceleration).
  • Magnitude: ac = v²/r = rω² (where ω is angular speed).
  • Centripetal Force (Fc): Fc = mac = mv²/r, also directed towards the center.

🎙️ The acceleration in UCM is called centripetal acceleration, always pointing towards the center. This acceleration requires a centripetal force to maintain the circular path. This force is not a new type of force but is provided by existing forces like tension, friction, or gravity.

Non-Uniform Circular Motion

  • Speed of the object changes along the circular path.
  • Has both centripetal (radial) and tangential acceleration.
  • Net acceleration: a = √(ac² + at²).

🎙️ In non-uniform circular motion, both the speed and direction of the object change. This means there's a tangential acceleration (changing speed) in addition to the centripetal acceleration (changing direction). The net acceleration is the vector sum of these two perpendicular components.

The Position Vector (r): Pinpointing Location

  • A vector drawn from the origin to a particle's location
  • Describes the exact location of an object in 2D space
  • Mathematically: r = xî + yĵ, where (x,y) are the Cartesian coordinates

🎙️ Think of it like giving directions from a central point, say your school. The position vector tells us precisely where an object is at any given moment relative to our chosen origin.

Displacement Vector (Δr): Change in Position

  • The change in an object's position vector
  • Δr = r₂ - r₁ = (x₂-x₁)î + (y₂-y₁)ĵ
  • Represents the straight line path between initial and final points

🎙️ Displacement is not the total distance travelled, but the shortest straight-line path from where you started to where you ended up. It's a vector, so both magnitude and direction are important.

Velocity in a Plane: How Fast & Where?

Analyzing Projectile Motion: Horizontal Component

  • No horizontal force (assuming negligible air resistance)
  • Therefore, horizontal acceleration (ax) = 0
  • Horizontal velocity (vx) remains constant throughout the motion

🎙️ This is a crucial insight: without air resistance, nothing pushes or pulls the object horizontally, so its horizontal speed never changes. It's like a constant speed car on a straight, frictionless road.

Analyzing Projectile Motion: Vertical Component

  • Only vertical force acting is gravity (downwards)
  • Vertical acceleration (ay) = -g (constant, approx. 9.8 m/s²)
  • Vertical velocity changes due to gravity: decreases upwards, increases downwards

🎙️ Vertically, it's just like free fall. The object accelerates downwards at 'g'. Its upward velocity decreases, becomes zero at the peak, and then increases downwards, mirroring its upward journey.

Time of Flight (T): How Long in the Air?

  • The total time the projectile remains in the air
  • From launch to landing at the same horizontal level
  • Formula: T = 2u sinθ / g (for launch from ground level)

🎙️ The time of flight is determined purely by the vertical motion. The projectile goes up and comes down. The symmetry of this motion often simplifies calculations, especially when landing at the same height.

Total air time

Maximum Height (H): The Peak of the Trajectory

  • The highest vertical point reached by the projectile
  • At this point, the vertical velocity (vy) momentarily becomes zero
  • Formula: H = u² sin²θ / 2g (for launch from ground level)

🎙️ At the very peak of its flight, the projectile momentarily stops moving upwards before it starts falling. This is the point where its vertical velocity component is exactly zero.

Horizontal Range (R): How Far Does It Go?

  • The total horizontal distance covered by the projectile
  • Measured from the point of launch to the point of landing (at same height)
  • Formula: R = u² sin(2θ) / g (for launch from ground level)

🎙️ The range depends on both the initial speed and the angle of projection. Interestingly, for a fixed speed, angles complementary to 45 degrees (e.g., 30 and 60) give the same horizontal range.

Trajectory of a Projectile: The Parabolic Path

  • The path followed by a projectile is always parabolic
  • Equation: y = x tanθ - (gx² / 2u² cos²θ)
  • This equation shows y is a quadratic function of x, characteristic of a parabola

🎙️ By combining the equations for horizontal and vertical motion, we can eliminate time and obtain an equation that describes the shape of the path. It's a perfect parabola, as Galileo discovered centuries ago.

Solution: Cricket Ball Example

  • Given: u = 20 m/s, θ = 30°, g = 9.8 m/s²
  • a) Time of Flight (T) = 2u sinθ / g = 2(20)sin30° / 9.8 ≈ 2.04 seconds
  • b) Max Height (H) = u² sin²θ / 2g = (20²) (sin30°)² / (2*9.8) ≈ 5.10 meters
  • c) Horizontal Range (R) = u² sin(2θ) / g = (20²) sin(2*30°) / 9.8 ≈ 35.35 meters

🎙️ Remember to use the correct units for all quantities and round your answers appropriately based on significant figures. Practice these calculations to become proficient for your exams.

Angular Displacement & Angular Velocity

  • Angular Displacement (Δθ): The angle swept by the radius vector
  • Angular Velocity (ω): Rate of change of angular displacement (Δθ/Δt)
  • Relation: ω = 2π/T = 2πf (where T=period, f=frequency)

🎙️ Instead of linear distance and speed, we use angular displacement and angular velocity to describe rotational motion. These are measured in radians and radians per second, respectively.

Connecting Linear and Angular Velocity

  • Linear velocity (v) is always tangent to the circular path at any point
  • The relation between them: v = rω (where r is the radius of the circle)
  • A larger radius 'r' means a higher linear speed 'v' for the same angular speed 'ω'

🎙️ At any point on the circle, the object has a linear velocity that's tangent to the path. This linear velocity is directly proportional to both the radius of the circle and the angular velocity.

Centripetal Acceleration (ac): Towards the Center

  • An acceleration always directed towards the center of the circular path
  • It causes a change in the direction of velocity, not its magnitude (speed)
  • Formula: ac = v²/r = rω²

🎙️ Even though speed is constant in UCM, velocity is changing due to its continuous change in direction. This 'center-seeking' acceleration is called centripetal acceleration and is crucial for circular motion.

Centripetal Force (Fc): The Cause of UCM

  • The force responsible for providing the centripetal acceleration
  • Formula: Fc = mac = mv²/r = mrω²
  • NOT a new type of force; it's a role played by existing forces (e.g., tension, friction, gravity)

🎙️ Centripetal force is not a fundamental force like gravity. It's the net force acting towards the center that *causes* the circular motion. For example, the tension in a string keeps a ball swinging in a circle.

Common Mistakes to Avoid in Motion in a Plane

  • Confusing distance with displacement, or speed with velocity.
  • Ignoring the vector nature of quantities in 2D problems.
  • Incorrectly applying 1D equations to components of 2D motion.
  • Forgetting to consider air resistance in real-world problems (unless stated otherwise).
  • Treating centripetal force as a separate, fundamental force.

🎙️ Many students lose marks due to these common errors. Always remember that in 2D, direction is paramount. Break down problems into components and apply the correct formulas carefully.

Chapter Recap: Key Concepts of Motion in a Plane

  • Vectors are essential for describing 2D motion.
  • Projectile motion involves independent horizontal (constant velocity) and vertical (constant acceleration) components.
  • Uniform Circular Motion (UCM) has constant speed but changing velocity due to centripetal acceleration (ac = v²/r).
  • Centripetal force (Fc = mv²/r) is the net force causing UCM, not a new fundamental force.

🎙️ We've covered a lot today! Remember the key takeaways: the independence of horizontal and vertical motion in projectiles, and the concept of centripetal acceleration and force in circular motion. These are the pillars of 2D kinematics.

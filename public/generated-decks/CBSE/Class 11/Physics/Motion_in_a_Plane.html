Recap: Scalars vs. Vectors

  • Scalars: Magnitude only (e.g., mass, time, distance).
  • Vectors: Magnitude AND direction (e.g., displacement, velocity, force).
  • Vector quantities require special addition/subtraction rules.

🎙️ Before diving into 2D motion, let's quickly recall the difference between scalars and vectors. This distinction is paramount as all quantities in 'Motion in a Plane' will be vector quantities, requiring vector algebra. Remember, a scalar is just a number, while a vector points somewhere.

Position Vector: Locating a Point

  • Vector from origin to particle's location.
  • Denoted by 'r'.
  • In 2D: r = xî + yĵ (where î, ĵ are unit vectors).

🎙️ The position vector tells us where an object is in a coordinate system. It's always drawn from the origin to the particle's current location. Understanding its representation in terms of unit vectors is key for calculations.

Displacement Vector: Change in Position

  • Vector connecting initial to final position.
  • Δr = r₂ - r₁.
  • Magnitude is shortest distance; direction is from initial to final.

🎙️ Displacement is not just distance; it's the directed change in position. If a particle moves from position r₁ to r₂, its displacement is the vector difference, Δr. Always remember that displacement is a vector quantity.

Velocity Vector: Rate of Change of Position

  • Average Velocity: Δr / Δt.
  • Instantaneous Velocity: dr/dt (tangent to path).
  • Direction is always tangential to the path.

🎙️ Just like in 1D, velocity is the rate of change of position, but now it's a vector. Instantaneous velocity is particularly important; its direction is always along the tangent to the path the object is taking at that moment. Its magnitude is the instantaneous speed.

Acceleration Vector: Rate of Change of Velocity

  • Average Acceleration: Δv / Δt.
  • Instantaneous Acceleration: dv/dt.
  • Can change magnitude or direction of velocity.

🎙️ Acceleration in 2D means that either the magnitude of velocity (speed) or its direction, or both, are changing. The acceleration vector points in the direction of the change in velocity, not necessarily in the direction of motion. This is crucial for understanding circular motion.

Vector Addition: Triangle Law

  • If two vectors represent two sides of a triangle in order.
  • Their resultant is the third side in the opposite order.
  • R = A + B.

🎙️ The Triangle Law is a graphical method for adding two vectors. Imagine walking along vector A, then along vector B. Your net displacement from start to finish is the resultant vector R. This law is intuitive for understanding vector sums.

Vector Addition: Parallelogram Law

  • If two vectors originate from a common point.
  • Their resultant is the diagonal of the parallelogram.
  • R = √(A² + B² + 2ABcosθ).

🎙️ The Parallelogram Law is another graphical method, especially useful when vectors originate from the same point. The formula for the magnitude of the resultant is very important and should be memorized. The angle θ is between vectors A and B.

Vector Subtraction: A - B

  • A - B is equivalent to A + (-B).
  • Negative vector has same magnitude, opposite direction.
  • Geometrically, it's finding the vector from B to A.

🎙️ Vector subtraction is essentially a special case of vector addition. Instead of adding vector B, we add its negative, which simply means reversing its direction. This concept is vital when calculating changes in vector quantities, like displacement or velocity.

Worked Example 1: Vector Addition

  • Problem: A car travels 3 km East, then 4 km North.
  • Find total displacement.
  • Solution: Using Pythagorean theorem, magnitude = √(3² + 4²) = 5 km.
  • Direction: tan⁻¹(4/3) North of East.

🎙️ Let's apply our understanding of vector addition. Here, the two displacements are perpendicular, so we can use the Pythagorean theorem for magnitude and trigonometry for direction. This is a classic example of finding resultant displacement.

Projectile Motion: An Introduction

  • Motion of an object thrown or projected into the air.
  • Moves under the influence of gravity only.
  • Examples: thrown ball, bullet fired, water from a hose.

🎙️ Projectile motion is a fascinating and common phenomenon. Anytime you throw an object, kick a ball, or even watch a water fountain, you're observing projectile motion. The key is that the only force acting on it after projection is gravity.

Assumptions in Projectile Motion

  • Air resistance is negligible.
  • Effect of Earth's curvature is negligible.
  • Effect of Earth's rotation is negligible.
  • Acceleration due to gravity 'g' is constant in magnitude and direction.

🎙️ To simplify our analysis, we make several idealizing assumptions. The most important is neglecting air resistance, which would otherwise complicate the motion significantly. We also assume 'g' is constant, which is valid for short ranges.

Trajectory of a Projectile: Parabola

  • Path traced by the projectile.
  • Equation: y = (tanθ₀)x - (g / 2u₀²cos²θ₀)x².
  • This equation represents a parabola.

🎙️ The path followed by a projectile is always a parabola, assuming our ideal conditions. The equation of trajectory clearly shows this quadratic relationship between y and x. This parabolic path is symmetrical.

Horizontal Motion in Projectile Motion

  • No horizontal acceleration (ax = 0).
  • Horizontal velocity (vx) remains constant.
  • x = (u cosθ₀)t.

🎙️ Since we neglect air resistance, there's no horizontal force acting on the projectile. This means its horizontal velocity component remains constant throughout its flight. This simplifies the horizontal motion to a simple uniform motion.

Vertical Motion in Projectile Motion

  • Under constant acceleration 'g' (downwards).
  • Vertical velocity (vy) changes.
  • vy = u sinθ₀ - gt; y = (u sinθ₀)t - ½gt².

🎙️ The vertical motion is governed by gravity, causing a constant downward acceleration 'g'. This is analogous to 1D motion under gravity. The vertical velocity changes, becoming zero at the maximum height and then increasing in the downward direction.

Time of Flight (T): How Long in Air?

  • Total time projectile remains in the air.
  • At T, vertical displacement y = 0.
  • Formula: T = (2u sinθ₀) / g.

🎙️ The time of flight is the total duration from projection until the projectile hits the ground again. We derive this by setting the vertical displacement to zero, as the projectile returns to its initial height. Remember the '2u sinθ₀' part.

Total time in air

Maximum Height (H): Peak of Trajectory

  • Highest vertical position reached by projectile.
  • At H, vertical velocity vy = 0.
  • Formula: H = (u² sin²θ₀) / 2g.

🎙️ The maximum height is the peak of the parabolic trajectory. At this point, the projectile momentarily stops moving vertically before starting its descent. We find this by setting the vertical component of velocity to zero. Note the 'sin²θ₀' term.

Horizontal Range (R): How Far it Travels

  • Total horizontal distance covered by projectile.
  • R = (Horizontal velocity) × (Time of Flight).
  • Formula: R = (u² sin2θ₀) / g.

🎙️ The horizontal range is the total horizontal distance the projectile travels before landing. Since horizontal velocity is constant, it's simply velocity multiplied by the total time of flight. The 'sin2θ₀' term is crucial and implies maximum range at 45 degrees.

Worked Example 2: Projectile Motion

  • Problem: A cricket ball is thrown at 30 m/s at 30° above horizontal.
  • Find Time of Flight (T) and Max Height (H). (g=10 m/s²).
  • Solution: T = (2*30*sin30°)/10 = 3 s.
  • H = (30²*sin²30°)/(2*10) = 11.25 m.

🎙️ Let's apply the formulas we just learned. It's important to correctly identify 'u' and 'θ₀' and use the value of 'g'. Make sure your calculator is in degree mode for trigonometric functions. Practice these derivations and calculations for exams.

Uniform Circular Motion (UCM): Basics

  • Motion of an object along a circular path.
  • Speed is constant, but velocity changes (due to changing direction).
  • Requires a continuous force towards the center.

🎙️ Uniform Circular Motion is another important type of motion in a plane. Even though the speed is constant, the velocity is continuously changing because its direction is always changing. This change in velocity implies the presence of an acceleration.

Centripetal Acceleration: Towards the Center

  • Acceleration always directed towards the center of the circle.
  • Perpendicular to the velocity vector.
  • Magnitude: a_c = v²/r = ω²r.

🎙️ This acceleration, called centripetal acceleration, is what causes the change in direction of velocity. It's always perpendicular to the instantaneous velocity and points towards the center of the circular path. Remember the formulas for its magnitude in terms of speed 'v' or angular speed 'ω'.

Worked Example 3: Circular Motion

  • Problem: A car takes a turn of radius 50 m at 10 m/s.
  • Find the centripetal acceleration.
  • Solution: a_c = v²/r = (10)² / 50 = 100 / 50 = 2 m/s².
  • Direction: Towards the center of the turn.

🎙️ This example demonstrates a straightforward application of the centripetal acceleration formula. It's crucial to understand that even at constant speed, there is acceleration due to the change in direction. This acceleration is essential for maintaining circular motion.

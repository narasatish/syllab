What are Conic Sections?

  • Curves formed by the intersection of a plane and a double-napped cone.
  • The angle of the intersecting plane determines the shape.
  • Four main types: Circle, Ellipse, Parabola, Hyperbola.

🎙️ Imagine a double cone, like two ice cream cones joined at their tips. When we slice this cone with a flat plane at different angles, the resulting cross-section is a conic section. Each angle gives us a unique and powerful curve.

Visualizing the Double Cone

  • Two cones joined at their vertices.
  • Extends infinitely in both directions.
  • The axis passes through the vertices and centres of the bases.

🎙️ It's crucial to visualize this double cone. The 'nappes' are the two parts above and below the vertex. The axis is the central line around which the cone is formed. The generating lines are the lines on the surface of the cone.

Types of Conic Sections

  • Circle: Plane perpendicular to the axis.
  • Ellipse: Plane inclined to the axis, but not parallel to a generator.
  • Parabola: Plane parallel to a generator.
  • Hyperbola: Plane parallel to the axis, intersecting both nappes.

🎙️ The key differentiator among these shapes is the angle at which the plane cuts the cone. A circle is formed by a horizontal cut, an ellipse by a slightly tilted cut, a parabola by a cut parallel to a generator, and a hyperbola by a vertical cut through both nappes.

Degenerate Conic Sections

  • Occur when the plane passes through the vertex of the cone.
  • Result in simpler geometric figures.
  • Types: A Point, A Straight Line, A Pair of Intersecting Lines.

🎙️ These are special cases. If the plane cuts exactly at the vertex, you don't get a curve but rather a point, a single line (if the plane is tangent to the cone), or two intersecting lines (if the plane cuts through both nappes at the vertex).

The Circle - Definition

  • A set of all points in a plane.
  • Equidistant from a fixed point called the 'centre'.
  • The fixed distance is called the 'radius'.

🎙️ The circle is the simplest conic section and one you are very familiar with. Its definition is straightforward: every point on the circle is the same distance from its centre. This property is crucial for its equation.

Equation of a Circle

  • Standard form: (x - h)² + (y - k)² = r².
  • Where (h, k) is the centre and 'r' is the radius.
  • If centre is at origin (0,0), equation is x² + y² = r².

🎙️ This equation comes directly from the distance formula. The distance between any point (x,y) on the circle and the centre (h,k) is always equal to the radius 'r'. Squaring both sides removes the square root and gives us the standard form.

Worked Example: Circle

  • Find the equation of a circle with centre (3, -2) and radius 5.
  • Using (x - h)² + (y - k)² = r².
  • Substitute h=3, k=-2, r=5.
  • Result: (x - 3)² + (y + 2)² = 25.

🎙️ Let's apply the formula. Carefully substitute the values for h, k, and r. Remember that (y - (-2)) becomes (y + 2). Squaring the radius gives 25. This is a common type of question you'll encounter.

The Parabola - Definition

  • A set of all points in a plane.
  • Equidistant from a fixed point (focus) and a fixed line (directrix).
  • The vertex is the midpoint between the focus and the directrix.

🎙️ The parabola is a bit more complex than a circle. Its definition involves both a point, the focus, and a line, the directrix. Every point on the parabola maintains an equal distance to both of these. This property gives it its characteristic U-shape.

🏹
Curved path

Standard Equations of Parabola

  • y² = 4ax (opens right, focus (a,0))
  • y² = -4ax (opens left, focus (-a,0))
  • x² = 4ay (opens up, focus (0,a))
  • x² = -4ay (opens down, focus (0,-a))

🎙️ There are four standard forms of the parabola, depending on its orientation. It's crucial to remember which equation corresponds to which direction of opening and where the focus lies. The 'a' value is the distance from the vertex to the focus and also to the directrix.

y²=4ax
y²=-4ax
x²=4ay
x²=-4ay

Key Terms of Parabola

  • Focus (F): The fixed point.
  • Directrix (l): The fixed line.
  • Vertex (V): Midpoint between focus and directrix, on the axis.
  • Latus Rectum: Chord through the focus, perpendicular to the axis, length |4a|.

🎙️ Understanding these terms is vital for solving problems. The vertex is the turning point of the parabola. The latus rectum helps in sketching the parabola accurately, as its length gives an idea of the width of the curve at the focus.

Worked Example: Parabola

  • Find the equation of a parabola with focus (0, 6) and directrix y = -6.
  • Since focus is (0,a) and directrix y=-a, it's x² = 4ay form.
  • Here, a = 6.
  • Result: x² = 4(6)y ⇒ x² = 24y.

🎙️ Identify the type of parabola first. The focus (0,6) on the y-axis and directrix y=-6 tells us it's an upward-opening parabola of the form x² = 4ay. The value of 'a' is directly given by the coordinates of the focus. Substitute and simplify.

The Ellipse - Definition

  • A set of all points in a plane.
  • The sum of whose distances from two fixed points (foci) is constant.
  • Looks like a 'squashed' circle.

🎙️ An ellipse has two foci, unlike a circle which has one centre. The defining property is that if you pick any point on the ellipse and measure its distance to each focus, then add those two distances, the total will always be the same constant value.

🥚
Elongated shape

Standard Equation of Ellipse

  • Horizontal Ellipse: x²/a² + y²/b² = 1 (a > b)
  • Vertical Ellipse: x²/b² + y²/a² = 1 (a > b)
  • a: semi-major axis, b: semi-minor axis, c: distance from centre to focus.
  • Relation: c² = a² - b².

🎙️ There are two standard forms, depending on whether the major axis is horizontal or vertical. Remember 'a' is always greater than 'b'. The relationship c² = a² - b² is crucial for finding the foci. The constant sum of distances is 2a.

Key Terms of Ellipse

  • Foci (F1, F2): The two fixed points.
  • Vertices: Endpoints of the major axis.
  • Major Axis (2a): Longest diameter.
  • Minor Axis (2b): Shortest diameter.
  • Eccentricity (e): c/a (0 < e < 1).

🎙️ The major axis contains the foci and vertices. The minor axis is perpendicular to the major axis, passing through the centre. Eccentricity 'e' tells us how 'squashed' the ellipse is; an 'e' close to 0 means it's nearly a circle, while an 'e' close to 1 means it's very elongated.

Worked Example: Ellipse

  • Find the equation of an ellipse whose foci are (±5, 0) and vertices are (±13, 0).
  • Foci on x-axis means horizontal ellipse: x²/a² + y²/b² = 1.
  • From foci, c = 5. From vertices, a = 13.
  • Use c² = a² - b² to find b: 5² = 13² - b² ⇒ 25 = 169 - b² ⇒ b² = 144.
  • Result: x²/169 + y²/144 = 1.

🎙️ First, identify the orientation (horizontal). Then extract 'c' from the foci and 'a' from the vertices. Use the fundamental relation c² = a² - b² to find b². Finally, substitute a² and b² into the standard equation.

The Hyperbola - Definition

  • A set of all points in a plane.
  • The absolute difference of whose distances from two fixed points (foci) is constant.
  • Consists of two separate, unbounded branches.

🎙️ Similar to the ellipse, the hyperbola also involves two foci. However, instead of the sum of distances being constant, it's the *absolute difference* of the distances that remains constant. This leads to two separate branches that curve away from each other.

⏳

Standard Equation of Hyperbola

  • Horizontal Hyperbola: x²/a² - y²/b² = 1 (foci on x-axis)
  • Vertical Hyperbola: y²/a² - x²/b² = 1 (foci on y-axis)
  • a: semi-transverse axis, b: semi-conjugate axis, c: distance from centre to focus.
  • Relation: c² = a² + b².

🎙️ Notice the minus sign between the terms, which distinguishes it from the ellipse equation. Also, the relation between a, b, and c is c² = a² + b², unlike the ellipse. The 'a' value is associated with the positive term in the equation.

x²/a² - y²/b² = 1
y²/a² - x²/b² = 1
23 / 25

Key Terms of Hyperbola

  • Foci (F1, F2): The two fixed points.
  • Vertices: Endpoints of the transverse axis.
  • Transverse Axis (2a): Connects the two vertices.
  • Conjugate Axis (2b): Perpendicular to transverse axis.
  • Asymptotes: Lines that the hyperbola branches approach but never touch.
  • Eccentricity (e): c/a (e > 1).

🎙️ The transverse axis passes through the foci and vertices. The asymptotes are very important for sketching hyperbolas; they act as guides for the branches. The eccentricity 'e' is always greater than 1 for a hyperbola, indicating its open, unbounded nature.

Worked Example: Hyperbola

  • Find the equation of a hyperbola with foci (±4, 0) and vertices (±2, 0).
  • Foci on x-axis means horizontal hyperbola: x²/a² - y²/b² = 1.
  • From foci, c = 4. From vertices, a = 2.
  • Use c² = a² + b² to find b: 4² = 2² + b² ⇒ 16 = 4 + b² ⇒ b² = 12.
  • Result: x²/4 - y²/12 = 1.

🎙️ Similar to the ellipse example, first identify the orientation. Extract 'c' from foci and 'a' from vertices. Use the hyperbola's specific relation c² = a² + b² to find b². Substitute a² and b² into the standard equation.

Recap & Exam Focus

  • Memorize definitions and standard equations for all conics.
  • Understand the relationship between a, b, c, and e for ellipse and hyperbola.
  • Practice deriving equations from given properties (foci, vertices, directrix, etc.).
  • Focus on common mistakes: signs in equations, correct 'a' and 'b' for ellipse/hyperbola.

🎙️ Conic sections are a high-scoring topic for CBSE and competitive exams like JEE/NEET. A strong grasp of definitions, standard forms, and the interrelations of parameters like 'a', 'b', and 'c' is essential. Practice problem-solving to avoid common pitfalls, especially with signs and orientations.

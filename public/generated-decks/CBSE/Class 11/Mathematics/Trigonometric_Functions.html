Understanding Angles: Degrees & Radians

  • An angle is a measure of rotation of a ray from an initial position to a terminal position.
  • Positive angles are measured anti-clockwise, negative angles clockwise.
  • Two primary units for measuring angles: Degrees and Radians.

🎙️ Angles are fundamental to trigonometry. Think of a clock hand moving; that's rotation. We define angles based on this rotation from a fixed starting line. We'll explore two main ways to quantify this rotation.

The Degree Measure

  • A full rotation is divided into 360 equal parts, each part being one degree (1°).
  • Each degree is divided into 60 minutes (60'), and each minute into 60 seconds (60'').
  • Commonly used in geometry and everyday measurements.

🎙️ The degree system is very intuitive, originating from ancient Babylonian astronomy. When you hear about a 90-degree angle, you're referencing this system. Remember the subdivisions: minutes and seconds.

The Radian Measure

  • One radian is the angle subtended at the center of a circle by an arc equal in length to the radius.
  • It's a ratio of arc length to radius, making it a dimensionless unit.
  • More natural for mathematical analysis, especially in calculus.

🎙️ Radian measure might seem abstract at first, but it's incredibly powerful in higher mathematics. Imagine a string the length of the circle's radius. If you lay that string along the circumference, the angle it makes at the center is one radian.

Converting Degrees to Radians

  • The relationship: π radians = 180°.
  • To convert degrees to radians, multiply by π/180.
  • Example: 90° = 90 * (π/180) = π/2 radians.

🎙️ This conversion factor is essential. Always remember that π radians is equivalent to 180 degrees. So, if you have an angle in degrees, simply multiply it by π over 180 to get its radian equivalent.

Converting Radians to Degrees

  • The relationship: 1 radian = 180°/π.
  • To convert radians to degrees, multiply by 180/π.
  • Example: π/4 radians = (π/4) * (180/π) = 45°.

🎙️ Conversely, if you have an angle in radians and need it in degrees, you multiply by 180 over π. Notice how the π in the numerator and denominator often cancel out, simplifying the calculation.

Review: Trigonometric Ratios in Right Triangles

  • SOH CAH TOA: Sine = Opposite/Hypotenuse, Cosine = Adjacent/Hypotenuse, Tangent = Opposite/Adjacent.
  • Cosecant (csc) = 1/sin, Secant (sec) = 1/cos, Cotangent (cot) = 1/tan.
  • These ratios are constant for a given angle in any right-angled triangle.

🎙️ You've studied these ratios in Class 10. They form the foundation. Remember the mnemonic SOH CAH TOA, which helps recall the definitions quickly. These ratios are always defined relative to an acute angle in a right triangle.

The Unit Circle: Expanding Trig Functions

  • A circle with radius 1 unit centered at the origin (0,0).
  • Allows us to define trigonometric functions for *any* real angle, not just acute angles.
  • For a point P(x,y) on the unit circle, x = cos θ and y = sin θ.

🎙️ The unit circle is a game-changer. It extends the definition of sine, cosine, and tangent beyond right triangles, enabling us to work with angles greater than 90 degrees or even negative angles. It's a visual tool for understanding the periodic nature of these functions.

Signs of Trigonometric Functions in Quadrants

  • The coordinate plane is divided into four quadrants.
  • The sign of sin, cos, tan depends on the quadrant in which the angle's terminal arm lies.
  • Understanding signs is crucial for solving equations and evaluating expressions.

🎙️ As we move around the unit circle, the x and y coordinates change signs. Since sine is related to y and cosine to x, their signs will vary across the quadrants. This is a very important concept for Class 11.

The CAST Rule: Remembering Signs

  • A mnemonic to remember which functions are positive in each quadrant.
  • Quadrant I (All): All functions (sin, cos, tan) are positive.
  • Quadrant II (Sine): Only sin and cosec are positive.
  • Quadrant III (Tan): Only tan and cot are positive.
  • Quadrant IV (Cos): Only cos and sec are positive.

🎙️ The CAST rule is an indispensable tool. Starting from Quadrant IV and going anti-clockwise, 'C' for Cosine, 'A' for All, 'S' for Sine, 'T' for Tangent. This helps you quickly determine the sign of any trigonometric function for a given angle.

Example: Find the Sign of sin(210°)

  • Locate 210° on the unit circle.
  • 210° lies in the third quadrant (between 180° and 270°).
  • According to the CAST rule, in Quadrant III, only Tangent is positive.
  • Therefore, sin(210°) is negative.

🎙️ Let's put the CAST rule into practice. First, visualize where 210 degrees falls. Then, recall which functions are positive in that specific quadrant. This will directly tell you the sign of sin(210°).

Trigonometric Functions of Allied Angles

  • Angles related by multiples of 90° or π/2 (e.g., 90±θ, 180±θ, 270±θ, 360±θ).
  • Rules for changing function and sign: 'No change' for 180°/360° multiples, 'Change' for 90°/270° multiples.
  • Use the CAST rule for the sign of the resulting function.

🎙️ Allied angles allow us to simplify trigonometric expressions involving larger or negative angles. Remember the two key parts: deciding if the function changes (sin to cos, tan to cot) and then determining the correct sign using the original angle's quadrant.

Fundamental Trigonometric Identities

  • Equations that are true for all values of the variables for which the expressions are defined.
  • Key identities: sin²θ + cos²θ = 1; 1 + tan²θ = sec²θ; 1 + cot²θ = cosec²θ.
  • Used to simplify expressions and prove other trigonometric relations.

🎙️ These three fundamental identities are the building blocks of proving more complex identities. They are derived directly from the Pythagorean theorem on the unit circle. Master them, as they appear frequently in problem-solving.

✅
Always True

Example: Prove (sin θ + cos θ)² = 1 + 2 sin θ cos θ

  • Start with the Left Hand Side (LHS): (sin θ + cos θ)².
  • Expand the square: sin²θ + cos²θ + 2 sin θ cos θ.
  • Apply the identity sin²θ + cos²θ = 1.
  • LHS = 1 + 2 sin θ cos θ = RHS. Hence Proved.

🎙️ Proving identities is a common exam question. Always start with one side, usually the more complex one, and manipulate it using known identities and algebraic rules until it matches the other side. Don't try to work on both sides simultaneously.

Domain & Range: Sine and Cosine Functions

  • Domain of sin(x) and cos(x): All real numbers (R). Angles can be any value.
  • Range of sin(x) and cos(x): [-1, 1]. The values oscillate between -1 and 1.
  • This is evident from the unit circle, where x and y coordinates never exceed 1 or go below -1.

🎙️ Understanding domain and range is critical for sketching graphs and solving inequalities. Sine and Cosine are well-behaved, always staying within the bounds of -1 and 1, regardless of how large or small the angle is.

Domain & Range: Tangent and Cotangent Functions

  • Domain of tan(x): R, except x = (2n+1)π/2, n ∈ Z (where cos x = 0).
  • Range of tan(x): All real numbers (R).
  • Domain of cot(x): R, except x = nπ, n ∈ Z (where sin x = 0).
  • Range of cot(x): All real numbers (R).

🎙️ Tangent and cotangent have restrictions on their domains because they involve division by cosine and sine respectively. Where the denominator is zero, the function is undefined, leading to vertical asymptotes in their graphs. Their ranges, however, span all real numbers.

Domain & Range: Secant and Cosecant Functions

  • Domain of sec(x): R, except x = (2n+1)π/2, n ∈ Z (where cos x = 0).
  • Range of sec(x): (-∞, -1] U [1, ∞). It never takes values between -1 and 1.
  • Domain of cosec(x): R, except x = nπ, n ∈ Z (where sin x = 0).
  • Range of cosec(x): (-∞, -1] U [1, ∞).

🎙️ Secant and cosecant also have restricted domains due to division by zero. Their ranges are particularly interesting; they never fall between -1 and 1. This is because they are reciprocals of sine and cosine, whose maximum absolute value is 1.

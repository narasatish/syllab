Understanding Angles: More Than Just a Corner!

  • An angle is a measure of rotation of a given ray about its initial point.
  • The initial position of the ray is called the initial side.
  • The final position of the ray after rotation is called the terminal side.
  • The point of rotation is called the vertex.

🎙️ Think of the hands of a clock or a rotating fan blade. The amount it turns from its starting position is an angle. It's not just about static corners; it's about dynamic rotation.

Positive & Negative Angles: Clockwise vs. Anti-clockwise

  • If the rotation of the ray is anti-clockwise, the angle is positive.
  • If the rotation of the ray is clockwise, the angle is negative.
  • The direction of rotation is crucial in defining the angle's sign.

🎙️ Imagine a car turning. Turning left (anti-clockwise) is a positive turn, while turning right (clockwise) is a negative turn. This convention helps us accurately describe angular displacement.

Measuring Angles: The Degree System

  • In the degree system, one complete revolution is divided into 360 equal parts, each called a degree (1°).
  • Each degree is subdivided into 60 minutes (1° = 60').
  • Each minute is further subdivided into 60 seconds (1' = 60'').

🎙️ The degree system is ancient and widely used, especially in geometry and everyday measurements. It's intuitive to think of a circle as 360 degrees, allowing for precise divisions.

The Radian System: Angle in Terms of Arc Length

  • A radian is the angle subtended at the centre of a circle by an arc whose length is equal to the radius of the circle.
  • One complete revolution is equal to 2π radians.
  • The radian is the SI unit for angle measurement and is crucial in higher mathematics.

🎙️ The radian system connects angles directly to the geometry of a circle, particularly arc length. This makes it more natural for calculus and physics applications, where 'π' frequently appears.

Converting Between Degrees and Radians

  • The relationship between degrees and radians is: π radians = 180°.
  • To convert degrees to radians, multiply by π/180.
  • To convert radians to degrees, multiply by 180/π.

🎙️ This conversion is a fundamental skill. Remember that π radians is exactly half a circle, just like 180 degrees. This equivalence is your key to switching between the two systems.

Extending Ratios to Functions: The Unit Circle

  • A unit circle is a circle with radius 1 unit, centered at the origin (0,0) of the coordinate plane.
  • For any point P(x, y) on the unit circle corresponding to an angle θ, we define:
  • cos θ = x-coordinate, sin θ = y-coordinate.
  • This extends trigonometric ratios to any angle, positive or negative.

🎙️ The unit circle is a powerful tool. It allows us to visualize trigonometric functions for any angle, not just acute angles in a right triangle. The coordinates of a point on this circle directly give us the sine and cosine values.

Signs of Trig Functions: Quadrant I (All Positive)

  • In the first quadrant (0° to 90° or 0 to π/2 radians), both x and y coordinates are positive.
  • Therefore, all trigonometric functions (sin, cos, tan, cosec, sec, cot) are positive.

🎙️ Think of the first quadrant as the 'starting point' where everything is straightforward. Both sine (y-coordinate) and cosine (x-coordinate) are positive, leading to all other functions being positive as well.

➕➕➕
All Positive

Signs of Trig Functions: Quadrant II (Sine & Cosec Positive)

  • In the second quadrant (90° to 180° or π/2 to π radians), the x-coordinate is negative, and the y-coordinate is positive.
  • Hence, sin θ and cosec θ (reciprocal of sin) are positive. All other functions are negative.

🎙️ Moving into the second quadrant, we cross the y-axis. While the y-values (sine) remain positive, the x-values (cosine) become negative. This changes the signs of most functions.

⬆️⬆️
Sine Positive

Signs of Trig Functions: Quadrant III (Tangent & Cotangent Positive)

  • In the third quadrant (180° to 270° or π to 3π/2 radians), both x and y coordinates are negative.
  • Since tan θ = y/x, and both are negative, tan θ and cot θ (reciprocal of tan) are positive. Others are negative.

🎙️ In the third quadrant, both x and y coordinates are negative. When you divide a negative by a negative, you get a positive, which explains why tangent and cotangent are positive here.

↔️↔️
Tangent Positive

Signs of Trig Functions: Quadrant IV (Cosine & Secant Positive)

  • In the fourth quadrant (270° to 360° or 3π/2 to 2π radians), the x-coordinate is positive, and the y-coordinate is negative.
  • Hence, cos θ and sec θ (reciprocal of cos) are positive. All other functions are negative.

🎙️ Finally, in the fourth quadrant, we see the x-coordinate (cosine) return to positive values, while the y-coordinate (sine) remains negative. This pattern completes the cycle of signs.

Remembering Signs: The CAST Rule

  • A simple mnemonic to remember the signs of trigonometric functions in each quadrant.
  • Starting from Quadrant IV and moving anti-clockwise:
  • C = Cosine positive (Quadrant IV)
  • A = All positive (Quadrant I)
  • S = Sine positive (Quadrant II)
  • T = Tangent positive (Quadrant III)

🎙️ The CAST rule is an invaluable tool for quickly determining the sign of any trigonometric function in any quadrant. Visualize it on your coordinate plane, starting from the bottom right and moving counter-clockwise.

Worked Example: Determine the sign of sin(210°)

🎙️ Let's apply the CAST rule to a specific example. First, locate the angle's quadrant, then recall which functions are positive in that quadrant. This systematic approach ensures accuracy.

1Identify Quadrant: 210° lies between 180° and 270°, so it's in Quadrant III.
2Apply CAST Rule: In Quadrant III, only Tangent (and Cotangent) are positive.
3State Sign: Therefore, sin(210°) is negative.

Fundamental Trigonometric Identities

  • sin²θ + cos²θ = 1
  • 1 + tan²θ = sec²θ
  • 1 + cot²θ = cosec²θ

🎙️ These three identities are the backbone of trigonometry. They are always true, regardless of the angle θ, and are frequently used to simplify expressions and solve equations. Make sure to memorize them thoroughly.

Domain and Range: Sine and Cosine Functions

  • For sin x: Domain = R (all real numbers), Range = [-1, 1].
  • For cos x: Domain = R (all real numbers), Range = [-1, 1].

🎙️ The domain tells us what input values the function can take, while the range tells us what output values it can produce. Sine and cosine functions can take any real angle, but their values always stay between -1 and 1.

Domain and Range: Tangent and Cotangent Functions

  • For tan x: Domain = R - { (2n+1)π/2, where n ∈ Z }, Range = R.
  • For cot x: Domain = R - { nπ, where n ∈ Z }, Range = R.

🎙️ Unlike sine and cosine, tangent and cotangent have vertical asymptotes where they are undefined. This is because tan x = sin x / cos x (undefined when cos x = 0) and cot x = cos x / sin x (undefined when sin x = 0).

Domain and Range: Secant and Cosecant Functions

  • For sec x: Domain = R - { (2n+1)π/2, where n ∈ Z }, Range = R - (-1, 1).
  • For cosec x: Domain = R - { nπ, where n ∈ Z }, Range = R - (-1, 1).

🎙️ Secant and cosecant are reciprocals of cosine and sine, respectively. Their domains are restricted where cosine or sine are zero. Their ranges are also unique, excluding values strictly between -1 and 1.

Periodicity of Trigonometric Functions

  • A function f(x) is periodic if f(x + T) = f(x) for some positive number T, called the period.
  • sin x, cos x, sec x, cosec x have a period of 2π.
  • tan x, cot x have a period of π.

🎙️ Periodicity means that the function's values repeat after a certain interval. This is evident when you go around the unit circle. After 360 degrees (2π radians), you're back to the same point, and the values repeat.

🎙️ When dealing with angles greater than 360 degrees, use the periodicity property to reduce them to an equivalent angle within 0 to 360 degrees. This simplifies the evaluation process significantly.

1Reduce angle: 765° = 2 × 360° + 45° (using periodicity)
2Find equivalent angle: sin(765°) = sin(360° × 2 + 45°) = sin(45°)

Common Mistakes in Trigonometric Functions

  • Incorrectly applying signs of functions in different quadrants.
  • Errors in converting between degrees and radians.
  • Confusing the domain and range of different functions.
  • Forgetting or misapplying fundamental trigonometric identities.

🎙️ Be mindful of these common pitfalls. A small error in sign or conversion can lead to a completely wrong answer. Double-check your work, especially when dealing with quadrant rules and unit conversions.

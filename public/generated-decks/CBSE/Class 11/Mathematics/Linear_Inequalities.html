Equations vs. Inequalities: A Quick Recall

  • Equations use an '=' sign (e.g., 2x + 3 = 7).
  • Inequalities use '<', '>', '≤', or '≥' signs (e.g., 2x + 3 < 7).
  • Solutions to equations are specific values; solutions to inequalities are ranges or regions.

🎙️ Remember how we solved linear equations? Linear inequalities are similar but introduce key differences in how we handle them and interpret their solutions. It's crucial to understand this distinction from the outset.

Types of Inequalities: Strict and Slack

  • Strict Inequalities: Use '<' (less than) or '>' (greater than). Solutions do NOT include the boundary value.
  • Slack Inequalities: Use '≤' (less than or equal to) or '≥' (greater than or equal to). Solutions INCLUDE the boundary value.

🎙️ Understanding the difference between strict and slack inequalities is vital, especially when representing solutions on a number line or graphing in two variables. It determines whether a boundary is included or excluded.

Rules for Solving Inequalities: Part 1 (Addition/Subtraction)

  • Rule 1: Adding the same number to both sides does not change the inequality sign.
  • Rule 2: Subtracting the same number from both sides does not change the inequality sign.
  • These operations work just like with equations.

🎙️ The first set of rules for solving inequalities is quite intuitive and mirrors what you already know from solving equations. You can add or subtract any number from both sides, and the inequality's direction remains the same.

🎙️ These rules also align with equation solving. As long as you're multiplying or dividing by a positive number, the inequality sign stays exactly where it is. No surprises here!

Critical Rule: Multiplying/Dividing by a Negative Number

  • Rule 5: Multiplying both sides by a negative number REVERSES the inequality sign.
  • Rule 6: Dividing both sides by a negative number REVERSES the inequality sign.
  • This is the most common mistake students make!

🎙️ This is the golden rule, and it's absolutely crucial for your success in this chapter. Whenever you multiply or divide by a negative number, you MUST flip the inequality sign. Forgetting this will lead to incorrect solutions.

Representing Solutions on a Number Line

  • For '<' or '>', use an open circle (or hollow dot) at the boundary value.
  • For '≤' or '≥', use a closed circle (or solid dot) at the boundary value.
  • Shade the region that satisfies the inequality.

🎙️ A number line is an excellent way to visualize the solution set. The type of circle you draw at the boundary point is determined by whether the inequality is strict or slack. Always remember to shade the correct region.

Linear Inequalities in Two Variables: Introduction

  • Involve two variables, usually 'x' and 'y' (e.g., ax + by < c).

  • Graphical representation is the primary method for solving.

🎙️ Now, let's extend our understanding to inequalities with two variables. Unlike one-variable inequalities where solutions are intervals on a number line, two-variable inequalities have solutions that are entire regions on a graph.

Steps to Graphing: Part 1 (The Boundary Line)

  • Step 1: Replace the inequality sign with an '=' sign to get the equation of the boundary line.
  • Step 2: Plot this line on the Cartesian plane.
  • Step 3: Use a dashed line for strict inequalities (<, >); use a solid line for slack inequalities (≤, ≥).

🎙️ The first step is to identify and draw the boundary line. This line separates the plane into two half-planes. The type of line – dashed or solid – is crucial as it indicates whether the points on the line are part of the solution.

Steps to Graphing: Part 2 (Shading the Solution Region)

  • Step 4: Choose a test point NOT on the boundary line (e.g., (0,0) if it's not on the line).
  • Step 5: Substitute the test point's coordinates into the ORIGINAL inequality.
  • Step 6: If the inequality is true, shade the region containing the test point. If false, shade the other region.

🎙️ Once the boundary line is drawn, we need to determine which side of the line represents the solution. A test point helps us do this efficiently. The origin (0,0) is often the easiest test point to use, unless the line passes through it.

Worked Example 3: Graphing x + y < 5

Beware! Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Forgetting to reverse the inequality sign when multiplying/dividing by a negative number.
  • Incorrectly drawing the boundary line (dashed vs. solid).
  • Shading the wrong region after testing a point.

🎙️ These are the most frequent errors students make. Pay close attention to these points, especially during exams. A single mistake can lead to an entirely incorrect solution, so practice is key to avoiding them.

Systems of Linear Inequalities: Finding Common Solutions

  • Involve two or more linear inequalities simultaneously.
  • The solution is the region that satisfies ALL inequalities at the same time.
  • Always solved graphically, finding the overlapping shaded region.

🎙️ When you have multiple conditions or constraints, you're dealing with a system of inequalities. The solution to such a system is the area on the graph where all individual inequalities overlap, forming a feasible region.

Steps to Solve Systems of Inequalities Graphically

  • Step 1: Graph each inequality individually on the same coordinate plane.
  • Step 2: Carefully determine the correct boundary line (dashed/solid) and shaded region for each.
  • Step 3: Identify the region where all shaded areas overlap. This is the feasible (solution) region.

🎙️ Solving systems graphically requires precision. Graph each inequality one by one, clearly marking its solution. The final solution is the area that is common to all individual solutions.

2Shade each

Worked Example 5: Solving a System (x ≥ 0, y ≥ 0, x + y ≤ 4)

  • Graph x=0 (y-axis) and y=0 (x-axis) as solid lines, shading the first quadrant.
  • Graph x + y = 4 as a solid line, shading towards the origin.
  • The feasible region is the triangle formed by these three lines in the first quadrant.

🎙️ This is a classic example often seen in linear programming. The conditions x ≥ 0 and y ≥ 0 restrict us to the first quadrant. Adding x + y ≤ 4 then cuts off a triangular region, which is our solution.

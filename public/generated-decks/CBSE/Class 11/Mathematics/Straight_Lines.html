  • Has constant direction

Calculating Distance Between Two Points

  • Based on Pythagorean theorem
  • Distance d = √[(x₂-x₁)² + (y₂-y₁)²]
  • Always a positive value

🎙️ The distance formula is crucial for finding the length of a line segment. It's a direct application of the Pythagorean theorem in a coordinate plane. Remember, the order of (x₁, y₁) and (x₂, y₂) doesn't matter as we square the differences.

↔️
Measuring Length
6 / 25

Example: Applying Distance Formula

  • Find distance between P(2,3) and Q(5,7).
  • d = √[(5-2)² + (7-3)²]
  • d = √[3² + 4²] = √[9+16] = √25 = 5 units

🎙️ Let's work through an example to solidify our understanding. Always write down the formula first, substitute the values carefully, and simplify step-by-step to avoid errors. This methodical approach is key for exams.

Section Formula: Internal Division

  • Point P divides line segment AB internally
  • Ratio m:n
  • P(x,y) = [(nx₁+mx₂)/(m+n), (ny₁+my₂)/(m+n)]

🎙️ The section formula helps us find the coordinates of a point that divides a line segment in a given ratio. For internal division, the point lies *between* the two endpoints. Pay close attention to the m and n terms with their respective coordinates.

➗
Dividing a Segment

Section Formula: External Division

  • Point P divides line segment AB externally
  • Ratio m:n
  • P(x,y) = [(nx₁-mx₂)/(n-m), (ny₁-my₂)/(n-m)]

🎙️ For external division, the point P lies *outside* the line segment AB, on the line extended. Notice the change in signs in the formula compared to internal division. This distinction is vital for accurate calculations.

Mid-point: A Special Case

  • Divides segment in ratio 1:1
  • P(x,y) = [(x₁+x₂)/2, (y₁+y₂)/2]
  • Average of the coordinates

🎙️ The midpoint formula is a simplified version of the section formula where the ratio m:n is 1:1. It simply gives us the average of the x-coordinates and the average of the y-coordinates. This is a very commonly used formula.

📍

Example: Using Section Formula

  • Find point dividing A(1,-3) and B(-4,7)
  • Internally in ratio 3:2
  • x = (2*1 + 3*(-4))/(3+2) = -10/5 = -2
  • y = (2*(-3) + 3*7)/(3+2) = 15/5 = 3
  • Point is (-2,3)

🎙️ Let's apply the internal section formula. Be careful with the signs of coordinates and the ratio values. It's always a good practice to label your points (x₁, y₁) and (x₂, y₂) and the ratio (m:n) clearly before substituting.

Area of a Triangle using Coordinates

  • Vertices (x₁,y₁), (x₂,y₂), (x₃,y₃)
  • Area = ½ |x₁(y₂-y₃) + x₂(y₃-y₁) + x₃(y₁-y₂)|
  • Absolute value ensures positive area

🎙️ This formula allows us to calculate the area of a triangle when its vertices' coordinates are known. The vertical bars denote absolute value, as area must always be positive. Remember the cyclic order of subscripts for y-coordinates.

Condition for Collinearity

  • Three points are collinear if they lie on the same straight line
  • Area of triangle formed by them is zero
  • Can also use slope method

🎙️ If three points are collinear, they cannot form a triangle, hence the area formed by them will be zero. This is a powerful application of the area formula. We'll also see how slopes can be used to check for collinearity later.

➖
On the Same Line

Example: Area and Collinearity

  • Find area of triangle with vertices (1,1), (2,3), (4,5)
  • Area = ½ |1(3-5) + 2(5-1) + 4(1-3)|
  • Area = ½ |-2 + 8 - 8| = ½ |-2| = 1 unit²

🎙️ Let's calculate the area for these points. If the result was zero, the points would be collinear. Practice these calculations carefully, as a small sign error can lead to an incorrect answer.

Understanding the Slope of a Line

  • Measure of a line's steepness or inclination
  • Ratio of 'rise' to 'run'
  • Represented by 'm'

🎙️ The slope, or gradient, is a fundamental concept for straight lines. It tells us how much the y-coordinate changes for a given change in the x-coordinate. A positive slope means an upward trend, a negative slope means a downward trend.

↗️
How Steep?

Calculating Slope: Two Ways

  • Using two points: m = (y₂-y₁)/(x₂-x₁)
  • Using angle with x-axis: m = tan θ
  • θ is the angle the line makes with positive x-axis

🎙️ You can find the slope if you have two points on the line, or if you know the angle it makes with the positive x-axis. Both methods are equally valid and useful depending on the information given in a problem. Remember tan 90° is undefined.

Slopes of Parallel and Perpendicular Lines

  • Parallel lines have equal slopes (m₁ = m₂)
  • Perpendicular lines have slopes whose product is -1 (m₁m₂ = -1)
  • Vertical lines have undefined slope

🎙️ These conditions are extremely important for solving problems involving geometric properties of lines. Understanding them allows us to determine the relationship between two lines just by looking at their slopes. Pay attention to the special case of vertical lines.

Example: Slopes and Line Relations

  • Line 1: (1,2) & (3,4); Line 2: (0,1) & (2,3)
  • m₁ = (4-2)/(3-1) = 2/2 = 1
  • m₂ = (3-1)/(2-0) = 2/2 = 1
  • Since m₁ = m₂, lines are parallel.

🎙️ Let's test the relationship between two lines using their slopes. Calculate each slope carefully. If the slopes are equal, the lines are parallel; if their product is -1, they are perpendicular. This is a common exam question.

Point-Slope Form of a Line

  • Given a point (x₁,y₁) and slope m
  • Equation: y - y₁ = m(x - x₁)
  • Versatile for many problems

🎙️ This form is incredibly useful when you know a single point on the line and its slope. It's often the first step in finding the general equation of a line. Always substitute the known values carefully into the formula.

✍️
Start with a Point
20 / 25

Two-Point Form of a Line

  • Given two points (x₁,y₁) and (x₂,y₂)
  • Equation: (y - y₁) = [(y₂-y₁)/(x₂-x₁)](x - x₁)
  • Essentially point-slope with slope derived

🎙️ If you have two points, you can first find the slope and then use the point-slope form. Or, you can directly use this two-point form, which combines both steps. Choose the method you find most comfortable and efficient.

✌️

Slope-Intercept Form of a Line

  • Given slope m and y-intercept c
  • Equation: y = mx + c
  • Easy to identify slope and y-intercept

🎙️ This form is perhaps the most recognized and simplest to use for graphing. The 'm' directly gives you the slope, and 'c' tells you where the line crosses the y-axis. It's very intuitive for visualizing the line's properties.

📈

Intercept Form of a Line

  • Given x-intercept 'a' and y-intercept 'b'
  • Equation: x/a + y/b = 1
  • Useful when intercepts are known

🎙️ When you know where the line crosses both the x and y axes, the intercept form is very direct. It provides a quick way to write the equation without needing to calculate the slope explicitly. Remember 'a' and 'b' are the intercepts, not points.

↔️
Crossing the Axes

Normal Form of a Line

  • Perpendicular distance 'p' from origin to line
  • Angle 'α' made by normal with x-axis
  • Equation: x cos α + y sin α = p

🎙️ The normal form is a bit more advanced but powerful. It uses the perpendicular distance from the origin to the line and the angle this perpendicular makes with the x-axis. It's particularly useful in problems involving distances from the origin.

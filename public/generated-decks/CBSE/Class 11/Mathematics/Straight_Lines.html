The Distance Formula

  • Distance between P(x1, y1) and Q(x2, y2)
  • Formula: √[(x2 - x1)² + (y2 - y1)²]
  • Derived from Pythagorean theorem

🎙️ The first key formula we encounter is the distance formula. It allows us to calculate the length of the line segment connecting any two given points in the coordinate plane. This formula is a direct application of the Pythagorean theorem, which you've studied before.

📏

Worked Example: Distance Formula

  • Find distance between A(3, -2) and B(-1, 1).
  • Steps: Identify (x1,y1), (x2,y2). Substitute into formula.
  • Calculation: √[(-1-3)² + (1-(-2))²] = √[(-4)² + (3)²] = √(16+9) = √25 = 5 units.

🎙️ Let's apply the distance formula to a practical example. Always identify your coordinates carefully and be mindful of negative signs during substitution. A common mistake is calculation error with squares and signs. The distance is always a positive value.

Section Formula (Internal Division)

  • Point P(x, y) divides line segment AB internally
  • Ratio m:n, where A(x1, y1) and B(x2, y2)
  • P = [(nx1 + mx2)/(m+n), (ny1 + my2)/(m+n)]

🎙️ The section formula helps us find the coordinates of a point that divides a line segment in a given ratio. For internal division, the point lies *between* the two endpoints. Remember the 'cross-multiplication' pattern for m and n with the coordinates.

Special Case: Mid-point Formula

  • Mid-point divides segment in ratio 1:1
  • Set m=1, n=1 in section formula
  • Mid-point = [(x1 + x2)/2, (y1 + y2)/2]

🎙️ The mid-point formula is a very important special case of the section formula. When a point divides a segment exactly in half, the ratio m:n becomes 1:1. This simplifies the formula significantly, making it easy to calculate the center of any line segment.

Section Formula (External Division)

  • Point P(x, y) divides line segment AB externally
  • Ratio m:n, where A(x1, y1) and B(x2, y2)
  • P = [(nx1 - mx2)/(n-m), (ny1 - my2)/(n-m)]

🎙️ Unlike internal division, external division means the point lies *outside* the line segment, on the line extended. The formula is very similar, but notice the minus signs in both the numerator and denominator. This distinction is crucial for correct application.

↔️
Extending a Line

Worked Example: Section Formula

  • Find point dividing A(1, -3) and B(-2, 5) in ratio 3:1 internally.
  • Use formula: P = [(1*1 + 3*(-2))/(3+1), (1*(-3) + 3*5)/(3+1)]
  • P = [(1-6)/4, (-3+15)/4] = [-5/4, 12/4] = [-5/4, 3]

🎙️ Let's work through an example for internal division. Carefully identify m, n, and the coordinates. Pay close attention to the order of points and the ratio. If the question specifies external division, remember to use the formula with the minus signs.

Area of a Triangle using Coordinates

  • Given vertices A(x1, y1), B(x2, y2), C(x3, y3)
  • Area = 1/2 |x1(y2-y3) + x2(y3-y1) + x3(y1-y2)|
  • Absolute value ensures positive area

🎙️ We can calculate the area of a triangle if we know the coordinates of its three vertices. This formula is derived using determinants or by dividing the triangle into trapezoids. Always remember to take the absolute value at the end, as area cannot be negative.

Condition for Collinearity of Three Points

  • Three points A, B, C are collinear if they lie on the same straight line
  • If A, B, C are collinear, area of triangle ABC is zero
  • Can also check if slopes of AB and BC are equal

🎙️ Collinearity is a fundamental concept. If three points are collinear, they essentially form a 'degenerate' triangle with no area. Therefore, setting the area of the triangle formed by these points to zero is a powerful way to check for collinearity. Alternatively, you can compare slopes.

↔️
Points on a Line

Worked Example: Area & Collinearity

  • Check if (1, -1), (2, 0), (4, 2) are collinear.
  • Area = 1/2 |1(0-2) + 2(2-(-1)) + 4(-1-0)|
  • Area = 1/2 |-2 + 2(3) + 4(-1)| = 1/2 |-2 + 6 - 4| = 1/2 |0| = 0.
  • Since Area = 0, points are collinear.

🎙️ Here's how to apply the area formula to check for collinearity. Substitute the coordinates carefully and perform the calculations step-by-step. If the result is zero, the points are collinear; otherwise, they form a triangle with a non-zero area.

Slope of a Line (Gradient)

  • Measures the steepness or inclination of a line
  • m = (y2 - y1) / (x2 - x1) = tanθ
  • Positive, negative, zero, or undefined slope

🎙️ The slope, often denoted by 'm', is a crucial characteristic of a straight line. It tells us how much the line rises or falls for a given horizontal change. It's the tangent of the angle the line makes with the positive x-axis. Understand the different types of slopes.

Angle Between Two Lines

  • If lines have slopes m1 and m2
  • tanθ = |(m1 - m2) / (1 + m1m2)|
  • θ is the acute angle between the lines

🎙️ When two lines intersect, they form two pairs of vertically opposite angles. This formula helps us find the acute angle between them using their slopes. Remember the absolute value ensures we get the acute angle. If tanθ is undefined, the lines are perpendicular.

Parallel and Perpendicular Lines

  • Parallel Lines: Slopes are equal (m1 = m2)
  • Perpendicular Lines: Product of slopes is -1 (m1 * m2 = -1)
  • Vertical lines are parallel, horizontal lines are parallel

🎙️ These are fundamental conditions. Parallel lines never intersect and have the same inclination, hence equal slopes. Perpendicular lines intersect at a 90-degree angle, and their slopes have a special inverse negative relationship. Be careful with vertical lines, as their slope is undefined.

Worked Example: Slopes and Angles

  • Find angle between lines with slopes m1=2 and m2=1/3.
  • tanθ = |(2 - 1/3) / (1 + 2 * 1/3)| = |(5/3) / (1 + 2/3)| = |(5/3) / (5/3)| = 1
  • So, θ = 45 degrees.

🎙️ Let's calculate the angle between two lines using their given slopes. Substitute the values carefully into the formula. Recognizing common tangent values like 1 for 45 degrees or undefined for 90 degrees is helpful. Always check for common mistakes in arithmetic.

🎙️ A straight line can be represented mathematically in several different forms, each useful depending on the information given. We will explore five key forms that are essential for solving various problems related to straight lines. Each form provides a unique perspective.

Point-Slope Form

  • If line passes through (x1, y1) with slope m
  • Equation: y - y1 = m(x - x1)
  • Useful when a point and slope are known

🎙️ The point-slope form is incredibly versatile. If you know any point on the line and its slope, you can immediately write its equation. This is often the first step in deriving other forms or solving problems where slope and a point are given directly.

1Identify Point (x1, y1)
2Identify Slope (m)
3Substitute into formula
20 / 25

Two-Point Form

  • If line passes through (x1, y1) and (x2, y2)
  • Equation: (y - y1) = [(y2 - y1)/(x2 - x1)](x - x1)
  • Essentially point-slope form with calculated slope

🎙️ When two points on a line are given, you can first calculate the slope using the slope formula. Then, using either of the given points and the calculated slope, you can apply the point-slope form to get the equation. This is a very common scenario in problems.

1Identify Two Points
2Calculate Slope (m)
3Use Point-Slope Form
21 / 25

Slope-Intercept Form

  • Equation: y = mx + c
  • m is the slope, c is the y-intercept
  • y-intercept is the point (0, c) where line crosses y-axis

🎙️ The slope-intercept form is perhaps the most recognizable form. It directly gives you the slope and the y-intercept of the line. This form is particularly useful for graphing lines quickly and for comparing slopes and y-intercepts of different lines.

1Identify Slope (m)
2Identify Y-intercept (c)
3Substitute into y = mx + c
22 / 25

Intercept Form

  • Equation: x/a + y/b = 1
  • a is x-intercept, b is y-intercept
  • x-intercept is (a, 0), y-intercept is (0, b)

🎙️ The intercept form is ideal when you know where the line crosses both the x-axis and the y-axis. 'a' represents the x-intercept and 'b' represents the y-intercept. This form is very symmetrical and straightforward when intercept information is available.

1Identify X-intercept (a)
2Identify Y-intercept (b)
3Substitute into x/a + y/b = 1
23 / 25

Normal Form of a Line

  • Equation: x cosα + y sinα = p
  • p is perpendicular distance from origin to line
  • α is angle normal makes with positive x-axis

🎙️ The normal form is less intuitive but powerful. It uses the perpendicular distance from the origin to the line (p) and the angle (α) this perpendicular makes with the x-axis. It's particularly useful in problems involving distances from the origin or properties of perpendiculars.

1Perpendicular distance (p)
2Angle of normal (α)
3Substitute into x cosα + y sinα = p
24 / 25

General Equation of a Straight Line

  • Any straight line can be written as Ax + By + C = 0
  • A, B, C are constants, A and B not both zero
  • All other forms can be converted to this general form

🎙️ This is the most universal way to represent a straight line. All the specific forms we discussed can be rearranged into this general form. It's important to be able to convert between the general form and other forms to solve different types of problems efficiently.

Understanding 3D Geometry

  • Study of shapes and figures in three dimensions.
  • Adds depth (z-axis) to the familiar x and y axes.
  • Essential for fields like engineering, physics, and computer graphics.

🎙️ In 2D geometry, we deal with flat shapes on a plane. 3D geometry extends this by introducing a third dimension, allowing us to describe objects that have length, breadth, and height, just like everything around us.

2D vs. 3D Space

  • 2D: Points on a plane (x, y).
  • 3D: Points in space (x, y, z).
  • 2D: Length, Width. 3D: Length, Width, Height.

🎙️ The key difference is the addition of the z-axis. While 2D geometry helps us understand flat surfaces, 3D geometry allows us to describe the volume and spatial arrangement of objects.

The Coordinate System

  • Three mutually perpendicular axes: X, Y, and Z.
  • They intersect at the origin (0,0,0).
  • These axes define three coordinate planes: XY, YZ, ZX.

🎙️ Imagine the corner of a room. The lines where the walls meet the floor, and where the two walls meet each other, represent the three axes. These axes divide the entire space into eight regions.

Dividing Space: Octants

  • The three coordinate planes divide space into eight regions.
  • Each region is called an octant.
  • Signs of coordinates (x, y, z) define the octant.

🎙️ Just like in 2D, where the axes divide the plane into four quadrants, in 3D, the three planes divide space into eight octants. Knowing the signs of the coordinates helps us identify which octant a point lies in.

Plotting a Point (x, y, z)

  • Start at the origin (0,0,0).
  • Move 'x' units along the X-axis.
  • Move 'y' units parallel to the Y-axis.
  • Move 'z' units parallel to the Z-axis.

🎙️ Visualizing this is crucial. First, find the point (x, y) on the XY-plane, then move vertically up or down 'z' units to locate the 3D point. Practice helps in developing this spatial sense.

Example: Identifying Octants

  • Point A (2, 3, 1) is in Octant I (+,+,+).
  • Point B (-1, 2, 4) is in Octant II (-,+,+).
  • Point C (3, -2, -5) is in Octant VI (+,-,-).
  • Point D (-4, -1, 2) is in Octant III (-, -, +).

🎙️ Remember that the signs of x, y, and z determine the octant. For example, in Octant I, all coordinates are positive. In Octant II, x is negative, y and z are positive.

The Distance Formula

  • Calculates the distance between two points P(x1, y1, z1) and Q(x2, y2, z2).
  • Extension of the 2D distance formula.
  • Formula: √[(x2-x1)² + (y2-y1)² + (z2-z1)²]

🎙️ This formula is one of the most important tools in 3D geometry. It allows us to find the length of a line segment connecting any two points in space. Notice the addition of the z-coordinate term.

Derivation Insight

  • Uses Pythagorean theorem twice.
  • First, find distance in XY plane (or any other plane).
  • Then, use this distance and the z-difference to find the 3D distance.

🎙️ The 3D distance formula can be derived by applying the Pythagorean theorem in two steps. First, find the diagonal on a plane, then use that diagonal as one side of another right triangle with the z-difference as the other side.

Worked Example: Distance

  • Find the distance between P(1, -3, 4) and Q(-4, 1, 2).
  • x1=1, y1=-3, z1=4
  • x2=-4, y2=1, z2=2
  • Distance = √[(-4-1)² + (1-(-3))² + (2-4)²] = √[(-5)² + (4)² + (-2)²] = √[25 + 16 + 4] = √45 = 3√5 units.

🎙️ Carefully substitute the coordinates into the formula. Pay attention to the signs, especially when subtracting negative numbers. Simplify the square root at the end if possible.

Section Formula (Internal Division)

  • Finds coordinates of a point R that divides a line segment PQ internally in ratio m:n.
  • Point P(x1, y1, z1), Q(x2, y2, z2).
  • R = [ (mx2+nx1)/(m+n), (my2+ny1)/(m+n), (mz2+nz1)/(m+n) ]

🎙️ This formula is a direct extension of the 2D section formula. Each coordinate (x, y, z) is calculated independently using the same ratio m:n. Memorize this formula as it's frequently tested.

✂️
Dividing a Line Segment

Section Formula (External Division)

  • Finds coordinates of a point R that divides a line segment PQ externally in ratio m:n.
  • Similar to internal division, but with a minus sign.
  • R = [ (mx2-nx1)/(m-n), (my2-ny1)/(m-n), (mz2-nz1)/(m-n) ]

🎙️ For external division, the point R lies outside the segment PQ on the line extended. The only change from the internal division formula is the minus sign in both the numerator and denominator.

✖️

Midpoint Formula

  • A special case of the section formula where the ratio m:n is 1:1.
  • The midpoint divides the segment into two equal halves.
  • Midpoint = [ (x1+x2)/2, (y1+y2)/2, (z1+z2)/2 ]

🎙️ When m=n=1, the section formula simplifies to the midpoint formula. This is a very common calculation, used to find the center of a line segment. It's simply the average of the coordinates.

Worked Example: Section Formula

  • Find the coordinates of the point that divides the join of P(2, -1, 4) and Q(4, 3, 2) in the ratio 2:3 internally.
  • m=2, n=3
  • x = (2*4 + 3*2)/(2+3) = (8+6)/5 = 14/5
  • y = (2*3 + 3*(-1))/(2+3) = (6-3)/5 = 3/5
  • z = (2*2 + 3*4)/(2+3) = (4+12)/5 = 16/5
  • Point R is (14/5, 3/5, 16/5).

🎙️ Carefully substitute the values of x1, y1, z1, x2, y2, z2, m, and n into the formula. Ensure you perform the multiplication before addition, and handle fractions correctly.

Section Formula Pitfalls

  • Confusing internal and external division signs.
  • Incorrectly swapping m and n values.
  • Arithmetic errors, especially with fractions or negative numbers.

🎙️ It's easy to mix up the m and n values, or forget the sign change for external division. Always write down the formula first, then substitute the values to minimize errors.

Centroid of a Triangle

  • The point of intersection of the medians of a triangle.
  • Divides each median in the ratio 2:1.
  • For A(x1,y1,z1), B(x2,y2,z2), C(x3,y3,z3):
  • Centroid G = [ (x1+x2+x3)/3, (y1+y2+y3)/3, (z1+z2+z3)/3 ]

🎙️ The centroid is often called the 'center of mass' of the triangle. Its coordinates are simply the average of the coordinates of the three vertices. This formula is derived using the section formula.

Centroid of a Tetrahedron

  • A tetrahedron is a 3D figure with four triangular faces.
  • Its centroid is the point where the medians (lines from a vertex to the centroid of the opposite face) intersect.
  • For A(x1,y1,z1), B(x2,y2,z2), C(x3,y3,z3), D(x4,y4,z4):
  • Centroid G = [ (x1+x2+x3+x4)/4, (y1+y2+y3+y4)/4, (z1+z2+z3+z4)/4 ]

🎙️ Similar to a triangle, the centroid of a tetrahedron is the average of the coordinates of its four vertices. This generalizes the concept of a center of mass to 3D solids.

Worked Example: Centroid

  • Find the centroid of the triangle with vertices A(1,2,3), B(4,-1,2), C(2,5,-1).
  • x-coordinate = (1+4+2)/3 = 7/3
  • y-coordinate = (2-1+5)/3 = 6/3 = 2
  • z-coordinate = (3+2-1)/3 = 4/3
  • Centroid G = (7/3, 2, 4/3).

🎙️ This is a straightforward application of the centroid formula. Sum the x-coordinates and divide by 3, repeat for y and z. Be careful with negative numbers.

Introducing the Third Dimension: The Z-axis

  • To describe space, we need a third axis.
  • The Z-axis is perpendicular to both X and Y axes.
  • It represents height or depth.

🎙️ Imagine moving off the flat paper into the room. This 'up' or 'down' movement requires a new direction, which we call the Z-axis. It originates from the intersection of X and Y, forming a right-handed system.

Coordinate Axes and Coordinate Planes

  • Three mutually perpendicular axes: X, Y, Z.
  • These axes form three coordinate planes: XY, YZ, ZX.
  • The intersection of these planes is the origin (0, 0, 0).

🎙️ The three axes divide the entire space. Each pair of axes forms a plane. For instance, the XY-plane is where Z=0. Understanding these planes is key to visualizing points in space.

Coordinates of a Point in Space (x, y, z)

  • A point P in space is represented by an ordered triplet (x, y, z).
  • x-coordinate: distance from YZ-plane.
  • y-coordinate: distance from ZX-plane.
  • z-coordinate: distance from XY-plane.

🎙️ Just as (x, y) located a point in 2D, (x, y, z) precisely locates a point in 3D space. Each coordinate tells us its perpendicular distance from one of the coordinate planes.

Octants: Dividing 3D Space

  • The three coordinate planes divide space into eight regions.
  • Each region is called an octant.
  • The signs of x, y, z determine the octant.

🎙️ In 2D, we had four quadrants. In 3D, the three planes create eight distinct regions, much like the eight corners of a room. We need to identify which octant a point belongs to based on its coordinates' signs.

  • Point A (2, 3, 1): All coordinates are positive.
  • Point B (-1, 4, 2): x is negative, y, z are positive.
  • Point C (3, -2, 5): y is negative, x, z are positive.

Distance Formula in 3D

  • Distance between P(x₁, y₁, z₁) and Q(x₂, y₂, z₂).
  • Formula: d = √((x₂-x₁)² + (y₂-y₁)² + (z₂-z₁)²).
  • This is an extension of the 2D distance formula.

🎙️ Just like in 2D, we can find the distance between two points in 3D space. The formula is a direct extension, simply adding the squared difference of the z-coordinates. This is a very important formula for exams.

Worked Example: Distance Between Two Points

  • Find the distance between P(1, -3, 4) and Q(-4, 1, 2).
  • x₁=1, y₁=-3, z₁=4; x₂=-4, y₂=1, z₂=2.
  • d = √((-4-1)² + (1-(-3))² + (2-4)²)
  • d = √((-5)² + (4)² + (-2)²) = √(25 + 16 + 4) = √45 = 3√5 units.

🎙️ Let's apply the distance formula to a practical problem. Be careful with the signs when substituting the values into the formula. Always simplify your answer to its simplest radical form if possible.

Common Mistake: Forgetting Z-coordinate

  • Students often forget to include the (z₂-z₁)² term.
  • Always double-check if it's a 2D or 3D problem.
  • Ensure all three coordinate differences are squared and summed.

🎙️ A frequent error in exams is applying the 2D distance formula to a 3D problem. Remember, if you have three coordinates, you MUST use the 3D distance formula. Always be mindful of the context of the problem.

Quick Question 1: Distance

  • Calculate the distance between points A(2, 0, -1) and B(4, 2, 3).
  • Options: (A) √20 (B) √24 (C) √28 (D) √32

🎙️ Take a moment to solve this problem on your own. Apply the distance formula carefully. The correct answer is (C) √((4-2)² + (2-0)² + (3-(-1))²) = √(2² + 2² + 4²) = √(4+4+16) = √24. My apologies, I made a mistake in calculation. The correct answer is (B) √24. Let's re-calculate: (4-2)^2 = 4, (2-0)^2 = 4, (3-(-1))^2 = 4^2 = 16. So, 4+4+16=24. The answer is √24.

Section Formula in 3D

  • Finds coordinates of a point dividing a line segment.
  • Divides P(x₁, y₁, z₁) and Q(x₂, y₂, z₂) in ratio m:n.
  • Internal division: ((mx₂+nx₁)/(m+n), (my₂+ny₁)/(m+n), (mz₂+nz₁)/(m+n)).

🎙️ The section formula also extends naturally from 2D to 3D. It helps us find a point that lies on a line segment and divides it into a specific ratio. Remember the order of m and n, and their corresponding points.

➗
Dividing a Segment

Worked Example: Section Formula

  • Find the point dividing A(1, -2, 3) and B(3, 4, -5) in ratio 2:3 internally.
  • x = (2*3 + 3*1)/(2+3) = 9/5.
  • y = (2*4 + 3*(-2))/(2+3) = (8-6)/5 = 2/5.
  • z = (2*(-5) + 3*3)/(2+3) = (-10+9)/5 = -1/5.
  • The point is (9/5, 2/5, -1/5).

🎙️ Let's apply the section formula. Substitute the coordinates and the ratio carefully. Each coordinate (x, y, z) is calculated independently using the same ratio and corresponding coordinates.

Midpoint Formula

  • A special case of the section formula where m:n = 1:1.
  • Midpoint of P(x₁, y₁, z₁) and Q(x₂, y₂, z₂).
  • M = ((x₁+x₂)/2, (y₁+y₂)/2, (z₁+z₂)/2).

🎙️ The midpoint is simply the point that divides a segment into two equal halves. This is a very common application of the section formula, where the ratio is 1:1. It's often used in geometry problems.

Worked Example: Midpoint

  • Find the midpoint of A(4, -6, 8) and B(-2, 2, 4).
  • x = (4 + (-2))/2 = 2/2 = 1.
  • y = (-6 + 2)/2 = -4/2 = -2.
  • z = (8 + 4)/2 = 12/2 = 6.
  • The midpoint is (1, -2, 6).

🎙️ This example demonstrates the simplicity of the midpoint formula. Just sum the corresponding coordinates and divide by two. It's a straightforward calculation but ensure you don't mix up the coordinates.

Centroid of a Triangle in 3D

  • For a triangle with vertices A(x₁,y₁,z₁), B(x₂,y₂,z₂), C(x₃,y₃,z₃).
  • The centroid G is the point of intersection of the medians.
  • G = ((x₁+x₂+x₃)/3, (y₁+y₂+y₃)/3, (z₁+z₂+z₃)/3).

🎙️ The centroid is the geometric center of a triangle. Its coordinates are simply the average of the coordinates of its three vertices. This formula is quite intuitive and easy to remember.

Worked Example: Centroid

  • Find the centroid of a triangle with vertices (1, 2, 3), (4, -1, 2), and (-2, 3, 1).
  • x = (1 + 4 + (-2))/3 = 3/3 = 1.
  • y = (2 + (-1) + 3)/3 = 4/3.
  • z = (3 + 2 + 1)/3 = 6/3 = 2.
  • The centroid is (1, 4/3, 2).

🎙️ Here's how to calculate the centroid. Sum the x-coordinates, y-coordinates, and z-coordinates separately, then divide each sum by 3. This is a direct application of the formula.

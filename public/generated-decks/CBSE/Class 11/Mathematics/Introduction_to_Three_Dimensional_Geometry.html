From 2D to 3D: A Simple Shift

  • 2D: Describing position on a flat surface (like a map).
  • 3D: Describing position in actual physical space (like a room).
  • Adding one more direction gives us depth.

🎙️ Imagine drawing a house on a piece of paper – that's 2D. Now, imagine walking around inside an actual house – that's 3D. The key difference is the addition of depth, which allows us to move up and down, not just left-right and front-back.

Entering the Third Dimension: The Z-axis

  • To describe depth, we introduce a third axis.
  • The Z-axis is perpendicular to both X and Y axes.
  • It represents height or depth from the XY-plane.

🎙️ Now, imagine a stick poking straight up from the origin of your 2D plane. That's our Z-axis! It's perpendicular to both the X and Y axes, completing our framework for three-dimensional space. Positive Z goes 'up', negative Z goes 'down'.

The Three Coordinate Axes

  • X-axis (horizontal), Y-axis (vertical), Z-axis (depth/height).
  • All three axes are mutually perpendicular.
  • They intersect at a single point called the origin.

🎙️ In 3D geometry, we have three fundamental lines: the X, Y, and Z axes. Think of the corner of your room where two walls meet the floor. The lines formed by these intersections are perfect examples of mutually perpendicular axes.

The Origin in 3D Space

  • The point where all three coordinate axes intersect.
  • Its coordinates are (0, 0, 0).
  • It serves as the reference point for all other locations.

🎙️ Just like in 2D, the origin is our starting block in 3D. It's the unique point (0,0,0) from which all other points are measured. Every coordinate value tells us how far along each axis a point is from this central origin.

The Three Coordinate Planes

  • XY-plane: Formed by the X and Y axes (Z=0).
  • YZ-plane: Formed by the Y and Z axes (X=0).
  • ZX-plane: Formed by the Z and X axes (Y=0).

🎙️ These three axes define three principal planes. Imagine the floor of your room as the XY-plane, one wall as the YZ-plane, and the other wall as the ZX-plane. These planes are crucial as they divide the entire space into distinct regions.

Dividing Space: Introduction to Octants

  • The three coordinate planes divide space into eight regions.
  • Each region is called an 'octant'.
  • Similar to quadrants in 2D geometry (which are four).

🎙️ Just as two axes divide a plane into four quadrants, three planes divide space into eight octants. Each octant is a distinct 'box' or region, and its identity is determined by the signs of the coordinates of any point within it.

Identifying Octants by Coordinate Signs

  • The first octant has all positive coordinates (+, +, +).
  • Other octants are defined by different combinations of signs.
  • Understanding signs helps in locating points correctly.

🎙️ This is a crucial slide for understanding octants. The first octant, where x, y, and z are all positive, is the easiest to remember. For example, if a point is (-2, 3, -5), its signs are (-, +, -), which corresponds to a specific octant. Make sure to learn these combinations.

Coordinates of a Point in Space

  • A point in 3D space is represented by an ordered triplet (x, y, z).
  • x: distance from YZ-plane along X-axis.
  • y: distance from ZX-plane along Y-axis.
  • z: distance from XY-plane along Z-axis.

🎙️ Just like (x,y) in 2D, (x,y,z) uniquely identifies a point in 3D. Each coordinate tells us its perpendicular distance from a specific coordinate plane. For example, 'x' is the distance from the YZ-plane.

Example: Locating a Point (2, 3, 4)

  • Start at the origin (0,0,0).
  • Move 2 units along the positive X-axis.

  • Finally, move 4 units parallel to the positive Z-axis.

Distance Formula: An Extension of Pythagoras

  • Recall the 2D distance formula: √[(x₂-x₁)² + (y₂-y₁)²].
  • In 3D, we simply add the Z-component.
  • It helps find the length of a line segment in space.

🎙️ The 3D distance formula is not entirely new; it's a logical extension of what you already know from 2D. The core idea remains the same: using the Pythagorean theorem to find the hypotenuse, but now in three dimensions.

📏
Measuring Length

Derivation of the 3D Distance Formula

  • Consider two points P(x₁,y₁,z₁) and Q(x₂,y₂,z₂).
  • Project P and Q onto the XY-plane to get P'(x₁,y₁,0) and Q'(x₂,y₂,0).
  • The distance PQ can be found using the 2D distance and a right triangle involving Δz.

🎙️ The derivation is elegant. We essentially form a right-angled triangle in space. One leg is the distance between the projections of the points on the XY-plane, and the other leg is the difference in their Z-coordinates. Then, we apply Pythagoras theorem.

1Points P, Q
2Project to XY
3Form right triangle
4Apply Pythagoras

The 3D Distance Formula

  • Distance between P(x₁, y₁, z₁) and Q(x₂, y₂, z₂) is:
  • d = √[(x₂ - x₁)² + (y₂ - y₁)² + (z₂ - z₁)²].
  • Always remember to square the differences and sum them before taking the square root.

🎙️ This is a key formula you must memorize and understand. Notice the symmetry with the 2D formula – we simply added the (z₂ - z₁)² term. Pay close attention to signs when calculating the differences.

  • Step 1: Identify (x₁,y₁,z₁) and (x₂,y₂,z₂).
  • Step 2: Substitute values into the distance formula.

1P(1,-3,4), Q(-4,1,2)
2x₂-x₁ = -5
3y₂-y₁ = 4
4z₂-z₁ = -2

Section Formula: Dividing a Line Segment

  • Recall the 2D section formula for internal division.
  • It helps find the coordinates of a point that divides a line segment.
  • In 3D, we extend this concept to include the Z-coordinate.

🎙️ Just like we can divide a line segment on a 2D plane, we can do the same in 3D space. The section formula helps us find the exact coordinates of the point that 'cuts' a line segment in a given ratio, either internally or externally.

✂️
Cutting a Line
22 / 30

Section Formula (Internal Division)

  • Point R(x,y,z) divides the line segment joining P(x₁,y₁,z₁) and Q(x₂,y₂,z₂)
  • in the ratio m:n internally, then:
  • x = (mx₂ + nx₁) / (m + n)
  • y = (my₂ + ny₁) / (m + n)
  • z = (mz₂ + nz₁) / (m + n)

🎙️ This formula is crucial. Notice how each coordinate (x, y, z) is calculated independently using the same ratio m:n. The point R lies *between* P and Q. Remember the cross-multiplication pattern: m times the second point's coordinate, plus n times the first point's coordinate.

➡️

Special Case: The Midpoint Formula

  • When a point divides a segment in the ratio 1:1, it's the midpoint.
  • Substitute m=1 and n=1 into the internal section formula.
  • Midpoint (x,y,z) = [ (x₁+x₂)/2, (y₁+y₂)/2, (z₁+z₂)/2 ].

🎙️ The midpoint is a special and very common case of the section formula. It simply averages the corresponding coordinates of the two endpoints. This makes intuitive sense, as the midpoint is exactly halfway between the two points.

  • in the ratio 2:3 internally.
  • Step 1: Identify (x₁,y₁,z₁), (x₂,y₂,z₂), m, and n.
  • Step 2: Apply the internal section formula for x, y, and z.

🎙️ Let's apply the internal section formula carefully. Remember to keep track of the signs of the coordinates. The calculation involves simple arithmetic, but precision is key to getting the correct coordinates for the dividing point.

1A(2,-1,4), B(4,3,2), m=2, n=3
2x = (2*4 + 3*2)/(2+3) = 14/5
3y = (2*3 + 3*-1)/(2+3) = 3/5
4z = (2*2 + 3*4)/(2+3) = 16/5
5Point = (14/5, 3/5, 16/5)

Section Formula (External Division)

  • Point R(x,y,z) divides the line segment joining P(x₁,y₁,z₁) and Q(x₂,y₂,z₂)
  • in the ratio m:n externally, then:
  • x = (mx₂ - nx₁) / (m - n)
  • y = (my₂ - ny₁) / (m - n)
  • z = (mz₂ - nz₁) / (m - n)

🎙️ For external division, the point R lies *outside* the segment PQ, either on the extension of PQ or QP. The only difference in the formula is the minus sign in both the numerator and denominator. Be careful with the order of subtraction.

  • Step 1: Identify (x₁,y₁,z₁), (x₂,y₂,z₂), m, and n.

🎙️ This example demonstrates external division. Notice how the point (5,6,7) lies outside the segment AB. The formula works consistently, just remember the sign change from internal division. Practice these calculations to avoid errors.

1A(1,2,3), B(3,4,5), m=2, n=1
2x = (2*3 - 1*1)/(2-1) = 5/1 = 5
3y = (2*4 - 1*2)/(2-1) = 6/1 = 6
4z = (2*5 - 1*3)/(2-1) = 7/1 = 7

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Sign Errors: Especially in distance and section formulas.
  • Confusing 2D and 3D: Forgetting the Z-coordinate.
  • Incorrect Formula Recall: Mixing up internal/external section.
  • Calculation Errors: Rushing through arithmetic.

🎙️ These are the typical pitfalls students encounter. Always double-check your signs, ensure you're using the 3D formulas completely, and practice recalling the correct formula for internal versus external division. A little extra care can save many marks.

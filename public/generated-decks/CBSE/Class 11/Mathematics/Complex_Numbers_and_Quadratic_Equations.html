Why do we need Complex Numbers?

  • Recall quadratic equations like x² + 1 = 0.
  • In real numbers, the square root of a negative number is undefined.
  • This limitation led mathematicians to invent a new type of number.

🎙️ Think about the equation x² + 1 = 0. If you try to solve it using real numbers, you'll find x² = -1, which has no solution in the set of real numbers. This mathematical 'gap' necessitated the expansion of our number system.

The Imaginary Unit 'i'

  • To solve x² = -1, we define 'i' such that i² = -1.
  • Therefore, i = √-1.
  • This 'i' is called the imaginary unit.

🎙️ To overcome the limitation of real numbers, mathematicians introduced the imaginary unit, denoted by 'i'. By definition, i squared is equal to minus one. This simple definition opens up a whole new world of numbers.

Powers of 'i': A Cyclic Pattern

  • i¹ = i
  • i² = -1
  • i³ = i² * i = -i
  • i⁴ = i² * i² = (-1) * (-1) = 1

🎙️ The powers of 'i' follow a fascinating cyclic pattern that repeats every four powers. This pattern is extremely useful for simplifying higher powers of 'i' and is a common topic in exams.

Cycle of 4

Worked Example: Simplify i⁵⁹

  • Divide the exponent by 4: 59 ÷ 4 = 14 remainder 3.
  • So, i⁵⁹ = i⁴*¹⁴⁺³ = (i⁴)¹⁴ * i³.
  • Since i⁴ = 1, (i⁴)¹⁴ = 1¹⁴ = 1.
  • Therefore, i⁵⁹ = 1 * i³ = -i.

🎙️ Let's apply the cyclic property to simplify i⁵⁹. The key is to divide the exponent by 4 and use the remainder. This method ensures you can simplify any power of 'i' quickly and accurately.

What is a Complex Number?

  • A number of the form a + ib, where 'a' and 'b' are real numbers.
  • 'a' is called the real part, and 'b' is called the imaginary part.
  • The set of complex numbers is denoted by C.

🎙️ Now we can formally define a complex number. It's simply a combination of a real part and an imaginary part. Think of it as extending the number line into a plane, which we'll discuss later as the Argand plane.

a + ib

Identifying Real & Imaginary Parts

  • For z = a + ib, Re(z) = a (the real part).
  • Im(z) = b (the imaginary part, without 'i').
  • Example: If z = 3 + 4i, then Re(z) = 3 and Im(z) = 4.

🎙️ It's crucial to correctly identify the real and imaginary parts of a complex number. Remember, the imaginary part is just the coefficient of 'i', not 'ib' itself. This distinction is important for various operations.

When are Two Complex Numbers Equal?

  • Two complex numbers, z₁ = a + ib and z₂ = c + id, are equal...
  • ...if and only if their real parts are equal (a = c)...
  • ...AND their imaginary parts are equal (b = d).
  • This property is often used to solve equations involving complex numbers.

🎙️ The condition for equality of complex numbers is straightforward: both their real and imaginary components must match. This principle is frequently used in problems where you need to find unknown values within complex numbers.

⚖️
a+ib = c+id

Addition of Complex Numbers

  • To add z₁ = a + ib and z₂ = c + id:
  • Add their real parts: (a + c).
  • Add their imaginary parts: (b + d).
  • Result: (a + c) + i(b + d).

🎙️ Adding complex numbers is like adding polynomials. You combine the like terms – the real parts with real parts, and the imaginary parts with imaginary parts. It's a very intuitive operation.

Subtraction of Complex Numbers

  • To subtract z₂ = c + id from z₁ = a + ib:
  • Subtract their real parts: (a - c).
  • Subtract their imaginary parts: (b - d).
  • Result: (a - c) + i(b - d).

🎙️ Subtraction follows the same logic as addition. Just ensure you subtract the corresponding real and imaginary parts correctly. Pay attention to the signs, as this is a common source of errors.

Multiplication of Complex Numbers

  • Multiply z₁ = a + ib and z₂ = c + id like binomials.
  • Remember that i² = -1.
  • Result: (ac - bd) + i(ad + bc).

🎙️ Multiplication is a bit more involved. You use the distributive property, just like with binomials. The key step is to replace any i² with -1, which will convert imaginary terms into real terms.

The Complex Conjugate

  • For z = a + ib, its conjugate is denoted by z̄ (z-bar).
  • z̄ = a - ib (change the sign of the imaginary part).
  • Geometrically, it's a reflection across the real axis.

🎙️ The complex conjugate is a very important concept. It's formed by simply changing the sign of the imaginary part. It has many useful properties, especially when it comes to division and finding moduli.

↔️
a+ib -> a-ib

Division of Complex Numbers

  • To divide z₁ by z₂, multiply the numerator and denominator by the conjugate of the denominator.
  • This makes the denominator a real number.
  • Example: (a+ib)/(c+id) = (a+ib)(c-id) / (c+id)(c-id).

🎙️ Division is not as straightforward as the other operations. We rationalize the denominator by multiplying both numerator and denominator by the conjugate of the denominator. This eliminates 'i' from the denominator, leaving a real number.

2Simplify Numerator
3Simplify Denominator

Worked Example: Operations with Complex Numbers

  • Given z₁ = 2 + 3i and z₂ = 1 - i.
  • Find z₁ + z₂ = (2+1) + i(3-1) = 3 + 2i.
  • Find z₁ * z₂ = (2+3i)(1-i) = 2 - 2i + 3i - 3i² = 2 + i + 3 = 5 + i.
  • Find z₁ / z₂ = (2+3i)/(1-i) = (2+3i)(1+i) / (1-i)(1+i) = (2+2i+3i+3i²) / (1² - i²) = (-1+5i) / 2 = -1/2 + 5/2 i.

🎙️ Here's a comprehensive example demonstrating addition, multiplication, and division. Practice these steps carefully, as they are fundamental for solving more complex problems. Pay close attention to the signs and the i² = -1 substitution.

Modulus of a Complex Number

  • For z = a + ib, the modulus is |z| = √(a² + b²).
  • It represents the distance of the complex number from the origin (0,0) in the Argand plane.
  • Always a non-negative real number.

🎙️ The modulus of a complex number is its magnitude or length. It's calculated using the Pythagorean theorem, treating 'a' and 'b' as coordinates. It's always a real and non-negative value, representing a distance.

Key Properties of Modulus

  • |z| = |z̄| = |-z|
  • |z₁z₂| = |z₁||z₂|
  • |z₁/z₂| = |z₁|/|z₂| (z₂ ≠ 0)
  • |zⁿ| = |z|ⁿ

🎙️ These properties of the modulus are extremely useful for simplifying expressions and solving problems efficiently in exams. Remember them well, especially for products and quotients of complex numbers.

Worked Example: Finding Modulus

  • Here, a = 3 and b = -4.
  • |z| = √(3² + (-4)²)

Polar Representation

  • A complex number z = a + ib can also be represented as z = r(cosθ + i sinθ).
  • Here, r = |z| = √(a² + b²) is the modulus.
  • θ is the argument or amplitude, tanθ = b/a (with quadrant consideration).

🎙️ Besides the Cartesian form (a + ib), complex numbers can be expressed in polar form. This form is particularly useful for multiplication, division, and finding powers of complex numbers, as it simplifies calculations significantly.

Worked Example: Convert to Polar Form

  • Convert z = 1 + i to polar form.
  • Modulus r = |1+i| = √(1² + 1²) = √2.
  • Argument θ: tanθ = 1/1 = 1. Since z is in Quadrant I, θ = π/4.
  • So, z = √2 (cos(π/4) + i sin(π/4)).

🎙️ Let's convert 1 + i into its polar form. Remember to correctly identify the quadrant of the complex number to determine the principal value of the argument θ. This is a common point of error.

Quadratic Equations Revisited

  • Standard form: ax² + bx + c = 0, where a ≠ 0.
  • The discriminant is D = b² - 4ac.
  • If D ≥ 0, roots are real. If D < 0, roots are non-real (complex).

🎙️ We're familiar with quadratic equations. Until now, if the discriminant was negative, we'd say 'no real roots'. Now, with complex numbers, we can find solutions even when the discriminant is negative.

➕➖
ax²+bx+c=0

Solving Quadratic Equations with Complex Roots

  • When D = b² - 4ac < 0, the roots are given by x = [-b ± √(D)] / 2a.
  • Since D is negative, let D = -k, where k > 0.
  • Then √D = √(-k) = √k * √(-1) = √k * i.
  • The roots are x = [-b ± i√k] / 2a.

🎙️ When the discriminant is negative, we use the property of the imaginary unit 'i' to express the square root of the negative number. This allows us to find the two complex conjugate roots of the quadratic equation.

1Calculate Discriminant D
2If D<0, let D=-k
3Substitute √D = i√k

Worked Example: Solving x² + x + 1 = 0

  • Identify a=1, b=1, c=1.
  • Discriminant D = b² - 4ac = 1² - 4(1)(1) = 1 - 4 = -3.
  • Since D < 0, roots are complex.
  • x = [-1 ± √(-3)] / (2*1) = [-1 ± i√3] / 2.
  • Roots are -1/2 + i√3/2 and -1/2 - i√3/2.

🎙️ Let's solve the equation x² + x + 1 = 0. Here, the discriminant is negative, leading to complex roots. Notice that the roots are always complex conjugates of each other when coefficients are real.

Common Mistakes & Exam Tips

  • Forgetting i² = -1 during multiplication.
  • Incorrectly identifying real/imaginary parts (e.g., Im(z) = ib).
  • Errors in finding the argument (θ) due to quadrant issues.
  • Not rationalizing the denominator during division.

🎙️ Be mindful of these common pitfalls. Practice regularly to avoid them. Always double-check your calculations, especially when dealing with signs and the i² substitution. Understanding the quadrant for argument is critical.

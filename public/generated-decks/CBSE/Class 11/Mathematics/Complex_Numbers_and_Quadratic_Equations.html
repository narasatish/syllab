Defining 'i': The Imaginary Unit

  • The imaginary unit 'i' is defined as √(-1).
  • This implies that i² = -1.
  • It's the fundamental building block of complex numbers.

🎙️ So, we formally define 'i' as the square root of minus one. This crucial definition means that 'i squared' is equal to minus one. This single definition is the cornerstone of all complex number theory, allowing us to work with negative square roots.

The Cyclic Nature of Powers of 'i'

  • i¹ = i
  • i² = -1
  • i³ = i² × i = -i
  • i⁴ = i² × i² = (-1) × (-1) = 1

🎙️ Observe the pattern when we raise 'i' to different positive integer powers. After i to the power of four, the pattern repeats itself. This cyclic nature is extremely useful for simplifying higher powers of 'i' efficiently.

Unveiling Complex Numbers: Beyond the Number Line

  • A complex number combines a real part and an imaginary part.
  • It takes the form a + ib, where 'a' and 'b' are real numbers.
  • These numbers cannot be placed on a single number line.

🎙️ A complex number is essentially a pair of real numbers, one representing its 'real' component and the other its 'imaginary' component, linked by 'i'. Unlike real numbers, complex numbers exist in a 2D plane, not just a line, which we'll explore later as the Argand plane.

The Standard Form: a + ib

  • A complex number 'z' is written as z = a + ib.
  • 'a' is the real part, denoted as Re(z).
  • 'b' is the imaginary part, denoted as Im(z).
  • Example: In 3 + 4i, Re(z)=3, Im(z)=4.

🎙️ The expression 'a + ib' is the standard or rectangular form of a complex number. It clearly separates the real and imaginary components. It's important to remember that 'b' itself is a real number; 'ib' is the imaginary term.

When are Two Complex Numbers Equal?

  • Two complex numbers, z₁ = a + ib and z₂ = c + id, are equal...
  • ...IF AND ONLY IF their real parts are equal (a=c)...
  • ...AND their imaginary parts are equal (b=d).

🎙️ This is a straightforward but crucial rule. Just like two vectors are equal if their corresponding components are equal, two complex numbers are equal only when both their real parts and their imaginary parts match exactly. This property is often used to solve for unknown variables.

Adding Complex Numbers: Like Terms!

  • To add z₁ = a + ib and z₂ = c + id:
  • Add the real parts: (a + c)
  • Add the imaginary parts: (b + d)
  • Result: (a + c) + i(b + d)

🎙️ Adding complex numbers is as simple as adding like terms in algebra. You just combine the real parts together and the imaginary parts together. Think of it like adding apples to apples and oranges to oranges; you don't mix them up.

Subtracting Complex Numbers: Mind the Signs!

  • To subtract z₂ = c + id from z₁ = a + ib:
  • Subtract the real parts: (a - c)
  • Subtract the imaginary parts: (b - d)
  • Result: (a - c) + i(b - d)

🎙️ Subtraction follows a similar logic to addition, but be extra careful with the signs, especially when distributing a negative. Again, you operate on the real parts separately from the imaginary parts, maintaining the structure of the complex number.

Multiplying Complex Numbers: Remember i² = -1

  • Treat them like binomials: (a + ib)(c + id)
  • Use the distributive property (FOIL method).
  • Crucially, substitute i² with -1.
  • Combine real and imaginary terms to get a + ib form.

🎙️ Multiplication is where 'i squared equals minus one' becomes critical. You multiply complex numbers just like you would any two binomials, using the FOIL method. However, any time you encounter an i-squared term, immediately replace it with -1 and simplify.

The Conjugate: Changing the Sign of 'i'

  • The conjugate of z = a + ib is denoted as z̄ (z-bar).
  • It is defined as z̄ = a - ib.
  • Only the sign of the imaginary part changes.
  • Useful for division and modulus calculations.

🎙️ The complex conjugate is a very important concept. It's formed by simply flipping the sign of the imaginary part. For instance, the conjugate of 3 + 4i is 3 - 4i. This operation has profound implications, especially when we want to eliminate 'i' from the denominator during division.

Dividing Complex Numbers: Rationalising the Denominator

  • To divide z₁ by z₂, multiply numerator and denominator by the conjugate of z₂.
  • This makes the denominator a real number (a² + b²).
  • Then simplify into the standard a + ib form.

🎙️ Division is often considered the trickiest operation. The key is to 'rationalize' the denominator, much like you would with surds. By multiplying both the numerator and denominator by the conjugate of the denominator, you ensure the denominator becomes a real number, making the complex fraction much easier to simplify.

Example: Performing Complex Division

  • Simplify (2 + 3i) / (1 - i).
  • Multiply by (1 + i) / (1 + i).
  • Numerator: (2+3i)(1+i) = 2+2i+3i+3i² = 2+5i-3 = -1+5i.
  • Denominator: (1-i)(1+i) = 1² - i² = 1 - (-1) = 2.
  • Result: (-1 + 5i) / 2 = -1/2 + 5/2 i.

🎙️ Let's work through this division example. Notice how the denominator simplifies beautifully to a real number. After multiplying, separate the real and imaginary parts of the numerator and divide each by the real denominator to get the final answer in the standard form.

Modulus: The Distance from the Origin

  • The modulus of z = a + ib is denoted as |z|.
  • It represents the distance of the point (a,b) from the origin in the Argand plane.
  • Calculated as |z| = √(a² + b²).

🎙️ The modulus of a complex number is its magnitude or length. Think of it as the distance from the origin (0,0) to the point (a,b) in a 2D coordinate system, which we call the Argand plane for complex numbers. It's always a non-negative real number.

Example: Calculating the Modulus

  • Find the modulus of z = 3 + 4i.
  • Here, a = 3 and b = 4.
  • |z| = √(3² + 4²) = √(9 + 16) = √25 = 5.
  • The modulus is always a real, non-negative value.

Revisiting Quadratic Equations: When Roots are Complex

  • For a quadratic equation ax² + bx + c = 0,
  • The discriminant is D = b² - 4ac.
  • If D < 0, the roots are complex and non-real.
  • This connects back to our initial question!

🎙️ Remember our initial question about x² + 1 = 0? Now we have the tools to solve it! When the discriminant of a quadratic equation is negative, the square root of a negative number appears in the quadratic formula, leading to complex roots. This is where complex numbers truly shine.

Solving ax² + bx + c = 0 (D < 0)

  • Use the quadratic formula: x = [-b ± √(b² - 4ac)] / 2a.
  • If D = b² - 4ac < 0, then √D = √(-|D|) = i√|D|.
  • The roots will be of the form α ± iβ, which are conjugates.

🎙️ The quadratic formula remains our go-to tool. The only difference now is how we handle the square root of a negative discriminant. By introducing 'i', we can express these roots as complex numbers. Notice that complex roots always appear in conjugate pairs.

Example: Finding Complex Roots

  • Solve the equation: x² + x + 1 = 0.
  • Here, a=1, b=1, c=1.
  • D = b² - 4ac = 1² - 4(1)(1) = 1 - 4 = -3.
  • x = [-1 ± √(-3)] / 2(1) = [-1 ± i√3] / 2.
  • Roots are -1/2 + i√3/2 and -1/2 - i√3/2.

🎙️ Let's solve the classic example x² + x + 1 = 0. The discriminant is negative, leading to complex roots. We simply substitute the value of the discriminant into the quadratic formula and simplify, expressing the square root of -3 as i√3.

Beware! Common Pitfalls in Complex Numbers

  • Not replacing i² with -1: Always simplify!
  • Sign errors in conjugate: Only imaginary part changes sign.
  • Distributive property mistakes: Be careful with multiplication.
  • Confusing real and imaginary parts: Keep them separate.

🎙️ Students often make a few recurring errors. The most common is forgetting to substitute i² with -1, which is fundamental. Also, be precise with signs when dealing with conjugates and while distributing terms during multiplication. Always ensure your final answer is in the standard a + ib form.

What is a Limit? (Intuitive Definition)

  • A limit describes the value a function 'approaches' as the input approaches some value.
  • It's about the trend of the function near a point, not necessarily at the point itself.
  • We write it as: lim f(x) as x → a = L

🎙️ Formally, if a function f(x) gets arbitrarily close to a unique number L as x approaches a from either side, then L is the limit of f(x) as x approaches a. The key is 'arbitrarily close' and 'from either side'.

Left-Hand Limit (LHL): Approaching from Below

  • This is the value f(x) approaches as x approaches 'a' from values smaller than 'a'.
  • Imagine walking towards a landmark from its left side.
  • Denoted as: lim f(x) as x → a⁻

🎙️ The left-hand limit considers the behaviour of the function when x is just a tiny bit less than 'a'. It's like checking the trend of a graph as you move towards 'a' from the left side. This is crucial for understanding continuity later.

Right-Hand Limit (RHL): Approaching from Above

  • This is the value f(x) approaches as x approaches 'a' from values greater than 'a'.
  • Imagine walking towards a landmark from its right side.
  • Denoted as: lim f(x) as x → a⁺

🎙️ Conversely, the right-hand limit examines the function's behaviour when x is slightly more than 'a'. You're approaching 'a' from the larger values. Both LHL and RHL must agree for a limit to exist.

When Does a Limit Exist?

  • For a limit to exist at a point 'a', the function must approach the SAME value from both the left and the right.
  • If LHL ≠ RHL, then the limit does NOT exist.
  • Think of two paths leading to the same destination.

🎙️ This is a fundamental condition for the existence of a limit. If the function's value is heading towards one number from the left and a different number from the right, then there's no single 'limit' value it's approaching at that point. The paths must converge.

Evaluating Limits: Method 1 - Direct Substitution

  • The simplest method: substitute the value 'a' directly into f(x).
  • If f(a) yields a finite, defined value, then that is the limit.
  • This works for most polynomial, trigonometric, and exponential functions.

🎙️ Always try direct substitution first. If you get a real number, that's your limit. This method is straightforward and applies to well-behaved functions where the point 'a' is within the domain or doesn't cause any undefined expressions.

1Identify 'a'
2Substitute 'a' into f(x)
3Check if result is finite
10 / 30

Example 1: Direct Substitution

  • Problem: Evaluate lim (x² + 3x + 2) as x → 1
  • Step 1: Substitute x = 1 into the expression.
  • Step 2: (1)² + 3(1) + 2 = 1 + 3 + 2 = 6.
  • Answer: The limit is 6.

🎙️ Here, direct substitution works perfectly. Since substituting x=1 gives a well-defined, finite number (6), this is the limit. No complex steps are needed for such polynomial functions.

Evaluating Limits: Method 2 - Factorization

  • Used when direct substitution results in an indeterminate form (0/0).
  • Factorize the numerator and denominator to cancel common factors.
  • Then, substitute the limit value into the simplified expression.

🎙️ When you encounter an indeterminate form like 0/0, it means there's a 'hole' in the graph. Factorization helps us remove the problematic term by cancelling it out, effectively 'filling' the hole and revealing the true limit.

1Substitute (get 0/0)
2Factorize numerator/denominator
3Cancel common factors
4Substitute again
12 / 30

Example 2: Factorization

  • Problem: Evaluate lim [(x² - 4) / (x - 2)] as x → 2
  • Step 1: Direct substitution gives (4-4)/(2-2) = 0/0 (Indeterminate).
  • Step 2: Factorize numerator: (x² - 4) = (x - 2)(x + 2).
  • Step 3: Expression becomes [(x - 2)(x + 2) / (x - 2)]. Cancel (x-2).
  • Step 4: Now, evaluate lim (x + 2) as x → 2. Substitute x=2: 2+2 = 4.
  • Answer: The limit is 4.

🎙️ This example clearly shows how factorization helps. Even though the original function is undefined at x=2, the limit exists and is 4. The function behaves like x+2 near x=2.

Evaluating Limits: Method 3 - Rationalization

  • Used when the expression involves square roots and gives 0/0 form.
  • Multiply the numerator and denominator by the conjugate of the term with the square root.
  • This helps eliminate the square root and often leads to cancellation of factors.

🎙️ Rationalization is a powerful technique, especially when dealing with expressions involving square roots that lead to an indeterminate form. Remember the difference of squares identity: (a-b)(a+b) = a²-b².

1Substitute (get 0/0)
2Multiply by conjugate
3Simplify and cancel factors
4Substitute again
15 / 30

Example 3: Rationalization

  • Problem: Evaluate lim [(√(x+1) - 1) / x] as x → 0
  • Step 1: Direct substitution gives (√1 - 1)/0 = 0/0.
  • Step 2: Multiply by conjugate (√(x+1) + 1) / (√(x+1) + 1).
  • Step 3: Numerator becomes (x+1) - 1 = x. Denominator is x(√(x+1) + 1).
  • Step 4: Cancel x. Expression becomes 1 / (√(x+1) + 1).
  • Step 5: Substitute x=0: 1 / (√1 + 1) = 1 / (1 + 1) = 1/2.
  • Answer: The limit is 1/2.

🎙️ This example demonstrates the effectiveness of rationalization. By multiplying by the conjugate, we were able to simplify the expression and resolve the indeterminate form, leading to a definite limit.

Algebra of Limits: Rules for Operations

  • If lim f(x) = L and lim g(x) = M as x → a, then:
  • Sum Rule: lim [f(x) + g(x)] = L + M
  • Product Rule: lim [f(x) ⋅ g(x)] = L ⋅ M
  • Quotient Rule: lim [f(x) / g(x)] = L / M (if M ≠ 0)

🎙️ These rules are incredibly useful as they allow us to break down complex limits into simpler ones. They state that limits behave well with basic arithmetic operations, as long as the individual limits exist and we don't divide by zero.

Important Trigonometric Limits

  • lim (sin x / x) as x → 0 = 1
  • lim (1 - cos x) / x as x → 0 = 0
  • lim (tan x / x) as x → 0 = 1
  • These are fundamental for solving many problems.

🎙️ These three trigonometric limit identities are absolutely crucial and must be memorized. They are derived using the Squeeze Theorem and are frequently tested. Make sure you understand when and how to apply them, especially the first one.

Example 4: Trigonometric Limit

  • Step 1: We need (sin θ / θ). Multiply and divide by 4.

Derivative from First Principles

  • The derivative of a function f(x) with respect to x is defined as:
  • f'(x) = lim [f(x + h) - f(x)] / h as h → 0
  • This is also known as the 'definition of the derivative'.
  • It's the limit of the average rate of change over an infinitesimally small interval.

🎙️ This formula, often called the 'first principles' definition, is the heart of derivatives. It represents the slope of the tangent line to the curve at point x, as the interval 'h' shrinks to zero. Understanding this is fundamental.

1Start with f(x)
2Find f(x+h)
3Calculate [f(x+h) - f(x)] / h
4Take limit as h→0
21 / 30

Example 5: Derivative of x² from First Principles

  • Problem: Find the derivative of f(x) = x² using first principles.
  • Step 1: f(x+h) = (x+h)² = x² + 2xh + h².
  • Step 2: f(x+h) - f(x) = (x² + 2xh + h²) - x² = 2xh + h².
  • Step 3: [f(x+h) - f(x)] / h = (2xh + h²) / h = 2x + h.
  • Step 4: lim (2x + h) as h → 0 = 2x + 0 = 2x.
  • Answer: The derivative of x² is 2x.

🎙️ This step-by-step example clearly illustrates how to apply the first principles definition. You substitute, simplify, cancel 'h', and then take the limit. This method helps build a strong conceptual foundation for derivatives.

Geometric Interpretation of the Derivative

  • The derivative f'(x) at a point 'x' represents the slope of the tangent line to the curve y = f(x) at that point.
  • It tells us the 'steepness' of the curve at a specific instant.
  • A positive derivative means the function is increasing; negative means decreasing.

🎙️ Beyond just a rate of change, the derivative has a powerful geometric meaning. It gives us the exact slope of the line that just touches the curve at a single point. This concept is crucial for understanding graphs and optimization problems.

Standard Derivatives: Memorize These!

  • d/dx (c) = 0 (where c is a constant)
  • d/dx (xⁿ) = nxⁿ⁻¹
  • d/dx (sin x) = cos x
  • d/dx (cos x) = -sin x
  • d/dx (eˣ) = eˣ
  • d/dx (log x) = 1/x

🎙️ These are the fundamental derivative formulas you must commit to memory. While they can all be derived from first principles, knowing them directly will save you immense time in problem-solving and exams. Practice using them regularly.

Algebra of Derivatives: Rules for Operations

  • If u and v are differentiable functions of x:
  • Sum/Difference Rule: d/dx (u ± v) = du/dx ± dv/dx
  • Product Rule: d/dx (u ⋅ v) = u(dv/dx) + v(du/dx)
  • Quotient Rule: d/dx (u / v) = [v(du/dx) - u(dv/dx)] / v²

🎙️ Similar to limits, derivatives also have rules for arithmetic operations. The Product and Quotient Rules are particularly important and require careful application. Make sure you practice these extensively to avoid common errors.

Example 6: Product Rule

  • Problem: Differentiate f(x) = x² sin x
  • Step 1: Identify u = x² and v = sin x.
  • Step 2: Find du/dx = 2x and dv/dx = cos x.
  • Step 3: Apply Product Rule: d/dx (uv) = u(dv/dx) + v(du/dx).
  • Step 4: d/dx (x² sin x) = x²(cos x) + sin x(2x).
  • Answer: The derivative is x² cos x + 2x sin x.

🎙️ This example demonstrates the application of the product rule. Ensure you correctly identify 'u' and 'v' and their respective derivatives before plugging them into the formula. Pay attention to the order of terms.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Forgetting to check LHL = RHL for limit existence.
  • Incorrectly applying factorization or rationalization for 0/0 forms.
  • Mixing up trigonometric limit identities (e.g., sin x / x vs (1-cos x)/x).
  • Errors in applying Product and Quotient Rules for derivatives.
  • Differentiating constants as non-zero values.

🎙️ Students often rush and make these simple yet crucial errors. Always verify LHL and RHL for limits. For derivatives, pay close attention to the rules, especially the product and quotient rules, as they are often misapplied. Practice is key to avoiding these pitfalls.

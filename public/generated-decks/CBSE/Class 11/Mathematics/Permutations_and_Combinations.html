Fundamental Principle of Counting: Multiplication Rule

  • If an event can occur in 'm' different ways
  • And a second event can occur in 'n' different ways
  • Then the total number of ways both events can occur in a sequence is m × n

🎙️ The multiplication rule is used when events happen one after another, and each choice affects the total number of possibilities. We multiply the number of ways for each independent event.

FPC Multiplication Rule: An Example

  • You have 3 shirts and 2 pairs of pants.
  • How many different outfits can you create?
  • Solution: 3 (shirts) × 2 (pants) = 6 outfits

🎙️ Let's visualize this: for each shirt, you have two pant options. So, Shirt 1 with Pant 1, Shirt 1 with Pant 2, and so on. This clearly shows 3 multiplied by 2 gives 6 unique combinations.

1Select Shirt (3 ways)
2Select Pant (2 ways)
3Total Outfits (3x2=6)

Fundamental Principle of Counting: Addition Rule

  • If an event can occur in 'm' different ways
  • And another event can occur in 'n' different ways
  • And the two events cannot occur simultaneously (mutually exclusive)
  • Then the total number of ways either event can occur is m + n

🎙️ The addition rule applies when you have choices that are alternatives to each other. You can choose one path OR another, but not both at the same time. The keyword here is 'OR'.

FPC Addition Rule: An Example

  • To travel from City A to City B, you can take 2 different trains or 3 different buses.
  • How many ways can you travel from A to B?
  • Solution: 2 (trains) + 3 (buses) = 5 ways

🎙️ You either take a train OR a bus; you cannot take both for the same journey. Since these are mutually exclusive options, we add the number of ways for each choice to get the total.

1Route by Train (2 ways)
2Route by Bus (3 ways)
3Total Ways (2+3=5)

FPC: Multiplication vs. Addition

  • Multiplication: Use when events happen 'AND' together (sequence)
  • Addition: Use when events happen 'OR' exclusively (alternatives)
  • Crucial to identify the relationship between events

🎙️ This distinction is vital for solving problems correctly. Always ask yourself: are these events happening together in a sequence, or are they mutually exclusive choices? This will guide you to use the correct principle.

Introduction to Permutations

  • A permutation is an arrangement of objects in a specific order.
  • The order of arrangement matters significantly.
  • It's about 'arranging' or 'ordering' items.

🎙️ In permutations, if you change the order of items, it counts as a new arrangement. For example, arranging the letters 'ABC' as 'ABC', 'ACB', 'BAC', 'BCA', 'CAB', 'CBA' are all distinct permutations.

Factorial Notation (n!)

  • The product of all positive integers less than or equal to 'n'.
  • Denoted by n! (read as 'n factorial').
  • n! = n × (n-1) × (n-2) × ... × 2 × 1.
  • By definition, 0! = 1.

🎙️ Factorial notation is a shorthand used extensively in permutations and combinations. Understanding how to calculate and simplify factorials is fundamental to solving problems in this chapter.

Factorial Notation: Solved Examples

  • Calculate 3! = 3 × 2 × 1 = 6
  • Calculate 4! = 4 × 3 × 2 × 1 = 24
  • Calculate 5! = 5 × 4! = 5 × 24 = 120
  • Simplify 7! / 5! = (7 × 6 × 5!) / 5! = 42

🎙️ Notice how we can simplify factorials by expanding the larger one until it matches the smaller one. This technique is very useful in permutation and combination calculations, making them much faster.

Permutations: Arranging 'r' from 'n' Objects

  • Number of permutations of 'n' distinct objects taken 'r' at a time.
  • First position has 'n' choices.
  • Second position has 'n-1' choices, and so on.
  • The r-th position has 'n-r+1' choices.

🎙️ This conceptual understanding, derived directly from the Fundamental Principle of Counting, helps us build the permutation formula. Each selection reduces the available options for the next position.

11st Position (n ways)
22nd Position (n-1 ways)
3...
4rth Position (n-r+1 ways)

Permutations Formula: P(n, r) or nPr

  • The number of permutations of 'n' distinct objects taken 'r' at a time is given by:
  • nPr = n! / (n-r)!, where 0 ≤ r ≤ n.
  • 'n' is the total number of objects.
  • 'r' is the number of objects to be arranged.

🎙️ This is the core formula for permutations. Remember, the 'r' objects are chosen from 'n' distinct objects, and their arrangement matters. Make sure to correctly identify 'n' and 'r' in problems.

Permutations Example 1: Arranging Letters

  • How many different words can be formed using all the letters of the word 'MATH'?
  • Here, n=4 (M, A, T, H) and r=4 (all letters).
  • Solution: 4P4 = 4! / (4-4)! = 4! / 0! = 24 / 1 = 24 ways.

🎙️ Since we are using all the distinct letters, it's a simple case of nPn, which is just n!. Each unique arrangement forms a different 'word', even if it's not a dictionary word.

Permutations Example 2: With Repeated Objects

  • How many different words can be formed using the letters of the word 'APPLE'?
  • Total letters n=5. Letter 'P' repeats 2 times.
  • Solution: n! / (p!) = 5! / 2! = (120) / 2 = 60 ways.

🎙️ When objects are not distinct, we divide by the factorial of the count of each repeated object. This corrects for the overcounting that would occur if we treated identical objects as distinct.

Introduction to Combinations

  • A combination is a selection of objects where the order does NOT matter.
  • It's about 'selecting' or 'choosing' items.
  • Think of forming a group or a committee.

🎙️ Unlike permutations, in combinations, selecting 'A' then 'B' is the same as selecting 'B' then 'A'. The group {A, B} is considered one combination, regardless of the order of selection.

🤝
Selection, Order Doesn't Matter

Combinations Formula: C(n, r) or nCr

  • The number of combinations of 'n' distinct objects taken 'r' at a time is given by:
  • nCr = n! / (r! * (n-r)!), where 0 ≤ r ≤ n.
  • Also written as (n choose r).
  • Note: nCr = nPr / r!

🎙️ This formula essentially takes the number of permutations and divides it by r! to remove the arrangements of the 'r' chosen items, since order doesn't matter for combinations. This is a very powerful formula.

Permutations vs. Combinations: Key Difference

  • Permutations: Order matters (e.g., arranging letters in a word, forming a password).
  • Combinations: Order does NOT matter (e.g., selecting a team, choosing cards).
  • Identifying 'order matters' is crucial for problem-solving.

🎙️ This slide summarizes the most important distinction. When you read a problem, the first thing to ask is whether the arrangement or sequence of items makes a difference. If yes, it's a permutation; if no, it's a combination.

Combinations Example 1: Selecting a Team

  • How many ways can a committee of 3 members be selected from a group of 10 people?
  • Here, n=10 (total people) and r=3 (members to select).
  • Order does not matter in selecting a committee.
  • Solution: 10C3 = 10! / (3! * 7!) = (10 × 9 × 8) / (3 × 2 × 1) = 120 ways.

🎙️ When forming a committee, selecting person A then B then C is the same as selecting B then C then A. Hence, this is a combination problem. The formula helps us quickly find the number of unique groups.

Combinations Example 2: Mixed Selection

  • From a group of 5 men and 4 women, a committee of 3 is to be formed.
  • How many ways if the committee must have exactly 2 men and 1 woman?
  • Select 2 men from 5: 5C2 = 10 ways.
  • Select 1 woman from 4: 4C1 = 4 ways.
  • Total ways (by FPC Multiplication): 10 × 4 = 40 ways.

🎙️ This problem combines two independent combination selections, which are then multiplied using the FPC. It's a common type of problem in exams, requiring careful breakdown into smaller, manageable parts.

Avoiding Common Mistakes

  • Confusing Permutations and Combinations (Order matters vs. Order doesn't).
  • Incorrectly applying FPC (Multiplication vs. Addition).
  • Errors in factorial calculations or simplifications.
  • Not accounting for repeated objects in permutations.

🎙️ These are the most frequent errors students make. Always pause and analyze the problem carefully. Does the sequence matter? Are the choices independent or alternatives? Practice is key to avoiding these pitfalls.

Defining Random Experiments

  • An experiment whose outcome cannot be predicted with certainty.
  • All possible outcomes are known in advance.
  • Can be repeated under identical conditions.

🎙️ A random experiment is the cornerstone of probability. Think about tossing a coin; you know it will be heads or tails, but you can't predict which one it will be for a single toss. This unpredictability, combined with known possible outcomes, defines a random experiment.

Understanding Sample Space (S)

  • The set of all possible outcomes of a random experiment.
  • Represented by 'S'.
  • Crucial for defining events and calculating probabilities.

🎙️ The sample space is essentially the 'universe' of all possible results for your experiment. For example, if you toss two coins, the sample space consists of {HH, HT, TH, TT}. Clearly defining 'S' is the first critical step in solving any probability problem.

What is an Event (E)?

  • A subset of the sample space (S).
  • A collection of some possible outcomes.
  • Example: Getting a 'Head' when tossing a coin.

🎙️ An event is simply a specific outcome or a group of outcomes that we are interested in. If the sample space is rolling a die {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}, then 'getting an even number' is an event, represented as {2, 4, 6}. Events are subsets of the sample space.

Algebra of Events: Operations on Events

  • Union (A ∪ B): A or B or both occur.
  • Intersection (A ∩ B): Both A and B occur.
  • Complement (A'): Event A does not occur.

🎙️ Just like sets, we can perform operations on events to describe more complex scenarios. Union means 'A or B', intersection means 'A and B', and complement means 'not A'. Understanding these operations is key to applying probability rules correctly.

No Overlap: Mutually Exclusive Events

  • Events that cannot occur simultaneously.
  • Their intersection is an empty set (A ∩ B = Φ).
  • Example: Getting a 'Head' and a 'Tail' on a single coin toss.

🎙️ Mutually exclusive events are like parallel lines; they never meet. If one event happens, the other cannot. This concept simplifies probability calculations significantly, especially when dealing with unions of events. Remember, A and B cannot both happen at the same time.

Covering All Possibilities: Exhaustive Events

  • A set of events whose union covers the entire sample space (A₁ ∪ A₂ ∪ ... ∪ Aₙ = S).
  • At least one of the events must occur.
  • Example: Getting an even number or an odd number when rolling a die.

🎙️ Exhaustive events collectively leave no stone unturned in the sample space. Together, they account for every possible outcome of the experiment. This concept is particularly useful when considering partitions of the sample space or when analyzing all possible scenarios.

Fair Chances: Equally Likely Outcomes

  • Outcomes that have the same probability of occurring.
  • No outcome is preferred over another.
  • Assumed in classical probability definition.

🎙️ When we say outcomes are equally likely, it means each outcome has an identical chance of happening. For instance, in a fair coin toss, heads and tails are equally likely. This assumption is crucial for applying the classical definition of probability, which we'll discuss next.

Classical Definition of Probability

  • P(E) = (Number of favorable outcomes) / (Total number of possible outcomes).
  • Applicable when all outcomes are equally likely.
  • Denoted as P(E) = n(E) / n(S).

🎙️ This is the most common and intuitive way to define probability. It's a simple ratio: how many ways can your event happen, divided by the total number of ways anything can happen. Remember, this formula relies on the assumption of equally likely outcomes.

Calculating P(E): Step-by-Step

  • Identify the sample space (S) and its size n(S).
  • Identify the event (E) and its size n(E).
  • Apply the formula: P(E) = n(E) / n(S).

🎙️ Following these steps systematically will help you avoid errors in probability problems. First, clearly list all possibilities, then identify the specific outcomes you're interested in. Finally, apply the ratio to get your probability.

Key Properties of Probability P(E)

  • 0 ≤ P(E) ≤ 1: Probability is always between 0 and 1.
  • P(S) = 1: Probability of the certain event is 1.
  • P(Φ) = 0: Probability of the impossible event is 0.

🎙️ These properties are fundamental. A probability of 0 means an event will never happen, while 1 means it's guaranteed. Any value outside this range indicates a calculation error. The sum of probabilities of all elementary events in a sample space must equal 1.

  • Experiment: Tossing three fair coins.
  • Find P(getting exactly two heads).
  • Find P(getting at least one tail).

🎙️ Let's apply our knowledge. For three coins, the sample space S has 2³ = 8 outcomes. List them carefully: {HHH, HHT, HTH, THH, HTT, THT, TTH, TTT}. Now identify the favorable outcomes for each event and use the formula P(E) = n(E)/n(S).

Worked Example 2: Die Roll Probability

  • Experiment: Rolling a single fair die.
  • Find P(getting an even number).
  • Find P(getting a number greater than 4).

🎙️ Here, the sample space S is {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}, so n(S) = 6. For an even number, E = {2, 4, 6}, so n(E) = 3. P(even) = 3/6 = 1/2. For a number greater than 4, E = {5, 6}, so n(E) = 2. P(>4) = 2/6 = 1/3. Simple, right?

Common Mistake 1: Incorrect Sample Space

  • Failing to list ALL possible outcomes.
  • Assuming outcomes are equally likely when they are not.
  • Example: Two coin tosses, thinking {HH, HT, TT} is S (missing TH).

🎙️ An incorrect sample space is the most frequent cause of errors in probability problems. Always take your time to systematically list every unique outcome. Especially for multiple events, use tree diagrams or tables to ensure completeness and correctness.

Probability of 'OR' (Union of Events)

  • P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A ∩ B).
  • This formula applies when events A and B are NOT mutually exclusive.
  • Subtract P(A ∩ B) to avoid double-counting common outcomes.

🎙️ When you want to find the probability of A or B happening, you add their individual probabilities. However, if they can happen together, you must subtract the probability of their intersection, as those outcomes were counted twice. This is the Addition Theorem of Probability.

Union of Disjoint Events (Mutually Exclusive)

  • If A and B are mutually exclusive, P(A ∩ B) = 0.
  • Formula simplifies: P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B).
  • This is a special case of the general addition rule.

🎙️ This simplified rule is very powerful. When events cannot occur together, calculating the probability of either happening is straightforward: just sum their individual probabilities. Always check if events are mutually exclusive before applying this simplified formula.

Complement of an Event: P(A')

  • P(A') = 1 - P(A).
  • A' (or Aᶜ) represents the event 'not A'.
  • Useful when calculating P(A) directly is complex.

🎙️ The complement rule is incredibly useful. If you know the probability of an event happening, you immediately know the probability of it not happening by subtracting from 1. This often simplifies problems, especially those involving 'at least one' scenarios.

Worked Example 3: Cards - Union & Intersection

  • Experiment: Drawing one card from a standard deck of 52 cards.
  • Event A: Getting a King.
  • Event B: Getting a Heart.
  • Find P(A ∪ B) and P(A ∩ B).

🎙️ Here, n(S) = 52. P(King) = 4/52, P(Heart) = 13/52. The King of Hearts is common to both, so P(King ∩ Heart) = 1/52. Now, apply the union formula: P(King ∪ Heart) = P(King) + P(Heart) - P(King ∩ Heart). This is a classic application of the addition theorem.

Worked Example 4: Mutually Exclusive Application

  • Bag contains 5 red, 3 blue, 2 green balls.
  • One ball is drawn at random.
  • Find P(drawing a red OR a blue ball).

🎙️ Total balls = 5+3+2 = 10. Drawing a red ball and drawing a blue ball are mutually exclusive events – you can't draw both at once. So, P(Red) = 5/10, P(Blue) = 3/10. P(Red or Blue) = P(Red) + P(Blue) = 5/10 + 3/10 = 8/10 = 4/5.

Quick Question 3: Complement Rule Check

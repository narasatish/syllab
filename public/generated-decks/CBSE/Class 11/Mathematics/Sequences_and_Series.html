Understanding Sequences

  • An ordered list of numbers
  • Each number is called a term
  • Follows a specific rule or pattern

🎙️ A sequence is simply a list of numbers arranged in a definite order, according to some rule. Think of it like a line of people, where each person has a specific position determined by a rule.

Arithmetic Progression (AP)

  • A sequence where the difference between consecutive terms is constant
  • This constant difference is called the common difference (d)
  • Example: 2, 5, 8, 11... (d=3)

🎙️ An Arithmetic Progression, or AP, is one of the most common types of sequences. The key characteristic is that you always add or subtract the same number, the common difference, to get to the next term.

📈
Constant Growth

General Term of an AP (an)

  • Formula: an = a + (n-1)d
  • 'a' is the first term
  • 'd' is the common difference
  • 'n' is the term number

🎙️ This formula is incredibly powerful as it allows us to find any term in an AP without having to list all the preceding terms. Understand each component: first term, common difference, and the term number 'n'.

1Start with 'a'
2Add 'd' (n-1) times
7 / 25

Example: Finding the 10th term of an AP

  • AP: 3, 7, 11, 15...
  • First term (a) = 3; Common difference (d) = 7 - 3 = 4
  • Using an = a + (n-1)d, a10 = 3 + (10-1)4
  • a10 = 3 + 9*4 = 3 + 36 = 39

🎙️ Let's walk through this example. First, identify 'a' and 'd' from the given sequence. Then, substitute these values and 'n' into the general term formula. Always double-check your calculations carefully.

Sum of n Terms of an AP (Sn)

  • Formula 1: Sn = n/2 [2a + (n-1)d]
  • Formula 2: Sn = n/2 [a + l], where 'l' is the last term
  • Both formulas yield the same result

🎙️ We have two main formulas for the sum of an AP. The first is useful when you know the first term and common difference. The second is handy if you know the first and last terms. Choose the one that best fits the given information.

Example: Sum of first 20 terms of an AP

  • AP: 5, 8, 11, ...; a = 5, d = 3, n = 20
  • Sn = n/2 [2a + (n-1)d]
  • S20 = 20/2 [2*5 + (20-1)*3]
  • S20 = 10 [10 + 19*3] = 10 [10 + 57] = 10 * 67 = 670

🎙️ Here, we're asked for the sum of the first 20 terms. Identify 'a', 'd', and 'n'. Substitute them into the first sum formula. Be careful with the order of operations, especially multiplication before addition.

Arithmetic Mean (AM)

  • If a, A, b are in AP, then A is the Arithmetic Mean between a and b
  • Formula: A = (a + b) / 2
  • It's simply the average of the two numbers

🎙️ The arithmetic mean is a concept you're already familiar with – it's the average. In the context of an AP, it's the middle term when you have three terms forming an arithmetic progression.

Geometric Progression (GP)

  • A sequence where the ratio between consecutive terms is constant
  • This constant ratio is called the common ratio (r)
  • Example: 2, 6, 18, 54... (r=3)

🎙️ Unlike an AP where we add a constant, in a GP, we multiply by a constant factor to get the next term. This leads to much faster growth or decay, depending on the value of 'r'.

✖️
Constant Multiplier
13 / 25

General Term of a GP (an)

  • Formula: an = a * r^(n-1)
  • 'a' is the first term
  • 'r' is the common ratio
  • 'n' is the term number

🎙️ This formula for the general term of a GP is similar in structure to the AP formula but involves multiplication and exponents. Be very careful with the exponent (n-1) in your calculations.

1Start with 'a'
2Multiply by 'r' (n-1) times
14 / 25

Example: Finding the 6th term of a GP

  • GP: 4, 12, 36...
  • First term (a) = 4; Common ratio (r) = 12/4 = 3
  • Using an = a * r^(n-1), a6 = 4 * 3^(6-1)
  • a6 = 4 * 3^5 = 4 * 243 = 972

🎙️ Again, identify 'a' and 'r' from the sequence. Substitute into the formula. Remember to calculate the exponent first (3^5) before multiplying by 'a' (4). Errors often occur if this order is not followed.

Sum of n Terms of a GP

  • Formula 1 (r > 1): Sn = a(r^n - 1) / (r - 1)
  • Formula 2 (r < 1): Sn = a(1 - r^n) / (1 - r)
  • These formulas are interchangeable, but using the correct one avoids negative denominators

🎙️ We have two versions for the sum of a finite GP. They are mathematically equivalent, but choosing the one that keeps the denominator positive (r-1 or 1-r) makes calculations cleaner and reduces sign errors.

📊
Exponential Sum

Sum of Infinite Geometric Progression (S∞)

  • Applies only when the common ratio |r| < 1
  • Formula: S∞ = a / (1 - r)
  • If |r| ≥ 1, the sum diverges (approaches infinity)

🎙️ This is a special and important case. An infinite GP only has a finite sum if the terms are getting progressively smaller, which happens when the common ratio 'r' is between -1 and 1. Otherwise, the sum just keeps growing indefinitely.

♾️
Converging Sum
17 / 25

Example: Sum of first 5 terms and Infinite Sum

  • GP: 16, 8, 4, ...; a = 16, r = 1/2
  • S5 = 16(1 - (1/2)^5) / (1 - 1/2) = 16(1 - 1/32) / (1/2) = 16(31/32) * 2 = 31
  • S∞ = a / (1 - r) = 16 / (1 - 1/2) = 16 / (1/2) = 32

🎙️ Notice how the sum of the first 5 terms is already very close to the infinite sum. This demonstrates the convergence of the series. Always check the condition |r|<1 before attempting an infinite sum.

Geometric Mean (GM)

  • If a, G, b are in GP, then G is the Geometric Mean between a and b
  • Formula: G = √(ab)
  • Applies only for positive numbers

🎙️ The geometric mean is often used when dealing with growth rates or ratios. It's the middle term in a GP of three terms. Remember, it's defined specifically for positive numbers.

Relationship between AM and GM

  • For any two positive numbers 'a' and 'b', AM ≥ GM
  • That is, (a+b)/2 ≥ √(ab)
  • Equality holds only when a = b

🎙️ This inequality is a very important result in mathematics. It tells us that the arithmetic mean is always greater than or equal to the geometric mean for positive numbers. Be prepared to prove this inequality.

Special Series: Sum of First n Natural Numbers

  • Sum of 1 + 2 + 3 + ... + n
  • Formula: Σn = n(n+1)/2
  • This is a very common and useful formula

🎙️ This formula, often attributed to Gauss, is for the sum of the first 'n' positive integers. It's essentially an AP with a=1 and d=1. This is a fundamental formula you should memorize.

Special Series: Sum of Squares of First n Natural Numbers

  • Formula: Σn^2 = n(n+1)(2n+1)/6
  • This formula is more complex but equally important

🎙️ This formula is for summing the squares of the first 'n' natural numbers. Deriving this can be quite involved, so for now, focus on memorizing and applying it correctly in problems.

Special Series: Sum of Cubes of First n Natural Numbers

  • Sum of 1^3 + 2^3 + 3^3 + ... + n^3
  • Formula: Σn^3 = [n(n+1)/2]^2 = (Σn)^2
  • Notice the interesting relationship with the sum of natural numbers!

🎙️ This formula for the sum of cubes has a beautiful connection to the sum of natural numbers. It's simply the square of the sum of the first 'n' natural numbers. This makes it easier to remember and apply.

Structural Types of Neurons

  • Unipolar: Cell body with one axon (e.g., embryonic stage).
  • Bipolar: One dendrite, one axon (e.g., retina of eye).
  • Multipolar: Many dendrites, one axon (e.g., cerebral cortex).

🎙️ The classification is based on the number of axons and dendrites extending from the cell body. Multipolar neurons are the most common type found in our brain and spinal cord, reflecting their complex roles.

Functional Types of Neurons

Nerve Impulse: The Signal that Travels

  • It's an electrochemical disturbance that travels along a neuron.
  • Often compared to a wave or a domino effect.
  • Involves changes in membrane potential due to ion movement.

🎙️ Imagine pushing the first domino in a long line; the 'wave' of falling dominos is analogous to a nerve impulse. It's not a flow of electrons like in a wire, but rather a rapid change in the electrical charge across the neuron's membrane.

Resting Membrane Potential: The 'Ready' State

  • Neuron membrane is polarized at rest, ~-70mV.
  • Higher K+ inside, higher Na+ outside.
  • Maintained by Na+/K+ pump (3 Na+ out, 2 K+ in) and K+ leak channels.

🎙️ Think of a charged battery; it has potential energy. Similarly, a resting neuron has a resting membrane potential, making it ready to fire an impulse. The sodium-potassium pump is crucial here, actively working to maintain this potential.

🔋
Charged Membrane

Action Potential: Depolarization - The 'Fire' Moment

  • Stimulus reaches threshold, causing voltage-gated Na+ channels to open.
  • Rapid influx of Na+ ions into the cell.
  • Membrane potential reverses, becoming positive (~+30mV).

🎙️ This is the crucial step where the nerve 'fires.' Once the threshold is reached, it's an 'all-or-none' event, meaning the action potential either fires completely or not at all, like flipping a light switch.

Action Potential: Repolarization & Refractory Period

  • Na+ channels inactivate, voltage-gated K+ channels open.
  • K+ efflux out of the cell, restoring negative potential.
  • Refractory period: Brief time when neuron cannot fire another impulse.

🎙️ After firing, the neuron needs to reset itself, which is repolarization. The refractory period ensures that the impulse travels in one direction and gives the neuron a brief rest before it can fire again, preventing constant stimulation.

Conduction of Nerve Impulse: Speed Matters!

  • Myelinated axons: Impulse 'jumps' between Nodes of Ranvier (saltatory conduction).
  • Unmyelinated axons: Impulse propagates continuously along the membrane.
  • Myelinated axons conduct impulses much faster.

🎙️ Myelin sheath acts like insulation, preventing leakage of ions and allowing the impulse to 'skip' along. This saltatory conduction is incredibly efficient, which is why our fast reflexes rely on myelinated neurons.

Types of Synapses: Electrical vs. Chemical

  • Electrical: Direct flow of ions via gap junctions, very fast.
  • Chemical: Neurotransmitters released into synaptic cleft, slower but more flexible.
  • Chemical synapses are more common in our body.

🎙️ Electrical synapses are like a direct wire connection, allowing for very rapid, synchronized firing. Chemical synapses, however, allow for more complex processing, modulation, and integration of signals, which is vital for brain function.

Chemical Synaptic Transmission: Step-by-Step

  • Action potential arrives at presynaptic terminal.
  • Ca2+ channels open, Ca2+ influx.
  • Neurotransmitter vesicles fuse with membrane, releasing neurotransmitters into cleft.
  • Neurotransmitters bind to receptors on postsynaptic membrane, causing potential change.

🎙️ This is a detailed process that ensures precise communication. Remember the role of calcium ions; they are the trigger for neurotransmitter release. The binding of neurotransmitters then determines whether the next neuron is excited or inhibited.

Neurotransmitters: The Chemical Messengers

  • Chemicals that transmit signals across a synapse.
  • Excitatory: Depolarize postsynaptic membrane (e.g., Acetylcholine, Glutamate).
  • Inhibitory: Hyperpolarize postsynaptic membrane (e.g., GABA, Glycine).

🎙️ Neurotransmitters are like keys that fit into specific locks (receptors) on the postsynaptic neuron. Their action determines whether the signal is passed on or stopped, allowing for complex decision-making in the nervous system.

The Reflex Arc: The Pathway of Reaction

  • Sensory receptor detects stimulus.
  • Sensory neuron transmits impulse to CNS.
  • Interneuron (in spinal cord) processes and relays.
  • Motor neuron transmits impulse to effector (muscle/gland).
  • Effector responds.

🎙️ This is the classic pathway for a reflex action. Note that the brain is not directly involved in the immediate reflex, though it receives information about the event later. This ensures speed over conscious control for protective responses.

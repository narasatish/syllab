What is Photosynthesis?

  • Process by which green plants synthesize organic food
  • Uses light energy, CO2, and water
  • Releases oxygen as a byproduct
  • Converts light energy into chemical energy

🎙️ Photosynthesis is the cornerstone process where plants convert inorganic substances into organic food using sunlight. Remember the overall equation: 6CO2 + 6H2O + Light Energy → C6H12O6 + 6O2. This process is vital for sustaining nearly all life on Earth.

Photosynthetic Pigments

  • Carotenoids: yellow, orange, red pigments

🎙️ Chlorophyll 'a' is the primary pigment, directly involved in light energy conversion. Chlorophyll 'b' and carotenoids are accessory pigments; they absorb light at different wavelengths and transfer the energy to chlorophyll 'a', protecting it from photo-oxidation. Light-Dependent Reactions (LDR) - Overview

  • Occur in the thylakoid membranes
  • Require light energy
  • Produce ATP and NADPH
  • Involve Photosystems I and II

🎙️ The light-dependent reactions are the initial phase of photosynthesis where light energy is captured and converted into chemical energy in the form of ATP and NADPH. These energy carriers are then used in the next phase, the dark reactions. Remember, oxygen is also released here.

Photosystem I (PSI) & Photosystem II (PSII)

  • Light-harvesting complexes with reaction centers
  • PSII (P680) absorbs light at 680 nm
  • PSI (P700) absorbs light at 700 nm
  • PSII is located in appressed regions, PSI in non-appressed

🎙️ Photosystems are crucial pigment-protein complexes. PSII initiates the electron transport chain by splitting water, while PSI receives electrons from PSII or participates in cyclic flow. Their distinct absorption maxima and locations are important for exam questions.

Non-Cyclic Photophosphorylation (Z-scheme)

  • Involves both PSII and PSI
  • Electrons flow from water → PSII → PSI → NADP+
  • Produces ATP and NADPH
  • Water splitting (photolysis) occurs at PSII

🎙️ The Z-scheme is the predominant pathway, generating both ATP and NADPH. Electrons move unidirectionally, starting from water and ultimately reducing NADP+. Remember the sequence: Water → PSII → Plastoquinone → Cytochrome b6f complex → Plastocyanin → PSI → Ferredoxin → NADP+ reductase.

Cyclic Photophosphorylation

  • Involves only PSI
  • Electrons cycle back to PSI from ETC
  • Produces only ATP, no NADPH
  • Occurs when CO2 is limiting or high light intensity

🎙️ Cyclic photophosphorylation is a simpler pathway that only produces ATP. It's important when the cell needs more ATP than NADPH, or when NADP+ is unavailable. No water splitting occurs, so no oxygen is released. It primarily happens in the stromal lamellae.

Chemiosmotic Hypothesis

  • Explains ATP synthesis in chloroplasts (and mitochondria)
  • Proton gradient across thylakoid membrane drives ATP synthase
  • Protons accumulate in thylakoid lumen
  • Energy from electron transport used to pump protons

🎙️ This hypothesis, proposed by Peter Mitchell, is crucial for understanding how ATP is generated. The accumulation of protons in the thylakoid lumen creates a proton motive force. As protons flow back through ATP synthase, ADP is phosphorylated to ATP. This is a high-yield concept.

Light-Independent Reactions (Calvin Cycle) - Overview

  • Occur in the stroma of chloroplasts
  • Do not directly require light
  • Use ATP and NADPH from light reactions
  • Fix CO2 into glucose

🎙️ The Calvin cycle, also known as the C3 pathway or dark reactions, is where the actual synthesis of sugars happens. It uses the energy (ATP) and reducing power (NADPH) generated during the light reactions to convert atmospheric CO2 into stable organic compounds. This cycle is universal in all photosynthetic eukaryotes.

Calvin Cycle - Step 1: Carboxylation

  • CO2 is fixed by RuBisCO enzyme
  • Combines with Ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate (RuBP)
  • Forms two molecules of 3-Phosphoglyceric acid (PGA)
  • PGA is the first stable 3-carbon product

🎙️ Carboxylation is the most critical step as it's the entry point of CO2 into the cycle. RuBisCO, the most abundant enzyme on Earth, catalyzes this reaction. The formation of 3-PGA, a 3-carbon compound, gives the pathway its name 'C3 pathway'.

Calvin Cycle - Step 2: Reduction

  • PGA is converted to Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (G3P)
  • Requires 2 ATP for phosphorylation per CO2 fixed
  • Requires 2 NADPH for reduction per CO2 fixed
  • One G3P molecule exits to form sugars

🎙️ This is the energy-consuming phase where the fixed carbon is reduced. The ATP and NADPH produced during the light reactions are utilized here. For every 6 molecules of G3P formed, one exits the cycle to synthesize glucose or other carbohydrates.

Calvin Cycle - Step 3: Regeneration

  • RuBP is regenerated to continue the cycle
  • Requires 1 ATP per CO2 fixed
  • Ensures continuous CO2 fixation
  • Involves several complex enzymatic reactions

🎙️ The regeneration of RuBP is vital because it allows the cycle to continue accepting new CO2 molecules. This step consumes another ATP molecule. In total, for every CO2 molecule fixed, 3 ATP and 2 NADPH molecules are consumed in the Calvin cycle.

C3 Pathway Summary

  • First stable product is 3-PGA (3-carbon)
  • Uses RuBisCO for CO2 fixation
  • Occurs in mesophyll cells
  • Common in temperate plants like wheat, rice

🎙️ Most plants follow the C3 pathway. While efficient in moderate conditions, RuBisCO's dual nature (carboxylation and oxygenation) makes C3 plants susceptible to photorespiration, especially in hot and dry climates. This is a key limitation to remember.

C4 Pathway - Introduction

  • Adaptation for hot, dry environments
  • Minimizes photorespiration
  • Characterized by 'Kranz Anatomy'
  • Examples: maize, sugarcane, sorghum

🎙️ C4 plants have evolved a specialized mechanism to overcome the limitations of C3 plants. Their unique anatomy and biochemistry allow them to concentrate CO2, thereby enhancing photosynthetic efficiency and reducing photorespiration, which is a major energy drain.

C4 Pathway - Mechanism

  • CO2 fixed in mesophyll cells by PEP carboxylase
  • Forms 4-carbon oxaloacetic acid (OAA)
  • OAA converted to malate/aspartate, transported to bundle sheath cells
  • CO2 released in bundle sheath, enters Calvin cycle

🎙️ The C4 pathway involves a spatial separation of initial CO2 fixation and the Calvin cycle. PEP carboxylase has a high affinity for CO2, allowing C4 plants to fix CO2 even at low concentrations. This effectively 'pumps' CO2 into the bundle sheath cells, where RuBisCO operates efficiently.

C3 vs C4 Plants: Key Differences

  • C3: Mesophyll cells only; C4: Mesophyll + Bundle Sheath
  • C3: RuBisCO; C4: PEPcase (mesophyll) + RuBisCO (bundle sheath)
  • C3: Photorespiration high; C4: Photorespiration negligible
  • C3: Less efficient in hot/dry; C4: More efficient in hot/dry

🎙️ This comparison is a favorite for exam questions! Understand the anatomical, biochemical, and physiological differences. C4 plants require more ATP (5 ATP per CO2 vs 3 ATP in C3) but gain efficiency by avoiding photorespiration in challenging environments.

Photorespiration: The Wasteful Process

  • RuBisCO binds O2 instead of CO2
  • Occurs at high O2 and low CO2 concentrations
  • Produces no ATP or NADPH
  • Wastes fixed carbon, reduces photosynthetic yield

🎙️ Photorespiration is a detrimental process where RuBisCO acts as an oxygenase, leading to the loss of already fixed carbon. It's a major reason for the lower productivity of C3 plants in hot and dry conditions. C4 plants have evolved mechanisms to suppress this wasteful pathway.

Blackman's Law of Limiting Factors

  • Proposed by F.F. Blackman in 1905
  • When a process is conditioned by several factors, the rate is limited by the factor that is nearest its minimum value
  • If the limiting factor is changed, the rate of the process will increase
  • Example: Low light limits rate, even if CO2 is abundant

🎙️ This fundamental law states that the rate of photosynthesis is determined by the factor that is in shortest supply. If light is low, increasing CO2 won't increase the rate until light intensity is also increased. This helps explain why plants optimize their environment for growth.

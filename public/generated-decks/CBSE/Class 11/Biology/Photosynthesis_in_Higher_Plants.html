Photosynthesis: The Definition

  • Process by which green plants synthesize organic food.
  • Uses CO2, water, and light energy.
  • Releases oxygen as a byproduct.

🎙️ Photosynthesis literally means 'synthesis using light'. It's the miracle where inorganic substances like carbon dioxide and water are transformed into energy-rich organic compounds like glucose, powered by sunlight.

Early Experiments: Jan Ingenhousz (1779)

  • Light is essential for plant processes.
  • Aquatic plant experiment with bubbles.
  • Bubbles (oxygen) only formed in light.

🎙️ Ingenhousz took Priestley's work further, showing that sunlight is absolutely crucial for this 'purification' process. He observed that only the green parts of plants, and only in the presence of light, could release oxygen.

Where Does Photosynthesis Occur?

  • Primarily in the leaves of plants.
  • Within specialized organelles: Chloroplasts.
  • Chloroplasts contain chlorophyll pigments.

🎙️ The primary site of photosynthesis in higher plants is the leaf, and more specifically, within the mesophyll cells of the leaf. These cells are packed with chloroplasts, the actual factories where food is made.

The Chloroplast: Structure & Function

  • Double membrane-bound organelle.
  • Stroma: Fluid-filled matrix.
  • Thylakoids: Flattened sacs, stacked into Grana.
  • Lumen: Space inside thylakoid.

🎙️ Chloroplasts have an intricate internal structure. The light-dependent reactions occur on the thylakoid membranes, while the light-independent reactions, or Calvin cycle, take place in the stroma, the fluid-filled space.

Light-Dependent Reactions: The Energy Capture

  • Occur in thylakoid membranes.
  • Light energy is converted to chemical energy.
  • Produces ATP and NADPH.
  • Oxygen is released.

🎙️ These reactions are the first stage of photosynthesis, directly utilizing light energy. They essentially capture the sun's energy and store it in the form of ATP, an energy currency, and NADPH, a reducing power, which will be used in the next stage.

Electron Transport Chain (ETC): The Z-Scheme

  • Non-cyclic photophosphorylation pathway.
  • Electrons flow from PS II to PS I.
  • Forms a 'Z' shape when redox potentials are plotted.
  • Involves Photosystem II and Photosystem I.

🎙️ The Z-scheme describes the path of electrons during non-cyclic photophosphorylation. It's a series of redox reactions where electrons, excited by light in Photosystem II, move through carriers, lose energy, get re-excited in Photosystem I, and finally reduce NADP+.

1PS II absorbs light, electrons excited
2Electrons pass through ETC to PS I
3PS I absorbs light, electrons re-excited
4Electrons reduce NADP+ to NADPH

Photophosphorylation: Cyclic Flow

  • Involves only Photosystem I (PS I).
  • Electrons cycle back to PS I.
  • Produces only ATP, no NADPH or O2.
  • Occurs when ATP is needed more than NADPH.

🎙️ Cyclic photophosphorylation is a simpler process involving only Photosystem I. Electrons, after being excited, are returned to PS I, generating only ATP. This pathway is crucial when the cell needs extra ATP to balance the ATP:NADPH ratio required for the Calvin cycle.

Photophosphorylation: Non-Cyclic Flow

  • Involves both PS I and PS II.
  • Electrons do not return to their origin.
  • Produces ATP, NADPH, and releases O2.
  • The primary pathway for ATP and NADPH synthesis.

🎙️ Non-cyclic photophosphorylation is the more common and complete pathway. It involves both photosystems, leading to the production of both ATP and NADPH, which are vital for the synthesis of sugars. Water splitting provides the replacement electrons and releases oxygen.

Light-Independent Reactions: The Calvin Cycle

  • Occur in the stroma of chloroplasts.
  • Uses ATP and NADPH from light reactions.
  • Fixes CO2 to synthesize sugars.
  • Also known as the C3 pathway.

🎙️ The Calvin cycle is where the 'food' is actually made. It doesn't directly use light, but it depends entirely on the ATP and NADPH generated during the light-dependent reactions to convert carbon dioxide into glucose.

Calvin Cycle: Step 1 - Carboxylation

  • CO2 is incorporated into a 5-carbon sugar, RuBP.
  • Catalyzed by the enzyme RuBisCO.
  • Forms two molecules of 3-PGA (a 3-carbon compound).

🎙️ This is the crucial step where atmospheric carbon dioxide is 'fixed' or incorporated into an organic molecule. RuBisCO, the most abundant enzyme on Earth, plays a central role here, combining CO2 with Ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate (RuBP).

Calvin Cycle: Step 2 - Reduction

  • 3-PGA molecules are converted to G3P (triose phosphate).
  • Requires ATP for phosphorylation.
  • Requires NADPH for reduction.
  • Some G3P leaves to form glucose.

🎙️ In this step, the energy stored in ATP and the reducing power of NADPH are put to use. The 3-PGA molecules are reduced to form glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (G3P). This is where the actual sugar is formed, with some G3P being used to build glucose.

Calvin Cycle: Step 3 - Regeneration

  • Remaining G3P molecules regenerate RuBP.
  • Requires ATP for phosphorylation.
  • Ensures continuous cycle operation.
  • Essential for CO2 fixation to continue.

🎙️ To keep the cycle running, the initial CO2 acceptor, RuBP, must be regenerated. The remaining G3P molecules undergo a series of reactions, consuming more ATP, to reform RuBP. This regeneration step is vital for the continuous fixation of CO2.

Photorespiration: A 'Wasteful' Process

  • Occurs when RuBisCO binds O2 instead of CO2.
  • Happens at high O2 and low CO2 concentrations.
  • Consumes ATP and NADPH, releases CO2.
  • No sugar synthesis, reduces photosynthetic efficiency.

🎙️ Photorespiration is a process that reduces the efficiency of photosynthesis, especially in hot, dry conditions. RuBisCO, unfortunately, has an affinity for oxygen as well as carbon dioxide. When it binds oxygen, it initiates a pathway that consumes energy and releases CO2, without producing any sugar.

C4 Pathway: An Adaptation to Avoid Photorespiration

  • Found in plants like maize, sugarcane.
  • Has Kranz anatomy in leaves.
  • Initial CO2 fixation in mesophyll cells.
  • Transfers CO2 to bundle sheath cells.

🎙️ C4 plants have evolved a clever mechanism to minimize photorespiration. They have a specialized leaf anatomy called Kranz anatomy and use a different initial CO2 fixation pathway, effectively concentrating CO2 around RuBisCO to ensure it binds CO2, not O2.

C4 Pathway: Key Steps

  • CO2 + PEP → OAA in mesophyll (PEPcase enzyme).
  • OAA converts to malate/aspartate.
  • Malate/aspartate transported to bundle sheath cells.
  • CO2 released, enters Calvin cycle in bundle sheath.

🎙️ The C4 pathway involves a spatial separation of CO2 fixation. PEP carboxylase (PEPcase) in mesophyll cells, which has a higher affinity for CO2 than RuBisCO, fixes CO2 into a 4-carbon compound. This compound then delivers CO2 to the bundle sheath cells, creating a high CO2 environment for RuBisCO.

Factors Affecting Photosynthesis

  • Light intensity, CO2 concentration, Temperature, Water.
  • Blackman's Law of Limiting Factors.
  • Rate is limited by the factor in shortest supply.

🎙️ Photosynthesis is influenced by several external and internal factors. Blackman's Law states that if a process is affected by multiple factors, its rate is determined by the factor that is nearest its minimum or optimal level, effectively 'limiting' the overall rate.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Confusing cyclic and non-cyclic photophosphorylation products.
  • Mixing up C3 and C4 pathway initial CO2 acceptors.
  • Forgetting the role of water in oxygen release.
  • Misunderstanding photorespiration's impact.

🎙️ Students often get confused between the products of cyclic and non-cyclic photophosphorylation. Remember, non-cyclic produces both ATP and NADPH, plus oxygen, while cyclic only produces ATP. Also, keep the primary CO2 acceptors for C3 (RuBP) and C4 (PEP) distinct.

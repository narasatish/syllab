What is Plant Morphology?

  • Study of external features of plants
  • Focuses on roots, stems, leaves, flowers, fruits, seeds
  • Helps in classification and identification of plants

🎙️ Plant morphology is essentially the study of the form and structure of plants. It involves observing and describing the various external parts, from the roots anchoring the plant to the vibrant flowers attracting pollinators. This field is vital for botanical classification and recognizing different plant species.

The Root System: Foundation of Life

  • Underground part of the plant axis
  • Develops from the radicle of the embryo
  • Main functions: anchorage, water & mineral absorption

🎙️ The root system is the subterranean part of the plant, often unseen but absolutely essential. It originates from the radicle during seed germination. Its primary roles are to firmly anchor the plant in the soil and absorb water and dissolved minerals, which are then transported to other parts of the plant.

Types of Root Systems

  • Tap Root: Primary root with lateral branches (dicots)
  • Fibrous Root: Cluster of roots from stem base (monocots)
  • Adventitious Roots: Arise from parts other than radicle

🎙️ We primarily distinguish between two main types: the tap root system, common in dicots, which features a prominent main root, and the fibrous root system, typical of monocots, where many roots originate from the stem base. Adventitious roots, a third category, can arise from any plant part other than the radicle, like a stem or leaf.

Regions of the Root Tip

  • Root Cap: Protection for delicate meristem
  • Meristematic Zone: Rapid cell division
  • Elongation Zone: Cells increase in length
  • Maturation Zone: Differentiation into tissues

🎙️ A root tip exhibits distinct regions, each with a specific function. The root cap protects the delicate meristematic zone, where rapid cell division occurs. Above this, the elongation zone is where cells grow significantly, pushing the root deeper, followed by the maturation zone where cells differentiate into specialized tissues like xylem and phloem.

Modifications of Tap Root for Storage

  • Conical: Carrot (Daucus carota)
  • Fusiform: Radish (Raphanus sativus)
  • Napiform: Turnip (Brassica rapa)

🎙️ Tap roots can be modified to store food, resulting in various shapes. Conical roots, like carrots, are broad at the base and taper downwards. Fusiform roots, such as radishes, are swollen in the middle and taper at both ends. Napiform roots, like turnips, are spherical at the top and abruptly taper at the bottom.

Modifications of Adventitious Roots

  • Storage: Sweet Potato (tuberous roots)
  • Prop Roots: Banyan tree (support from branches)
  • Stilt Roots: Maize, Sugarcane (support from lower nodes)

🎙️ Adventitious roots also undergo various modifications. Sweet potato stores food in its tuberous roots. Prop roots, seen in the Banyan tree, grow vertically downwards from branches, providing additional support. Stilt roots, found in maize and sugarcane, grow obliquely from the lower nodes of the stem, offering mechanical support.

Key Functions of the Root System

  • Anchorage: Firmly holds plant in soil
  • Absorption: Water and minerals from soil
  • Storage: Food reserves (e.g., carrot)
  • Synthesis: Plant growth regulators

🎙️ Beyond just anchoring the plant, roots are crucial for absorbing water and essential minerals from the soil. They also serve as storage organs for food in many plants, like carrots and radishes. Furthermore, roots are known to synthesize certain plant growth regulators, playing a role in overall plant development.

The Stem: The Ascending Axis

  • Ascending part of the plant axis
  • Develops from the plumule of the embryo
  • Bears leaves, flowers, and fruits at nodes

🎙️ The stem is the aerial, ascending part of the plant, developing from the plumule of the embryo. It forms the main axis, bearing leaves at nodes, and eventually flowers and fruits. Stems are crucial for positioning leaves to maximize sunlight absorption and for transporting substances.

🌳

Underground Stem Modifications

  • Rhizome: Ginger, Turmeric (horizontal growth)
  • Tuber: Potato (swollen tip of stolon)
  • Corm: Colocasia, Saffron (condensed, vertical)
  • Bulb: Onion, Garlic (reduced stem with fleshy leaves)

🎙️ Underground stems are modified for food storage and perennation. Rhizomes like ginger grow horizontally. Tubers, such as potatoes, are swollen underground stem tips. Corms like colocasia are condensed, vertical stems, while bulbs, like onions, have a reduced stem disc surrounded by fleshy leaves.

Sub-aerial Stem Modifications

  • Runner: Grasses, Strawberry (creeping stem)
  • Stolon: Jasmine (arches downwards to root)
  • Offset: Pistia, Eichhornia (short, thick runner)
  • Sucker: Mint, Pineapple (underground, then oblique)

🎙️ Sub-aerial stems are partially above and partially below ground, aiding in vegetative propagation. Runners, like in grasses, spread horizontally. Stolons, as in jasmine, arch downwards to root. Offsets are short, thick runners seen in aquatic plants. Suckers emerge from underground stems and grow obliquely upwards.

Aerial Stem Modifications

  • Stem Tendrils: Cucumber, Grapes (climbing support)
  • Thorns: Bougainvillea, Citrus (protection)
  • Phylloclade: Opuntia (flattened, photosynthetic stem)
  • Cladode: Asparagus (one or two internode long stem)

🎙️ Aerial stems also undergo modifications for various functions. Stem tendrils, found in grapes, help the plant climb. Thorns, like those in citrus, provide protection from herbivores. Phylloclades, seen in Opuntia, are flattened, green stems that perform photosynthesis, mimicking leaves in arid regions. Cladodes are similar but typically have limited growth.

Essential Functions of the Stem

  • Support: Holds leaves, flowers, fruits
  • Conduction: Water, minerals, food transport
  • Storage: Food and water (e.g., potato)
  • Vegetative Propagation: Asexual reproduction

🎙️ The stem provides crucial structural support, holding leaves in optimal positions for photosynthesis and elevating flowers for pollination. It's the primary conduit for transporting water and minerals from roots to leaves, and manufactured food from leaves to other parts. Many stems also store food and water, and are vital for vegetative propagation.

The Leaf: The Food Factory

  • Flattened, lateral outgrowth from stem node
  • Contains chlorophyll for photosynthesis
  • Parts: Leaf base, petiole, lamina (leaf blade)

🎙️ The leaf is a vital photosynthetic organ, typically a flattened, green outgrowth from a stem node. It's designed to efficiently capture sunlight for photosynthesis. A typical leaf consists of three main parts: the leaf base, which attaches to the stem; the petiole, or stalk; and the lamina, or the broad, flat leaf blade.

Types of Leaves: Simple vs. Compound

  • Simple Leaf: Single, undivided lamina
  • Compound Leaf: Lamina divided into leaflets
  • Pinnately Compound: Leaflets on common axis (rachis)
  • Palmately Compound: Leaflets attached at one point

🎙️ Leaves are broadly categorized into simple and compound. A simple leaf has an undivided lamina, though it may be incised without reaching the midrib. In contrast, a compound leaf has its lamina divided into distinct leaflets. Compound leaves can be pinnately compound, like a neem leaf, or palmately compound, like a silk cotton leaf.

Phyllotaxy: Arrangement of Leaves

  • Alternate: Single leaf at each node (e.g., China rose)
  • Opposite: Two leaves at each node (e.g., Guava)
  • Whorled: More than two leaves at each node (e.g., Alstonia)

🎙️ Phyllotaxy refers to the pattern of leaf arrangement on the stem or branch. In alternate phyllotaxy, a single leaf arises at each node in an alternating manner. Opposite phyllotaxy involves two leaves at each node, facing each other. Whorled phyllotaxy occurs when more than two leaves arise at a node, forming a circle.

Leaf Modifications for Special Functions

  • Leaf Tendrils: Peas (climbing support)
  • Spines: Cacti (protection, reduced transpiration)
  • Fleshy Leaves: Onion (food storage)
  • Phyllode: Australian Acacia (petiole becomes leaf-like)

🎙️ Leaves can also be modified for various purposes. Leaf tendrils, like those in peas, help the plant climb. Spines, as seen in cacti, reduce water loss and offer protection. Fleshy leaves in onions store food. A phyllode, found in some Australian Acacias, is a flattened petiole that performs photosynthesis after the true leaf blade falls off.

Primary Functions of the Leaf

  • Photosynthesis: Food production for the plant
  • Transpiration: Water evaporation, cooling, ascent of sap
  • Gaseous Exchange: CO2 intake, O2 release via stomata
  • Storage: Food and water in some cases (e.g., fleshy leaves)

🎙️ The most critical function of a leaf is photosynthesis, converting light energy into chemical energy. Leaves also regulate water loss through transpiration, which helps in cooling the plant and driving the ascent of sap. Gaseous exchange for respiration and photosynthesis occurs through stomata on the leaf surface.

The Flower: The Reproductive Unit

  • Modified shoot for sexual reproduction
  • Composed of four whorls: Calyx, Corolla, Androecium, Gynoecium
  • Attached to stem by pedicel on thalamus

🎙️ The flower is the reproductive organ of angiosperms, essentially a highly modified shoot. It typically consists of four distinct whorls arranged on the thalamus: the sepals (calyx), petals (corolla), stamens (androecium), and carpels (gynoecium). Understanding these parts is crucial for comprehending plant reproduction.

Classifying Flowers

  • Complete: Has all four whorls
  • Incomplete: Lacks one or more whorls
  • Bisexual: Both androecium & gynoecium present
  • Unisexual: Only one reproductive whorl present

🎙️ Flowers can be classified based on the presence of their whorls and reproductive organs. A complete flower has all four whorls, while an incomplete one lacks at least one. Bisexual flowers contain both male (androecium) and female (gynoecium) parts, whereas unisexual flowers have only one, making them either male or female.

Inflorescence: Arrangement of Flowers

  • Racemose: Main axis grows indefinitely, flowers lateral (acropetal)
  • Cymose: Main axis terminates in a flower, limited growth (basipetal)
  • Solitary: Single flower arising directly from the stem

🎙️ Inflorescence refers to the arrangement of flowers on the floral axis. In racemose inflorescence, the main axis grows indefinitely, and flowers are borne laterally in an acropetal succession (older at base, younger at apex). In cymose inflorescence, the main axis terminates in a flower, limiting its growth, and flowers are borne in a basipetal succession.

The Fruit and The Seed

  • Fruit: Mature or ripened ovary after fertilization
  • Seed: Ovule matured after fertilization
  • Primary functions: Protection and dispersal of seeds

🎙️ After fertilization, the ovary matures into a fruit, which primarily functions to protect the developing seeds. The ovules within the ovary develop into seeds, containing the embryo and stored food. Fruits and seeds are crucial for the survival and dispersal of plant species, ensuring the continuation of life cycles.

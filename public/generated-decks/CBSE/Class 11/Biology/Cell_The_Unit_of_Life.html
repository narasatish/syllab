What is a Cell?

  • Basic structural and functional unit of all living organisms.
  • Capable of independent existence.
  • Performs all essential life functions.

🎙️ Think of a cell as the smallest building block that can still do everything needed for life. From a tiny bacterium to a giant whale, every organism is either a single cell or made up of many cells working together.

The Discovery of Cell

  • Robert Hooke (1665): Observed 'honeycomb' structures in cork slice.
  • Coined the term 'cell' (Latin 'cellula' - small room).
  • Anton von Leeuwenhoek (1674): First saw living cells (bacteria, protozoa) in pond water.

🎙️ The journey began with Robert Hooke looking at dead cork cells. It was Leeuwenhoek, with improved microscopes, who truly opened our eyes to the vibrant, living world within a single drop of water. Remember both names and their contributions.

Cell Theory: Early Postulates

  • Matthias Schleiden (1838): German botanist, all plants are made of cells.
  • Theodor Schwann (1839): British zoologist, all animals are made of cells.
  • Schwann proposed that cells have a thin outer layer (plasma membrane) and plant cells have a cell wall.

🎙️ These two scientists independently studied different kingdoms but came to similar conclusions. Their combined observations laid the groundwork for one of biology's most fundamental theories. Note Schwann's early insight into the plasma membrane.

Modern Cell Theory & Virchow

  • Rudolf Virchow (1855): 'Omnis cellula e cellula' (All cells arise from pre-existing cells).
  • Challenged spontaneous generation.
  • Completed the cell theory, making it universally accepted.

🎙️ Virchow's contribution was pivotal. Before him, the origin of new cells was unclear. His statement clarified that life begets life at the cellular level, completing the comprehensive cell theory we know today.

Summary: Key Postulates

  • All living organisms are composed of cells and products of cells.
  • All cells arise from pre-existing cells.
  • The cell is the basic structural and functional unit of all living organisms.

🎙️ These three points are the essence of the Cell Theory. Make sure you can state them clearly. They are frequently asked in exams and form the basis for understanding biology.

Prokaryotic vs. Eukaryotic

  • Prokaryotic: Primitive, simpler, no membrane-bound organelles.
  • Eukaryotic: Advanced, complex, distinct nucleus and organelles.
  • Fundamental division based on internal organization.

🎙️ This is a crucial distinction. Prokaryotes are like basic studio apartments, while eukaryotes are like multi-room houses with specialized rooms. Understanding this difference is key to studying cell biology.

Prokaryotic Cell: A Simpler Design

  • No true nucleus (nucleoid region with genetic material).
  • No membrane-bound organelles (e.g., ER, Golgi).
  • Possess ribosomes, cell wall, plasma membrane, cytoplasm.

🎙️ Examples include bacteria and cyanobacteria. Despite their simplicity, they are incredibly diverse and successful. Their genetic material is not enclosed in a nuclear envelope, and they lack complex internal compartmentalization.

Eukaryotic Cell: Complex Organization

  • True nucleus with nuclear envelope.
  • Numerous membrane-bound organelles (e.g., mitochondria, ER, Golgi).
  • Found in plants, animals, fungi, and protists.

🎙️ Eukaryotic cells are much larger and more sophisticated. The presence of a nucleus and specialized organelles allows for efficient division of labor and complex metabolic processes. This organization is a hallmark of higher life forms.

Diversity in Cell Size & Shape

  • Size: Mycoplasma (smallest, 0.3 µm) to Ostrich egg (largest, 15 cm).
  • Shape: Varies with function (e.g., nerve cells long, RBC biconcave).
  • Examples: RBCs, WBCs, columnar epithelial cells, nerve cells.

🎙️ Cells are not all uniform! Their size and shape are perfectly adapted to their specific roles within an organism. A neuron's long process allows it to transmit signals over distances, while a red blood cell's shape helps it squeeze through capillaries.

The Plasma Membrane: Boundary of Life

  • Outer boundary of animal cells, inner to cell wall in plant cells.
  • Composed of lipids (bilayer) and proteins (Fluid Mosaic Model).
  • Selectively permeable, regulating passage of substances.

🎙️ The plasma membrane is not just a static barrier; it's a dynamic, fluid structure. The Fluid Mosaic Model, proposed by Singer and Nicolson, best describes its structure. Its selective permeability is vital for maintaining cell homeostasis.

The Cell Wall: Structural Support

  • Rigid outer covering in plant cells, fungi, algae, and some prokaryotes.
  • Provides structural support, protection, and prevents bursting.
  • Plant cell wall: cellulose; Fungi: chitin; Algae: cellulose, galactans, mannans.

🎙️ The cell wall is crucial for plants, giving them shape and strength against gravity and turgor pressure. Remember, it's fully permeable, unlike the selectively permeable plasma membrane. The chemical composition varies across different organisms.

A plant cell is a eukaryotic cell with a rigid cell wall, large vacuole, and chloroplasts.
14 / 25

Cytoplasm: The Cellular Arena

  • Main arena of cellular activities in both prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells.
  • Comprises cytosol (fluid matrix) and organelles.
  • Site of many metabolic pathways (e.g., glycolysis).

🎙️ Everything inside the plasma membrane, excluding the nucleus in eukaryotes, is the cytoplasm. It's where most of the cell's work happens – chemical reactions, protein synthesis, and more. It's a dynamic, bustling environment.

The Endomembrane System: Coordinated Functions

  • Includes Endoplasmic Reticulum (ER), Golgi apparatus, Lysosomes, and Vacuoles.
  • Their functions are coordinated.
  • Mitochondria, chloroplasts, peroxisomes are *not* part of this system.

🎙️ This system is a network of membranes and organelles that work together to synthesize, modify, package, and transport lipids and proteins. It's important to remember which organelles are part of it and which are not.

Endoplasmic Reticulum: The Cellular Network

  • Network of tiny tubular structures and flattened sacs (cisternae).
  • Rough ER (RER): Ribosomes on surface, involved in protein synthesis & secretion.
  • Smooth ER (SER): Lacks ribosomes, involved in lipid synthesis & detoxification.

🎙️ The ER is like the cell's internal highway system. RER is studded with ribosomes, making it a site for protein production destined for secretion or insertion into membranes. SER is crucial for making fats and breaking down toxins.

Golgi Apparatus: Packaging & Secretion

  • Discovered by Camillo Golgi.
  • Consists of flattened, disc-shaped sacs or cisternae (0.5-1.0 µm diameter).
  • Modifies, packages, and transports proteins and lipids synthesized in the ER.

🎙️ The Golgi acts like a post office. It receives materials from the ER, processes them, sorts them, and then packages them into vesicles for transport to other cellular destinations or for secretion outside the cell. Glycosylation is a key function here.

Lysosomes & Vacuoles: Digestion & Storage

  • Lysosomes: Single membrane-bound, contain hydrolytic enzymes (acid hydrolases).
  • Lysosomes: 'Suicidal bags' of the cell, break down waste and debris.
  • Vacuoles: Large in plant cells (central vacuole), store water, sap, excretory products.

🎙️ Lysosomes are crucial for cellular cleanup and recycling. Vacuoles, especially the large central vacuole in plants, play roles in maintaining turgor pressure, storage, and waste disposal. Remember their distinct functions.

Mitochondria: The Powerhouse

  • Double membrane-bound organelle, site of aerobic respiration.
  • Inner membrane folded into cristae, increasing surface area.
  • Produces ATP (adenosine triphosphate), the energy currency of the cell.

🎙️ Mitochondria are vital for energy production. The folding of the inner membrane into cristae is a classic example of structure facilitating function, as it provides more surface for the enzymes involved in ATP synthesis. They have their own DNA and ribosomes.

Plastids: Diverse Functions in Plants

  • Found in all plant cells and euglenoids.
  • Chloroplasts: Contain chlorophyll, site of photosynthesis.
  • Chromoplasts: Non-photosynthetic, provide color (e.g., fruits, flowers).
  • Leucoplasts: Colorless, store nutrients (e.g., amyloplasts for starch).

🎙️ Plastids are a diverse group. Chloroplasts are the most famous, enabling plants to make their own food. Chromoplasts give vibrant colors, attracting pollinators and seed dispersers, while leucoplasts are important for storing reserves.

Ribosomes: Protein Factories

  • Non-membrane bound organelles, found in both prokaryotes and eukaryotes.
  • Composed of ribosomal RNA (rRNA) and proteins.
  • Site of protein synthesis (translation).

🎙️ Ribosomes are essential for all life, as proteins carry out most cellular functions. Prokaryotic ribosomes are 70S type (50S + 30S subunits), while eukaryotic ribosomes are 80S type (60S + 40S subunits). This is a common exam question.

The Nucleus: Control Center

  • Double membrane-bound organelle, contains genetic material (DNA).
  • Nuclear envelope has pores for transport between nucleus and cytoplasm.
  • Nucleolus: Site of ribosomal RNA synthesis.

🎙️ The nucleus is the cell's command center, housing the DNA that dictates all cellular activities. The nuclear pores are crucial for regulating what enters and exits, maintaining genetic integrity and enabling gene expression.

Plant vs. Animal Cell: Key Differences

  • Plant cells have cell wall, chloroplasts, and a large central vacuole.
  • Animal cells lack cell wall and chloroplasts, usually have smaller vacuoles.
  • Animal cells have centrioles, which are absent in most plant cells.

🎙️ This comparison is a very common exam question. Be prepared to list and explain at least three to four distinct differences. Understanding these distinctions helps clarify the unique adaptations of plants and animals.

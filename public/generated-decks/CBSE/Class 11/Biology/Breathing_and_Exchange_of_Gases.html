The Fundamental Need: Why Do We Breathe?

  • Obtain Oxygen (O₂): Essential for cellular respiration
  • Remove Carbon Dioxide (CO₂): A toxic metabolic waste
  • Maintain pH balance in the body

🎙️ Breathing isn't just an automatic action; it's vital. We need oxygen for our cells to produce energy, and equally important, we must expel carbon dioxide, a harmful byproduct of metabolism.

Breathing vs. Respiration: A Key Distinction

  • Breathing (Pulmonary Ventilation): Physical process of gas exchange between atmosphere and lungs.
  • Respiration (Cellular Respiration): Biochemical process of energy release within cells using O₂.
  • Breathing is external, Respiration is internal (cellular level).

🎙️ Students often confuse these terms. Breathing is simply the mechanical movement of air. Respiration, however, is the complex chemical process happening inside our cells to generate ATP.

Diverse Respiratory Organs in Animals

  • Lower Invertebrates (e.g., Sponges, Coelenterates): Simple diffusion across body surface.
  • Earthworms: Moist cuticle (cutaneous respiration).
  • Insects: Tracheal system (network of tubes).
  • Fish: Gills (specialised for aquatic environment).

🎙️ Evolution has equipped different organisms with various structures to facilitate gas exchange, depending on their habitat and metabolic needs. Simple organisms use their body surface, while complex ones have specialized organs.

Human Respiratory System: A Functional Pathway

  • Starts from nostrils, leads to pharynx, larynx, trachea, bronchi.
  • Branches into bronchioles, ending in alveoli.
  • Lungs are the primary organs, protected by ribs.
  • Diaphragm and intercostal muscles aid breathing.

🎙️ Our respiratory system is an intricate network designed to efficiently bring air in and out. It's a continuous pathway, ensuring air reaches the deepest parts of our lungs for gas exchange.

Upper Respiratory Tract: Air's Entry Point

  • Nose/Nostrils: Entry, filtration, warming, moistening of air.
  • Pharynx: Common passage for food and air.
  • Larynx (Voice Box): Produces sound, prevents food entry into trachea (epiglottis).

🎙️ The upper tract prepares the inhaled air. Hairs in the nose filter dust, and the mucous membrane warms and moistens it. The epiglottis is a crucial flap preventing choking.

Lower Respiratory Tract: Air's Journey to Lungs

  • Trachea (Windpipe): Extends from larynx to mid-thoracic cavity, supported by C-shaped cartilaginous rings.
  • Bronchi: Trachea divides into primary bronchi (one for each lung).
  • Bronchioles: Primary bronchi branch into secondary, tertiary bronchi, and then fine bronchioles.

🎙️ The C-shaped rings in the trachea are vital; they prevent the windpipe from collapsing. This branching structure ensures air is distributed to all parts of the lungs.

Lungs & Alveoli: The Core of Gas Exchange

  • Lungs: Paired organs in thoracic cavity, covered by pleura (double-layered membrane).
  • Alveoli: Tiny, balloon-like structures at the end of bronchioles, primary site of gas exchange.
  • Each lung has millions of alveoli, providing a vast surface area.

🎙️ The lungs are protected by the pleural membranes, which reduce friction. The alveoli are the true heroes – their immense number and thin walls are perfectly adapted for efficient gas exchange.

Inspiration: Taking Air In (An Active Process)

  • Diaphragm contracts (flattens), external intercostal muscles contract.
  • Ribs and sternum lift up and out, increasing thoracic volume.
  • Intrapulmonary pressure decreases, becoming less than atmospheric pressure.
  • Air rushes into the lungs until pressures equalize.

🎙️ Inspiration is an active process requiring muscle contraction. Think of it like pulling a syringe plunger – increasing volume reduces pressure, drawing air in.

Expiration: Releasing Air Out (Passive/Active)

  • Diaphragm relaxes (domes upward), external intercostal muscles relax.
  • Ribs and sternum return to original position, decreasing thoracic volume.
  • Intrapulmonary pressure increases, becoming greater than atmospheric pressure.
  • Air moves out of the lungs until pressures equalize.

🎙️ Normal expiration is usually passive, relying on the elastic recoil of the lungs. Forced expiration, however, uses additional muscles like internal intercostals and abdominal muscles.

Pressure Gradients: The Driving Force of Breathing

  • Air flows from a region of higher pressure to a region of lower pressure.
  • Inspiration: Atmospheric Pressure > Intrapulmonary Pressure.
  • Expiration: Intrapulmonary Pressure > Atmospheric Pressure.
  • Generated by changes in thoracic volume due to muscle action.

🎙️ Understanding pressure gradients is crucial. It's simply physics in action. Our body manipulates these pressures by changing the volume of the thoracic cavity.

Key Muscles of Respiration

  • Diaphragm: Primary muscle, dome-shaped, separates thoracic and abdominal cavities. Contracts downwards.
  • External Intercostal Muscles: Between ribs, contract to pull ribs upward and outward.
  • Internal Intercostal Muscles: Used during forced expiration, pull ribs inward and downward.
  • Abdominal Muscles: Also aid forced expiration.

🎙️ The diaphragm alone accounts for a significant portion of the air moved during quiet breathing. The intercostal muscles work in coordination to expand and contract the rib cage.

Respiratory Volumes: Measuring Lung Function (Part 1)

  • Tidal Volume (TV): Volume of air inspired or expired during a normal quiet respiration (~500 mL).
  • Inspiratory Reserve Volume (IRV): Additional volume of air a person can inspire by forceful inspiration (~2500-3000 mL).
  • TV + IRV = Maximum air inspired after normal expiration.

🎙️ These volumes help assess lung health. TV is what we breathe without thinking. IRV is the extra effort we can put in to inhale more air.

Respiratory Volumes: Measuring Lung Function (Part 2)

  • Expiratory Reserve Volume (ERV): Additional volume of air a person can expire by forceful expiration (~1000-1100 mL).
  • Residual Volume (RV): Volume of air remaining in the lungs even after a forceful expiration (~1100-1200 mL).
  • RV ensures continuous gas exchange and prevents lung collapse.

🎙️ ERV is the extra air you can push out. RV is the air that always stays in your lungs; it's vital for keeping the alveoli open and allowing continuous gas exchange.

Respiratory Capacities: Combinations of Volumes

  • Inspiratory Capacity (IC) = TV + IRV (Total air inspired after normal expiration).
  • Expiratory Capacity (EC) = TV + ERV (Total air expired after normal inspiration).
  • Vital Capacity (VC) = IRV + ERV + TV (Maximum air exhaled after maximum inhalation).
  • Total Lung Capacity (TLC) = RV + ERV + TV + IRV (Total air lungs can hold after max inspiration).

🎙️ Capacities are derived from volumes and provide a broader picture of lung function. Vital Capacity is a common measure of lung health, especially for athletes.

Worked Example: Calculating Lung Capacities

  • Given: TV = 500 mL, IRV = 3000 mL, ERV = 1100 mL, RV = 1200 mL.
  • Calculate: Vital Capacity (VC) and Total Lung Capacity (TLC).
  • Solution: VC = TV + IRV + ERV = 500 + 3000 + 1100 = 4600 mL.
  • Solution: TLC = VC + RV = 4600 + 1200 = 5800 mL.

🎙️ Practice these calculations. Remember the formulas. It's a direct application of the definitions we just learned and a common exam question.

Gas Exchange at Alveoli: Oxygenating Blood

  • Occurs by simple diffusion, driven by partial pressure gradients.
  • P(O₂) in alveolar air is higher (104 mmHg) than in deoxygenated blood (40 mmHg).
  • P(CO₂) in deoxygenated blood is higher (45 mmHg) than in alveolar air (40 mmHg).
  • O₂ diffuses from alveoli to blood; CO₂ diffuses from blood to alveoli.

🎙️ This is where the magic happens! The large pressure difference for oxygen ensures its rapid movement into the blood, while a smaller but effective gradient moves CO2 out.

Gas Exchange at Tissues: Supplying Cells

  • Occurs by simple diffusion, driven by partial pressure gradients.
  • P(O₂) in oxygenated blood is higher (95 mmHg) than in tissue cells (40 mmHg).
  • P(CO₂) in tissue cells is higher (45 mmHg) than in oxygenated blood (40 mmHg).
  • O₂ diffuses from blood to tissues; CO₂ diffuses from tissues to blood.

🎙️ At the tissue level, the gradients are reversed. Blood delivers oxygen and picks up the metabolic waste, carbon dioxide, from the working cells.

Factors Affecting Rate of Gas Exchange

  • Partial pressure gradient: Higher gradient = faster diffusion.
  • Solubility of gases: CO₂ is 20-25 times more soluble than O₂.
  • Thickness of diffusion membrane: Thinner membrane = faster diffusion (alveolar membrane is very thin).
  • Surface area: Larger surface area (alveoli) = faster diffusion.

🎙️ These factors are critical for maintaining efficient gas exchange. Any compromise to these, like thickening of the membrane in certain diseases, can severely impact respiration.

Oxygen Transport: The Role of Haemoglobin

  • About 97% of O₂ is transported by Red Blood Cells (RBCs) as oxyhaemoglobin.
  • Haemoglobin (Hb): Iron-containing pigment, reversibly binds O₂.
  • Each Hb molecule can bind up to four O₂ molecules.
  • Remaining 3% of O₂ is dissolved in plasma.

🎙️ Haemoglobin is an amazing molecule, perfectly adapted to pick up oxygen where it's abundant and release it where it's needed. This reversible binding is key.

Oxygen Dissociation Curve: Understanding O₂ Release

  • Sigmoid (S-shaped) curve showing the relationship between P(O₂) and percentage saturation of Hb with O₂.
  • Right Shift (Bohr Effect): Lower pH, higher P(CO₂), higher temperature decrease Hb affinity for O₂ (favors O₂ release at tissues).
  • Left Shift: Higher pH, lower P(CO₂), lower temperature increase Hb affinity for O₂ (favors O₂ uptake at lungs).

🎙️ This curve is crucial for understanding how oxygen is loaded in the lungs and unloaded at the tissues. The Bohr effect is a prime example of physiological adaptation.

Carbon Dioxide Transport: Removing Waste

  • About 70% transported as bicarbonate ions (HCO₃⁻) in plasma.
  • About 20-25% transported by Hb as carbamino-haemoglobin.
  • About 7% transported dissolved in plasma.
  • Carbonic anhydrase enzyme in RBCs facilitates CO₂ conversion to bicarbonate.

🎙️ CO₂ is transported in multiple forms, with bicarbonate being the most significant. This mechanism also plays a vital role in maintaining the body's pH balance.

Regulation of Respiration: Keeping it Balanced

  • Neural Control: Medulla oblongata (respiratory rhythm center) and Pons (pneumotaxic center).
  • Chemoreceptors: Sensitive to P(CO₂) and H⁺ concentration (acidity), primarily in carotid and aortic bodies.
  • Increase in P(CO₂) or H⁺ is the most potent stimulus for increasing respiratory rate.
  • Oxygen's role is relatively minor in normal regulation.

🎙️ Our breathing is tightly regulated by the brain, primarily by monitoring CO₂ and H⁺ levels, which are direct indicators of metabolic activity. Oxygen levels usually don't fluctuate enough to be the primary driver.

Common Respiratory Disorders

  • Asthma: Inflammation and constriction of bronchi/bronchioles, leading to difficulty breathing (wheezing).
  • Emphysema: Damage to alveolar walls, reducing surface area for gas exchange, often due to smoking.
  • Occupational Respiratory Disorders: Caused by long-term exposure to dust (e.g., silicosis, asbestosis).
  • Pneumonia: Infection causing inflammation of alveoli, filling them with fluid.

🎙️ Awareness of these disorders is important. Many are preventable, especially those linked to lifestyle choices like smoking or occupational hazards. Always protect your lungs!

Quick Recap & Common Mistakes

  • Breathing is physical, Respiration is cellular.
  • Pressure gradients drive air movement.
  • Haemoglobin transports O₂, CO₂ transported mainly as bicarbonate.
  • Regulation is primarily by CO₂/H⁺ levels, not O₂.
  • Don't confuse volumes and capacities; know their formulas.

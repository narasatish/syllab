Why Do We Breathe? The Energy Connection

  • To obtain Oxygen (O2).
  • To release Carbon Dioxide (CO2).
  • For cellular energy production (ATP).

🎙️ Breathing isn't just an automatic action; it's fundamental. We breathe to supply our cells with oxygen, which is essential for breaking down food and generating energy in the form of ATP. Without oxygen, our cells cannot function effectively.

Breathing vs. Respiration: The Key Difference

  • Breathing: Physical process of gas exchange (lungs).
  • Respiration: Biochemical process of energy release (cells).
  • Breathing is part of Respiration.

🎙️ Often confused, breathing and respiration are distinct. Breathing is the mechanical act of inhaling and exhaling, while cellular respiration is the complex metabolic process occurring inside cells to produce energy. Breathing facilitates respiration.

Conducting Pathway: Upper Respiratory Tract

Conducting Pathway: Lower Respiratory Tract

  • Trachea (Windpipe): Extends to mid-thoracic cavity.
  • Primary Bronchi: Trachea divides into left and right.
  • Bronchioles: Further divisions, very fine tubes.
  • Alveoli: Thin-walled air sacs, main exchange site.

🎙️ The trachea, supported by cartilaginous rings, prevents collapse. It branches into bronchi, which further divide into increasingly smaller bronchioles, eventually leading to the alveoli. These tiny sacs are where the magic of gas exchange truly happens.

Alveoli: The Ultimate Exchange Units

  • Millions of thin-walled, balloon-like structures.
  • Richly supplied with blood capillaries.
  • Total surface area for exchange is vast (~100m²).

🎙️ The alveoli are the primary sites of gas exchange. Their incredibly thin walls, combined with a dense network of capillaries, create an enormous surface area. This design is critical for rapid and efficient diffusion of oxygen into the blood and carbon dioxide out of it.

Mechanism of Breathing: Inspiration (Inhaling)

  • Diaphragm contracts (flattens).
  • External intercostal muscles contract (ribs lift).
  • Thoracic cavity volume increases.
  • Intra-pulmonary pressure decreases below atmospheric pressure.

🎙️ Inspiration is an active process. The diaphragm and external intercostal muscles are key players. Their contraction increases the volume of the thoracic cavity, leading to a drop in pressure inside the lungs, which then draws air in from the outside.

Mechanism of Breathing: Expiration (Exhaling)

  • Diaphragm relaxes (domes up).
  • External intercostal muscles relax (ribs lower).
  • Thoracic cavity volume decreases.
  • Intra-pulmonary pressure increases above atmospheric pressure.

🎙️ Expiration is generally a passive process, relying on the elastic recoil of the lungs and relaxation of the inspiratory muscles. This reduces the thoracic volume, increasing intra-pulmonary pressure and forcing air out. During strenuous activity, internal intercostal muscles assist.

Respiratory Volumes: Measuring Air in Lungs

  • Tidal Volume (TV): Air in/out during normal breath.
  • Inspiratory Reserve Volume (IRV): Extra air inhaled forcefully.
  • Expiratory Reserve Volume (ERV): Extra air exhaled forcefully.
  • Residual Volume (RV): Air remaining after forceful exhalation.

🎙️ To assess lung function, we measure various air volumes. Tidal volume is what we normally breathe. IRV and ERV represent our capacity for deeper breaths, while residual volume is crucial for keeping alveoli open and preventing lung collapse.

Respiratory Capacities: Combining Volumes for Assessment

  • Inspiratory Capacity (IC) = TV + IRV.
  • Expiratory Capacity (EC) = TV + ERV.
  • Functional Residual Capacity (FRC) = ERV + RV.
  • Vital Capacity (VC) = IRV + ERV + TV.
  • Total Lung Capacity (TLC) = VC + RV.

🎙️ Capacities are combinations of two or more volumes. They provide a more comprehensive picture of lung function. For instance, Vital Capacity indicates the maximum amount of air a person can exhale after a maximal inhalation, which is a key clinical indicator.

Gas Exchange: From Alveoli to Blood

  • O2 moves from alveoli to blood (high PO2 to low PO2).
  • CO2 moves from blood to alveoli (high PCO2 to low PCO2).
  • Diffusion driven by partial pressure gradients.

🎙️ The exchange of gases across the alveolar membrane is a passive process, driven solely by differences in partial pressures. Oxygen, being at a higher partial pressure in the alveoli, diffuses into the capillary blood, while carbon dioxide moves in the opposite direction.

Gas Exchange: From Blood to Tissues

  • O2 moves from blood to tissues (high PO2 to low PO2).
  • CO2 moves from tissues to blood (high PCO2 to low PCO2).
  • Tissues are constantly consuming O2 and producing CO2.

🎙️ Once oxygen-rich blood reaches the body tissues, the partial pressure gradient reverses. Oxygen diffuses from the blood into the cells, where it's used for metabolism, and the carbon dioxide produced by cells diffuses into the blood to be transported back to the lungs.

Factors Affecting the Rate of Gas Diffusion

  • Partial pressure gradient: The steeper, the faster.
  • Solubility of gases: CO2 is 20-25x more soluble than O2.
  • Thickness of membrane: Thinner, faster diffusion.
  • Surface area: Larger surface, faster diffusion.

🎙️ Several factors influence the efficiency of gas exchange. The partial pressure difference is the primary driver. The solubility of gases and the characteristics of the diffusion membrane, like its thickness and surface area, are also critical for optimal gas transfer.

Oxygen Transport: Haemoglobin's Crucial Role

  • 97% of O2 transported by Haemoglobin in RBCs.
  • 3% transported in dissolved state in plasma.
  • Haemoglobin binds reversibly with O2 to form Oxyhaemoglobin.

🎙️ Haemoglobin, the red pigment in our red blood cells, is the primary transporter of oxygen. Each haemoglobin molecule can bind up to four oxygen molecules, forming oxyhaemoglobin. This reversible binding is crucial for both loading oxygen in the lungs and unloading it in the tissues.

Carbon Dioxide Transport: Multiple Mechanisms

  • 70% as Bicarbonate ions (HCO3-) in plasma.
  • 20-25% as Carbamino-haemoglobin in RBCs.
  • 7% in dissolved state in plasma.

🎙️ Carbon dioxide is transported in the blood mainly in three forms. The majority is carried as bicarbonate ions, a process facilitated by the enzyme carbonic anhydrase. A significant portion also binds to haemoglobin, and a small amount remains dissolved in the plasma.

Regulation of Respiration: Neural Control

  • Medulla Oblongata: Respiratory Rhythm Centre (primary).
  • Pons: Pneumotaxic Centre (modifies rhythm).
  • Chemoreceptors: Sensitive to CO2 and H+ concentration.
  • Oxygen plays minor role in normal regulation.

🎙️ Breathing is an involuntary process, tightly regulated by the brain. The medulla oblongata sets the basic rhythm, while the pons fine-tunes it. Chemoreceptors detect changes in blood CO2 and H+ levels, providing feedback to adjust breathing rate and depth, ensuring homeostasis.

Common Disorders of the Respiratory System

  • Asthma: Inflammation of bronchi/bronchioles, difficulty breathing.
  • Emphysema: Damage to alveolar walls, decreased surface area.
  • Occupational Respiratory Disorders: Due to dust exposure (e.g., silicosis).

🎙️ Various conditions can impair the respiratory system. Asthma involves constricted airways, while emphysema permanently damages the delicate alveoli, reducing gas exchange efficiency. Occupational hazards highlight the importance of protective measures in dusty environments.

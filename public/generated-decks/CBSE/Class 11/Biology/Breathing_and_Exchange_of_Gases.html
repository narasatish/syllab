Breathing vs. Respiration: A Key Distinction

  • Breathing: Mechanical process of inhaling and exhaling air.
  • Respiration: Biochemical process of breaking down food to release energy.

🎙️ This is a common point of confusion. Breathing is simply the physical movement of air. Respiration, on the other hand, is the cellular process where glucose is oxidized in the presence of oxygen to produce ATP, our energy currency. Breathing facilitates respiration.

Mechanism of Breathing: Pressure Matters

  • Breathing is driven by pressure gradients.
  • Air moves from a region of higher pressure to lower pressure.
  • Diaphragm and intercostal muscles change thoracic volume and pressure.

🎙️ Our body doesn't just pull or push air randomly. Breathing is a precise, mechanical process governed by pressure differences. Air, like any gas, always flows from an area of high pressure to an area of low pressure. Our muscles create these pressure changes.

⚙️
Pressure-Driven Process

Inspiration: Taking Air In

  • Diaphragm contracts (flattens), external intercostals contract (ribs lift).
  • Thoracic cavity volume increases.
  • Intrapulmonary pressure decreases (becomes less than atmospheric pressure).
  • Air rushes into the lungs.

🎙️ When we inhale, our diaphragm flattens, and rib cage lifts. This increases the volume inside our chest cavity. According to Boyle's Law, increased volume means decreased pressure. Since the pressure inside our lungs is now lower than outside, air flows in.

2Volume Up

Expiration: Pushing Air Out

  • Diaphragm relaxes (domes up), external intercostals relax (ribs lower).
  • Thoracic cavity volume decreases.
  • Intrapulmonary pressure increases (becomes greater than atmospheric pressure).
  • Air moves out of the lungs.

🎙️ Expiration is largely a passive process during normal breathing. The diaphragm and intercostal muscles relax, reducing the volume of the thoracic cavity. This increases the intrapulmonary pressure above atmospheric pressure, forcing air out of the lungs.

Key Volumes: Tidal Volume and IRV

  • Tidal Volume (TV): Volume of air inhaled or exhaled during a normal quiet breath (approx. 500 mL).
  • Inspiratory Reserve Volume (IRV): Additional volume of air that can be inhaled after a normal inspiration (approx. 2500-3000 mL).

🎙️ Tidal volume is the air we move effortlessly with each breath. IRV is the extra air you can force in if you try, like taking a deep sniff. Together, they represent the dynamic capacity of our lungs to bring in oxygen.

Key Volumes: ERV and Residual Volume

  • Expiratory Reserve Volume (ERV): Additional volume of air that can be exhaled after a normal expiration (approx. 1000-1100 mL).
  • Residual Volume (RV): Volume of air remaining in the lungs even after a forceful expiration (approx. 1100-1200 mL).

🎙️ ERV is the extra air you can force out after a normal exhale. Residual volume is the air that always stays in your lungs, preventing them from collapsing and allowing continuous gas exchange even between breaths.

Lung Capacities: Combining Volumes

  • Vital Capacity (VC): Max air a person can breathe in after a forced expiration (TV + IRV + ERV).
  • Total Lung Capacity (TLC): Total volume of air lungs can hold after a max inspiration (VC + RV).

🎙️ These capacities combine the individual volumes to give a broader picture of lung function. Vital capacity measures the maximum movable air, while total lung capacity represents the absolute maximum amount of air the lungs can contain.

Exchange of Gases: The Diffusion Principle

  • Gases move by simple diffusion.
  • Driven by partial pressure gradient.
  • Solubility of gases and thickness of membrane also affect diffusion rate.

🎙️ The exchange of oxygen and carbon dioxide across membranes is a simple physical process: diffusion. Gases move from an area where their partial pressure is higher to where it is lower. The efficiency also depends on how soluble the gas is and the thickness of the membrane.

Gas Exchange at Alveoli: Oxygen In, CO2 Out

  • Partial pressure of O2 (pO2) is higher in alveolar air than in deoxygenated blood.
  • pO2 gradient: Alveoli (104 mmHg) > Blood (40 mmHg).
  • Partial pressure of CO2 (pCO2) is higher in deoxygenated blood than in alveolar air.
  • pCO2 gradient: Blood (45 mmHg) > Alveoli (40 mmHg).

🎙️ At the alveoli, oxygen from the inhaled air, which has a higher partial pressure, diffuses into the blood. Simultaneously, carbon dioxide, which has a higher partial pressure in the deoxygenated blood, diffuses out into the alveoli to be exhaled.

Gas Exchange at Tissues: Oxygen Out, CO2 In

  • Partial pressure of O2 (pO2) is higher in oxygenated blood than in tissue cells.
  • pO2 gradient: Blood (95 mmHg) > Tissues (40 mmHg).
  • Partial pressure of CO2 (pCO2) is higher in tissue cells than in oxygenated blood.
  • pCO2 gradient: Tissues (45 mmHg) > Blood (40 mmHg).

🎙️ The reverse happens at the body tissues. Oxygenated blood arrives with high pO2, which then diffuses into the metabolically active tissue cells where pO2 is low. Carbon dioxide, a waste product with high pCO2 in tissues, diffuses into the blood to be carried away.

Transport of Oxygen: The Hemoglobin Highway

  • 97% of O2 transported by red blood cells (RBCs) via hemoglobin.
  • Hemoglobin (Hb) is an iron-containing pigment.
  • Forms oxyhemoglobin (HbO2), a reversible binding.
  • Binding influenced by pO2, pCO2, H+ concentration, temperature.

🎙️ Oxygen is primarily transported by hemoglobin, a remarkable protein in our red blood cells. Hemoglobin binds reversibly with oxygen to form oxyhemoglobin. The efficiency of this binding is influenced by several factors, ensuring oxygen is released where it's most needed.

Transport of Carbon Dioxide: Multiple Routes

  • ~70% as bicarbonate ions (HCO3-) in plasma.
  • ~20-25% as carbaminohemoglobin (bound to Hb).
  • ~7% dissolved in plasma.

🎙️ Carbon dioxide, a waste product, is transported back to the lungs in three main forms. The majority is carried as bicarbonate ions, a crucial buffering system. A significant portion also binds to hemoglobin, forming carbaminohemoglobin, and a small amount dissolves directly in plasma.

Regulation of Respiration: Brain's Control Center

  • Respiratory rhythm center in medulla oblongata primarily regulates breathing.
  • Pneumotaxic center in pons can moderate medullary function.
  • Chemosensitive area near rhythm center senses CO2 and H+ levels.
  • Oxygen's role in regulation is relatively minor.

🎙️ Breathing is mostly an involuntary process, tightly controlled by our brain. The medulla oblongata houses the primary respiratory control center. It constantly monitors blood CO2 and H+ levels, which are the main stimuli for regulating breathing rate and depth, not oxygen.

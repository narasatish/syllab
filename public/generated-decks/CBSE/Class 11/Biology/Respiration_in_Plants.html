What is Respiration?

  • Process of breaking down complex organic molecules
  • Release of energy in the form of ATP
  • Occurs in all living cells, day and night

🎙️ Respiration isn't just breathing; it's the cellular process where plants break down glucose or other organic compounds. This breakdown releases energy, primarily stored as ATP, which fuels all their metabolic activities.

Why do Plants Respire?

  • Energy for growth and development
  • Nutrient absorption and transport
  • Synthesis of organic compounds

🎙️ Plants need energy for everything – from growing new leaves and roots to absorbing minerals from the soil and transporting water. Respiration provides this essential energy currency, ATP, for all these vital processes.

  • Aerobic Respiration: Requires oxygen
  • Anaerobic Respiration: Occurs without oxygen

🎙️ There are two main types of respiration. Aerobic respiration is highly efficient and requires oxygen, while anaerobic respiration occurs in the absence of oxygen and yields much less energy.

Aerobic Respiration: Overview of Stages

  • Complete oxidation of organic substances
  • Produces CO2, water, and large amount of energy
  • Occurs in four main stages

🎙️ Aerobic respiration is a complex process with several interconnected stages. It starts in the cytoplasm with glycolysis and then moves into the mitochondria for the subsequent steps, leading to maximum energy release.

Glycolysis: The First Step

  • Universal pathway, occurs in cytoplasm
  • Breaks down glucose (6C) into two pyruvate (3C) molecules
  • Does not require oxygen

🎙️ Glycolysis is the initial pathway for both aerobic and anaerobic respiration. It literally means 'splitting sugar'. This pathway takes a 6-carbon glucose molecule and breaks it down into two 3-carbon pyruvate molecules in the cytoplasm.

Glycolysis: Key Reactions

  • Glucose phosphorylated to Glucose-6-phosphate
  • Fructose-1,6-bisphosphate cleaved into two 3C compounds
  • Oxidative phosphorylation produces ATP and NADH

🎙️ Glycolysis involves a series of 10 enzyme-catalyzed reactions. Key steps include initial phosphorylation using ATP, rearrangement, cleavage, and finally, oxidation and ATP formation through substrate-level phosphorylation.

2F-6-P → F-1,6-BP

Glycolysis: Net Energy Yield

  • Gross ATP produced: 4 ATP
  • ATP consumed: 2 ATP
  • Net ATP gain: 2 ATP (via substrate-level phosphorylation)
  • NADH produced: 2 NADH

🎙️ While 4 ATP molecules are produced during glycolysis, 2 ATP are consumed in the initial steps, leading to a net gain of 2 ATP. Importantly, 2 molecules of NADH are also generated, which will contribute to ATP later in the ETS.

2 Net ATP, 2 NADH

Fate of Pyruvic Acid

  • Depends on the presence or absence of oxygen
  • Aerobic conditions: enters mitochondria
  • Anaerobic conditions: undergoes fermentation

🎙️ The fate of the pyruvate molecules produced from glycolysis is critical. If oxygen is available, they move into the mitochondria for further oxidation. If not, they remain in the cytoplasm and undergo fermentation.

Pyruvate Oxidation: The Link Reaction

  • Pyruvate enters mitochondrial matrix
  • Oxidized to Acetyl CoA and CO2
  • Produces 1 NADH per pyruvate (2 NADH per glucose)

🎙️ Before entering the Krebs cycle, pyruvate undergoes a crucial 'link reaction'. This involves its oxidative decarboxylation, forming Acetyl CoA, which is the entry molecule for the Krebs cycle, and releasing CO2 and NADH.

Krebs Cycle: Central Metabolic Hub

  • Occurs in the mitochondrial matrix
  • Acetyl CoA combines with Oxaloacetic Acid (OAA)
  • Cyclic pathway, regenerating OAA

🎙️ Also known as the Citric Acid Cycle or TCA cycle, the Krebs cycle is a central metabolic pathway. Acetyl CoA enters this cycle, where it is completely oxidized, releasing CO2 and generating electron carriers.

🔄
The Metabolic Cycle

Krebs Cycle: Key Reactions

  • Acetyl CoA (2C) + OAA (4C) → Citric Acid (6C)
  • Sequential decarboxylations release CO2
  • Oxidations generate NADH and FADH2

🎙️ The cycle involves several oxidation-reduction reactions. For each Acetyl CoA, two molecules of CO2 are released, and multiple molecules of NADH and FADH2 are produced, which are vital for the next stage.

2Citrate Formation
3CO2 Release (x2)

Krebs Cycle: Energy Output

  • Per Acetyl CoA: 3 NADH, 1 FADH2, 1 ATP (or GTP)
  • Per Glucose (2 Acetyl CoA): 6 NADH, 2 FADH2, 2 ATP
  • ATP via substrate-level phosphorylation

🎙️ The primary energy output of the Krebs cycle isn't direct ATP, but rather the high-energy electron carriers, NADH and FADH2. These molecules carry electrons to the Electron Transport System, where the bulk of ATP is generated.

Electron Transport System (ETS): Introduction

  • Located in the inner mitochondrial membrane
  • Series of electron carriers (Complexes I-IV)
  • Transfers electrons from NADH and FADH2 to oxygen

🎙️ The ETS is where the energy stored in NADH and FADH2 is finally harnessed. It's a chain of protein complexes embedded in the inner mitochondrial membrane, acting like a relay race for electrons.

💡
Generating Power

ETS: Electron Flow & Proton Gradient

  • Electrons move from higher to lower energy levels
  • Energy released is used to pump protons (H+) into intermembrane space
  • Creates a proton gradient across the membrane

🎙️ As electrons pass through the complexes, energy is released. This energy is used to actively pump protons from the mitochondrial matrix into the intermembrane space, establishing an electrochemical proton gradient.

1NADH/FADH2 donate e-
2e- move through complexes
3Protons pumped out
4O2 is final e- acceptor

Oxidative Phosphorylation: ATP Synthesis

  • Protons flow back into matrix through ATP synthase (Complex V)
  • Energy from proton flow drives ATP synthesis from ADP and Pi
  • Chemiosmotic hypothesis explains this mechanism

🎙️ The proton gradient represents potential energy. Protons flow back into the matrix through a special enzyme called ATP synthase. This flow drives the synthesis of ATP, a process known as chemiosmosis or oxidative phosphorylation.

Overall ATP Yield (Aerobic Respiration)

  • Glycolysis: Net 2 ATP + 2 NADH (approx. 6 ATP)
  • Pyruvate Oxidation: 2 NADH (approx. 6 ATP)
  • Krebs Cycle: 2 ATP + 6 NADH (approx. 18 ATP) + 2 FADH2 (approx. 4 ATP)
  • Total: ~30-32 ATP per glucose molecule

🎙️ Calculating the exact ATP yield can be tricky due to shuttle systems, but typically, complete aerobic oxidation of one glucose molecule yields about 30-32 ATP. This highlights the high efficiency of aerobic respiration.

Anaerobic Respiration: Fermentation

  • Occurs without oxygen, primarily in cytoplasm
  • Incomplete breakdown of glucose
  • Main purpose: regenerate NAD+ for glycolysis

🎙️ When oxygen is absent, cells resort to anaerobic respiration, or fermentation. This process is less efficient, producing much less ATP, but it's crucial for regenerating NAD+ so glycolysis can continue.

Alcoholic Fermentation

  • Pyruvic acid converted to ethanol and CO2
  • NAD+ regenerated from NADH
  • Occurs in yeast and some plant tissues under anaerobic conditions

🎙️ In alcoholic fermentation, pyruvate is first decarboxylated to acetaldehyde, then reduced to ethanol. This process, common in yeast, is used in brewing and baking, and also occurs in waterlogged plant roots.

Lactic Acid Fermentation

  • Pyruvic acid directly reduced to lactic acid
  • NAD+ regenerated from NADH
  • Occurs in some bacteria, animal muscle cells, and some plants

🎙️ Lactic acid fermentation is a simpler, one-step process where pyruvate is directly converted to lactic acid. This happens in our muscles during strenuous exercise and in certain plant tissues under oxygen deprivation.

Respiration Quotient (RQ)

  • Ratio of CO2 evolved to O2 consumed during respiration
  • RQ = Volume of CO2 evolved / Volume of O2 consumed
  • Used to determine the type of respiratory substrate being oxidized

🎙️ The Respiration Quotient, or RQ, is an important concept. By measuring the gases exchanged, we can infer what type of organic molecule a plant is primarily breaking down for energy.

➗
Measuring Substrate Use
22 / 25

RQ: Examples for Substrates

  • Carbohydrates: RQ = 1 (e.g., C6H12O6 + 6O2 -> 6CO2 + 6H2O)
  • Fats: RQ < 1 (e.g., Tripalmitin: ~0.7)
  • Proteins: RQ < 1 (approx. 0.8-0.9)
  • Organic acids: RQ > 1

🎙️ Different respiratory substrates have characteristic RQ values. Carbohydrates yield an RQ of 1 because the O2 consumed equals the CO2 released. Fats and proteins have lower RQs as they require more oxygen for complete oxidation.

Factors Affecting Respiration

  • Temperature: Optimal range (20-30°C), increases with temperature up to a point
  • Oxygen Concentration: Essential for aerobic respiration, low O2 inhibits it
  • CO2 Concentration: High CO2 can inhibit respiration

🎙️ Several environmental factors can influence the rate of respiration in plants. Temperature, oxygen availability, and even carbon dioxide levels play significant roles in regulating this vital process.

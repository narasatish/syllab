Chemical Composition: Organic vs. Inorganic

  • Living tissue has both organic (carbon-based) and inorganic compounds.
  • Inorganic components: Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, PO₄³⁻, SO₄²⁻.
  • Organic components: Carbohydrates, Proteins, Lipids, Nucleic Acids.

🎙️ Our bodies are a complex blend of chemicals. While we often focus on the big organic molecules, don't forget the vital role of inorganic ions like sodium, potassium, and calcium. These maintain osmotic balance, nerve function, and bone structure, among other things.

Acid-soluble vs. Acid-insoluble Pool

  • Grinding tissue with trichloroacetic acid (TCA).
  • Filtration separates into two fractions.
  • Acid-soluble: Micromolecules (MW < 1000 Da).
  • Acid-insoluble: Macromolecules (MW > 10000 Da).

🎙️ This experiment is key to classifying biomolecules by size. The acid-soluble fraction contains smaller molecules like amino acids and simple sugars, while the acid-insoluble fraction holds the larger polymers like proteins and nucleic acids. This distinction helps us understand their differing roles.

Micromolecules: Building Blocks

  • Small molecular weight compounds (18 to 800 Daltons).
  • Include amino acids, monosaccharides, nucleotides, fatty acids.
  • Precursors for synthesizing macromolecules.

🎙️ Micromolecules are the fundamental units from which all larger biomolecules are constructed. Think of them as the individual bricks that build a house. Understanding their basic structure is crucial for grasping the complexity of the macromolecules they form.

Amino Acids: Proteins' Monomers

  • Organic compounds with an amino group (-NH₂), carboxyl group (-COOH), hydrogen atom, and a variable 'R' group.
  • All attached to a central alpha-carbon atom.
  • Over 20 types, differing by their 'R' group.

🎙️ Amino acids are the monomers of proteins. Their unique characteristic is the 'R' group, which can vary greatly, giving each amino acid its distinct chemical properties. This variation is what allows proteins to have such diverse structures and functions.

Amino Acids: Types and Classification

  • Based on 'R' group: acidic (e.g., Glutamic acid), basic (e.g., Lysine), neutral (e.g., Valine).
  • Essential amino acids: must be obtained from diet (e.g., Leucine, Isoleucine).
  • Non-essential amino acids: synthesized by the body.

🎙️ The classification of amino acids is important. Knowing which ones are essential helps in understanding nutritional requirements. The 'R' group determines if an amino acid is acidic, basic, or neutral, which in turn influences the overall charge and folding of a protein.

Proteins: Polymers of Amino Acids

  • Long chains of amino acids linked by peptide bonds.
  • Peptide bond formed by dehydration synthesis between -COOH and -NH₂ groups.
  • Exhibit primary, secondary, tertiary, and quaternary structures.

🎙️ Proteins are incredibly versatile macromolecules. The peptide bond is the covalent link that holds amino acids together, forming a polypeptide chain. The specific sequence of amino acids, and how this chain folds, determines the protein's final 3D structure and function.

Worked Example: Identifying Amino Acid Type

  • Question: An amino acid has an R-group: -CH₂-CH₂-COOH. Classify it.
  • Solution: The R-group contains an extra carboxyl group (-COOH).
  • This makes the amino acid acidic.
  • Example: Glutamic acid.

🎙️ When classifying amino acids, always look at the 'R' group. If it has an extra carboxyl group, it's acidic. If it has an extra amino group, it's basic. If it's mostly hydrocarbon, it's neutral. This is a common exam question, so practice identifying them.

Common Mistake: Essential vs. Non-essential

  • Mistake: Confusing essential amino acids with 'more important' ones.
  • Correction: 'Essential' means the body cannot synthesize them.
  • Both types are equally vital for protein synthesis and health.

🎙️ Carbohydrates are broadly known as sugars. They are our body's preferred fuel source. Beyond energy, they also play crucial structural roles, particularly in plants forming cell walls, and in insects as chitin.

Monosaccharides: Simple Sugars

  • Simplest form of carbohydrates, cannot be hydrolyzed further.
  • Examples: Glucose (blood sugar), Fructose (fruit sugar), Galactose.
  • Typically 3-7 carbon atoms, forming ring structures in aqueous solutions.

🎙️ Monosaccharides are the basic building blocks of all other carbohydrates. Glucose is particularly important as the main energy source for cellular respiration. Remember their common examples and their general ring structure.

Disaccharides & Polysaccharides

  • Disaccharides: Two monosaccharides linked (e.g., Sucrose = Glucose + Fructose).
  • Polysaccharides: Long chains of many monosaccharides.
  • Storage: Starch (plants), Glycogen (animals).
  • Structural: Cellulose (plant cell walls).

🎙️ Disaccharides are formed by linking two simple sugars, like sucrose in table sugar. Polysaccharides are much larger polymers, serving roles in energy storage (starch, glycogen) and structural support (cellulose). Understand the key differences and examples.

Lipids: Diverse and Hydrophobic

  • Group of compounds insoluble in water but soluble in organic solvents.
  • Includes fatty acids, glycerol, triglycerides, phospholipids, steroids.
  • Characterized by long hydrocarbon chains.

🎙️ Lipids are a very diverse group, but their defining characteristic is their hydrophobicity – they don't mix with water. This property is crucial for their roles in cell membranes and energy storage. They are essential for many biological functions.

Lipids: Structure and Examples

  • Fatty acids: Carboxyl group attached to a hydrocarbon chain (saturated/unsaturated).
  • Triglycerides: Glycerol molecule esterified with three fatty acids (fats/oils).
  • Phospholipids: Two fatty acids + phosphate group + glycerol (cell membranes).

🎙️ Understanding the basic structure of different lipids is vital. Fatty acids are the building blocks. Triglycerides are for energy storage, while phospholipids are the primary components of cell membranes, forming a bilayer due to their amphipathic nature.

Lipids: Crucial Functions

  • Long-term energy storage (more efficient than carbohydrates).
  • Major component of cell membranes (phospholipids).
  • Insulation (thermal) and protection (cushioning organs).
  • Hormones (steroids like cholesterol, testosterone).

🎙️ Lipids offer a concentrated form of energy storage, insulation against cold, and protection for vital organs. Their role in forming cell membranes is fundamental to cell structure, and steroid hormones derived from lipids regulate many body processes.

Nucleotides: The Building Blocks

  • Each nucleotide has three components: a nitrogenous base, a pentose sugar, and a phosphate group.
  • Bases: Purines (Adenine, Guanine) and Pyrimidines (Cytosine, Thymine, Uracil).
  • Sugar: Deoxyribose in DNA, Ribose in RNA.

🎙️ The structure of a nucleotide is fundamental. Remember the three parts: a base (A, T, C, G, U), a five-carbon sugar (deoxyribose or ribose), and a phosphate group. These link together to form the long chains of DNA and RNA.

Quick Question 1: Macromolecule Identification

  • Which of the following is NOT a macromolecule?
  • A) Protein
  • B) Polysaccharide
  • C) Lipid
  • D) Nucleic Acid

🎙️ Take a moment to recall the definition of macromolecules and their common examples. Remember the distinction made by the acid-soluble and acid-insoluble pools. The answer is C) Lipid, as individual lipids are generally micromolecules, though they can aggregate to form larger structures.

Quick Question 2: Protein Bonds

  • What type of bond links amino acids together to form proteins?
  • A) Glycosidic bond
  • B) Ester bond
  • C) Peptide bond
  • D) Phosphodiester bond

🎙️ Think back to the formation of proteins from amino acids. Each type of biomolecule polymer has a specific bond linking its monomers. The correct answer is C) Peptide bond. Glycosidic bonds are in carbohydrates, ester bonds in lipids, and phosphodiester bonds in nucleic acids.

Quick Question 3: Storage Polysaccharide

  • Which of the following is a storage polysaccharide found in animals?
  • A) Starch
  • B) Cellulose
  • C) Glycogen
  • D) Chitin

🎙️ Recall the different types of polysaccharides and their roles in plants versus animals. Starch is plant storage, cellulose is plant structure, and chitin is found in fungal cell walls and exoskeletons. The correct answer is C) Glycogen.

Enzymes: Biological Catalysts

  • Mostly proteins, function as biocatalysts.
  • Increase the rate of biochemical reactions without being consumed.
  • Highly specific for their substrates (Lock and Key model).
  • Activity is affected by temperature and pH.

🎙️ Enzymes are vital for virtually all biological processes. They act as molecular machines, dramatically increasing reaction rates. Their specificity is key, meaning each enzyme typically acts on only one or a few specific molecules, much like a lock and key.

Recap: Key Biomolecules and Their Roles

  • Proteins: Structure, enzymes, transport (made of amino acids).
  • Carbohydrates: Energy, structure (made of monosaccharides).
  • Lipids: Energy storage, membranes, hormones (fatty acids, glycerol).
  • Nucleic Acids: Genetic information (DNA, RNA, made of nucleotides).

🎙️ In summary, we've covered the four major classes of biomolecules: proteins, carbohydrates, lipids, and nucleic acids. Remember their basic building blocks, key examples, and primary functions.

What are Biomolecules?

  • Organic compounds found in living organisms.
  • Form the building blocks of cells and tissues.
  • Crucial for metabolism, growth, and reproduction.

🎙️ Biomolecules are essentially the organic molecules that are produced by living organisms. They are vital for all the processes that occur within us, from building our cells to enabling complex chemical reactions. Think of them as the fundamental components that make life possible.

Chemical Analysis of Tissues

  • Grind tissue with trichloroacetic acid (TCA).
  • Filter to separate acid-soluble and acid-insoluble pools.
  • Further analysis identifies specific biomolecules.

🎙️ To understand what our bodies are made of, scientists perform chemical analysis. A common method involves grinding tissue, say a piece of liver, with trichloroacetic acid. This process helps us separate the components into two main categories based on their solubility.

Micromolecules vs. Macromolecules

  • Acid-soluble pool: Small molecular weight (<1000 Da).
  • Acid-insoluble pool: Large molecular weight (>1000 Da).
  • Key distinction for classifying biomolecules.

🎙️ The acid-soluble pool contains what we call micromolecules, which are generally smaller in size and molecular weight. The acid-insoluble pool, on the other hand, contains macromolecules, which are much larger. This distinction is fundamental to our study of biomolecules.

Micromolecules: The Small Players

  • Low molecular weight (18-800 Da).
  • Examples: Amino acids, simple sugars, nucleotides, fatty acids.
  • Often serve as precursors or energy sources.

🎙️ Micromolecules are the 'little guys' of the biomolecular world. They are small, easily soluble, and include essential components like amino acids, which build proteins, and simple sugars, which provide energy. They are the building blocks for larger structures.

Macromolecules: The Big Builders

  • High molecular weight (10,000 Da and above).
  • Examples: Proteins, polysaccharides, nucleic acids, lipids (exception).
  • Form structural components and carry out complex functions.

🎙️ Macromolecules are the 'giants' – large, complex molecules formed by linking many micromolecules together. Proteins, polysaccharides, and nucleic acids are true polymers. Lipids are an exception here; they are found in the acid-insoluble pool due to their insolubility, but their molecular weight doesn't strictly exceed 10,000 Da. This is a common point of confusion, so remember the exception!

Primary Metabolites: Essential for Life

  • Directly involved in normal growth, development, and reproduction.
  • Found in all living cells, crucial for basic metabolic processes.
  • Examples: Amino acids, sugars, nucleotides, fatty acids.

🎙️ Primary metabolites are the workhorses of the cell. They are directly involved in fundamental metabolic pathways that are absolutely essential for an organism's survival. Without these, life as we know it simply wouldn't exist. Think of them as the basic ingredients for living.

Secondary Metabolites: Unique Roles

  • Not directly involved in normal growth or development.
  • Specific to certain organisms, often for defense or signaling.
  • Examples: Alkaloids, terpenes, rubber, essential oils, toxins.

🎙️ Secondary metabolites, in contrast, are not universally found and often have specialized roles, such as defense against predators or attracting pollinators. While not strictly essential for *individual* survival, they play crucial ecological roles and are often of great economic importance, for example, as drugs or spices.

Monosaccharides: Simple Sugars

  • Simplest carbohydrates, cannot be hydrolyzed further.
  • Sweet in taste, readily soluble in water.
  • Examples: Glucose, Fructose, Galactose, Ribose.

🎙️ Monosaccharides are the basic units of carbohydrates. They are single sugar molecules, like glucose, which is the primary energy source for most living cells. They are sweet and dissolve easily in water, making them readily available for metabolic processes.

Disaccharides: Two Sugars Together

  • Formed by two monosaccharide units joined by a glycosidic bond.
  • Sweet and soluble, like monosaccharides.
  • Examples: Sucrose (glucose+fructose), Lactose (glucose+galactose), Maltose (glucose+glucose).

🎙️ Disaccharides are formed when two monosaccharides link together, typically through a dehydration reaction forming a glycosidic bond. Common examples include sucrose, our table sugar, and lactose, the sugar found in milk. Remember these specific combinations for your exams.

Polysaccharides: Complex Carbs

  • Long chains of many monosaccharide units.
  • Storage forms of energy and structural components.
  • Examples: Starch (plant energy), Glycogen (animal energy), Cellulose (plant structure).

🎙️ Polysaccharides are large, complex carbohydrates made of hundreds or thousands of monosaccharide units. They serve crucial roles like energy storage, as seen with starch in plants and glycogen in animals, and structural support, like cellulose in plant cell walls. Proteins: The Workhorses of the Cell

  • Most abundant biomolecules in living systems.
  • Polymers of amino acids linked by peptide bonds.
  • Perform diverse functions: enzymes, hormones, structural components.

🎙️ Proteins are incredibly versatile molecules, performing almost every function within a cell. They are the 'workhorses' that do everything from catalyzing reactions to transporting molecules and providing structural support. Their functions are directly tied to their complex structures.

Amino Acids: Protein Building Blocks

  • Organic compounds with an amino group (-NH2) and a carboxyl group (-COOH).
  • Differ by their 'R' group (side chain).
  • 20 common types, some are essential (dietary).

🎙️ Amino acids are the monomers that make up proteins. Each amino acid has a central carbon atom bonded to an amino group, a carboxyl group, a hydrogen atom, and a unique side chain called the 'R' group. It's this R-group that gives each amino acid its specific properties.

Peptide Bond Formation

  • Amino acids link via a dehydration reaction.
  • Carboxyl group of one reacts with amino group of another.
  • Forms a strong covalent peptide bond, releasing water.

🎙️ Proteins are formed when amino acids join together. This occurs through the formation of a peptide bond, a covalent bond formed between the carboxyl group of one amino acid and the amino group of another, with the removal of a water molecule. This process is crucial for protein synthesis.

Protein Structures: Primary & Secondary

  • Primary: Linear sequence of amino acids.
  • Secondary: Local folding into alpha-helices or beta-pleated sheets.
  • Stabilized by hydrogen bonds within the polypeptide chain.

🎙️ Proteins have hierarchical structures. The primary structure is simply the specific order of amino acids. The secondary structure involves local folding patterns like alpha-helices and beta-pleated sheets, stabilized by hydrogen bonds between the backbone atoms. These initial folds are critical.

Protein Structures: Tertiary & Quaternary

  • Tertiary: Overall 3D folding of a single polypeptide chain.
  • Stabilized by interactions between R-groups (ionic, H-bonds, disulfide).
  • Quaternary: Arrangement of multiple polypeptide subunits.

🎙️ The tertiary structure is the complete three-dimensional shape of a single polypeptide, crucial for its function. The quaternary structure arises when multiple polypeptide chains, or subunits, assemble together to form a functional protein complex, like hemoglobin. Remember the forces stabilizing each level!

Lipids: Fats, Oils & More

  • Diverse group of hydrophobic (water-insoluble) molecules.
  • Primarily composed of carbon and hydrogen.
  • Functions: energy storage, structural components (membranes), hormones.

🎙️ Lipids are a fascinating group because of their defining characteristic: insolubility in water. This property is due to their high proportion of nonpolar hydrocarbon bonds. They are vital for long-term energy storage, forming cell membranes, and even acting as signaling molecules.

Fatty Acids & Glycerol: Lipid Building Blocks

  • Fatty acids: Long hydrocarbon chains with a carboxyl group.
  • Glycerol: A three-carbon alcohol.
  • Triglycerides formed by esterification of 3 fatty acids to 1 glycerol.

🎙️ While not strictly polymers in the same way as proteins or carbohydrates, many lipids are built from fatty acids and glycerol. Fatty acids can be saturated or unsaturated, impacting their physical properties. Triglycerides, the main form of energy storage, are formed from these two components.

Nucleotides & Nucleosides

  • Nucleotide = Nitrogenous base + Pentose sugar + Phosphate group.
  • Nucleoside = Nitrogenous base + Pentose sugar (no phosphate).
  • ATP is a nucleotide, critical for energy currency.

🎙️ The building blocks of nucleic acids are nucleotides. A nucleotide is composed of three parts: a nitrogenous base, a five-carbon sugar (pentose), and a phosphate group. If you remove the phosphate group, you get a nucleoside. ATP, the energy currency of the cell, is a prime example of a nucleotide.

Enzymes: Biological Catalysts

  • Mostly proteins, some RNA (ribozymes).
  • Speed up biochemical reactions without being consumed.
  • Highly specific to their substrates.

🎙️ Enzymes are truly remarkable molecules. They act as biological catalysts, dramatically increasing the rate of chemical reactions in living systems. Without enzymes, most metabolic reactions would occur too slowly to sustain life. Remember, they are specific and remain unchanged after the reaction.

Enzyme Mechanism: How They Work

  • Substrate binds to the enzyme's active site.
  • Forms an enzyme-substrate complex (ES complex).
  • Product released, enzyme is ready for new substrate.

🎙️ Enzymes work by binding to specific molecules called substrates at a region called the active site. This binding forms an enzyme-substrate complex, which facilitates the conversion of the substrate into a product. The enzyme then releases the product and is free to catalyze another reaction.

Factors Affecting Enzyme Activity

  • Temperature: Optimal range, denaturation at high temps.
  • pH: Optimal pH, activity drops outside range.
  • Substrate Concentration: Increases activity up to saturation point.

🎙️ Several environmental factors can significantly influence how well an enzyme functions. Temperature and pH are critical, as enzymes have an optimal range where they are most active. Beyond these ranges, they can lose their shape and activity, a process called denaturation. Substrate concentration also affects activity, up to a point where all active sites are saturated.

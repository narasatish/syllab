Unveiling the Chemical Composition

  • Analogy: Grinding spices to extract their essence for a dish.
  • Biologists grind tissue (e.g., liver) in trichloroacetic acid and filter it.
  • This separates compounds into an acid-soluble pool (micromolecules) and an acid-insoluble pool (macromolecules).

🎙️ To understand what's inside a cell, scientists use a technique involving trichloroacetic acid. This process allows us to categorize biomolecules based on their size and solubility, giving us the 'acid-soluble' and 'acid-insoluble' pools.

Micromolecules vs. Macromolecules

  • Micromolecules: Small molecular weight (<1000 Da), found in the acid-soluble pool.
  • Examples: Amino acids, simple sugars, nucleotides, fatty acids.
  • Macromolecules: Large molecular weight (>10000 Da), found in the acid-insoluble pool.
  • Examples: Proteins, polysaccharides, nucleic acids.

🎙️ This classification helps us understand the complexity. Micromolecules are the individual building blocks, while macromolecules are the large polymers formed by joining many micromolecules together. Lipids are an exception; though small, they are found in the acid-insoluble pool due to their insolubility.

Water: The Essence of Life

  • Water constitutes 70-90% of a cell's total weight, making it the most abundant chemical.
  • Crucial for metabolic reactions, transport of substances, and maintaining body temperature.
  • Often called the 'universal solvent' due to its ability to dissolve many substances.

🎙️ Water is not just a medium; it's an active participant in life. Its unique properties, like high specific heat and cohesive forces, make it indispensable for all biological processes and maintaining homeostasis in organisms.

Amino Acids: Zwitterionic Nature

  • In aqueous solutions, amino acids exist as zwitterions (dipolar ions).
  • Both the amino group (as -NH3+) and the carboxyl group (as -COO-) are ionized.
  • This dual charge allows amino acids to act as buffers, resisting changes in pH.

🎙️ The zwitterionic form is crucial for the biological function of amino acids and proteins. It enables them to maintain a stable pH environment within cells and tissues, which is vital for enzyme activity and overall cellular health.

Essential vs. Non-Essential Amino Acids

  • Essential Amino Acids: Must be obtained from the diet (e.g., Valine, Leucine, Lysine). Our body cannot synthesize them.
  • Non-Essential Amino Acids: Can be synthesized by the body from other precursors (e.g., Glycine, Alanine, Serine).

🎙️ Understanding the difference between essential and non-essential amino acids is important, especially in nutrition. A balanced diet ensures you get all the essential amino acids your body needs, as it cannot produce them on its own.

Types of Carbohydrates

  • Monosaccharides: Simplest sugars, single units (e.g., Glucose, Fructose).
  • Disaccharides: Two monosaccharides linked together (e.g., Sucrose, Lactose).

🎙️ This classification is based on the number of sugar units. Monosaccharides are the basic building blocks, which can then combine to form disaccharides and complex polysaccharides, each with different roles and properties.

Lipids: Energy & Structure

  • Analogy: Think of ghee or cooking oil – energy-rich, insulating, and flavourful.

🎙️ Lipids are unique because they don't form true polymers like other biomolecules. Their defining characteristic is their insolubility in water, which is crucial for their roles in cell membranes and energy storage.

Protein Structure: From Simple to Complex

  • Primary Structure: The unique linear sequence of amino acids.
  • Secondary Structure: Local folding into alpha-helices or beta-pleated sheets.
  • Tertiary Structure: Overall 3D folding, forming a functional protein shape.
  • Quaternary Structure: Interaction of multiple polypeptide chains (e.g., Hemoglobin).

🎙️ The specific 3D structure of a protein is critical for its function. Even a slight change in the primary sequence can lead to a misfolded protein and loss of function, as seen in diseases like sickle cell anemia.

Enzymes: Speeding Up Reactions

  • Analogy: Think of a matchmaker who helps two people meet and get married faster.
  • Enzymes do a similar job for chemical reactions in our bodies.
  • Definition: Mostly proteins, they act as biological catalysts, accelerating biochemical reactions without being consumed.

🎙️ Enzymes are indispensable for life. Without them, metabolic reactions would occur too slowly to sustain life. They achieve this by lowering the activation energy, the energy barrier that reactants must overcome to form products.

How Enzymes Work: Lock and Key Model

  • Specificity: Each enzyme has a unique active site that binds to a specific substrate.
  • Analogy: Much like a specific key fits only one lock.
  • Process: Enzyme-substrate complex forms, reaction occurs, product is released, and the enzyme is ready again.

🎙️ The lock and key model, though a simplification, beautifully illustrates the high specificity of enzymes. The precise fit ensures that enzymes catalyze only the correct reactions, preventing unwanted side reactions.

Regulating Enzyme Activity

  • Temperature: Enzymes have an optimal temperature; too high or too low reduces activity (denaturation at high temp).
  • pH: Optimal pH range; extremes can denature the enzyme.
  • Substrate Concentration: Activity increases with substrate concentration up to a saturation point.

🎙️ The environment significantly impacts enzyme activity. Maintaining optimal temperature and pH is crucial for our body's enzymes to function correctly. Any deviation can lead to loss of enzyme structure and function, impacting metabolism.

DNA vs. RNA: Key Differences

  • Sugar: DNA has deoxyribose, RNA has ribose.
  • Bases: DNA has Adenine, Thymine, Guanine, Cytosine. RNA has Adenine, Uracil, Guanine, Cytosine.
  • Structure: DNA is typically double-stranded, RNA is usually single-stranded.
  • Function: DNA stores genetic information, RNA is involved in gene expression.

🎙️ These structural and compositional differences are crucial for their distinct roles. DNA's double-stranded nature makes it stable for long-term storage, while RNA's versatility allows it to participate in various stages of protein synthesis.

Avoid Common Mistakes & Ace Your Exam!

  • Confusing essential vs. non-essential amino acids: Know which ones your body can't make.
  • Interchanging functions of starch and glycogen: Remember plant vs. animal storage.
  • Misremembering the bases in DNA vs. RNA: Uracil in RNA, Thymine in DNA.
  • Forgetting enzyme denaturation due to extreme pH/temp: Structure = Function!

🎙️ Pay close attention to these common pitfalls. A small mistake can cost you marks. Understanding the 'why' behind each concept will help you avoid these errors and build a strong foundation. Practice drawing structures and recalling definitions.

Why Do Cells Divide?

  • Growth: Increase in organism size
  • Repair: Replace damaged or dead cells
  • Reproduction: Formation of new individuals

🎙️ Cells divide for several critical reasons. For multicellular organisms, it's about growth and replacing old or damaged tissues. For single-celled organisms, it's how they reproduce and create new life.

  • Two main phases: Interphase and M-Phase

🎙️ The cell cycle is a precisely regulated series of events. It's broadly divided into a long preparatory phase called Interphase and a relatively short M-phase, where actual cell division takes place.

  • Cell grows and synthesizes proteins/RNA
  • Metabolically active, but no DNA synthesis

🎙️ The G1 phase is a crucial growth period for the cell. It's metabolically very active, synthesizing all necessary components except DNA, preparing for the next stage of the cycle.

Interphase: S Phase (Synthesis)

  • DNA replication occurs
  • DNA content doubles (2C to 4C)
  • Chromosome number remains same (2n)

🎙️ The S phase is where the cell's genetic material is duplicated. Each chromosome now consists of two sister chromatids, but the number of chromosomes themselves does not change.

Interphase: G2 Phase (Gap 2)

  • Cell continues to grow
  • Synthesizes proteins for M-phase
  • Prepares for mitosis

🎙️ In G2, the cell ensures all necessary components for division are present and checks for any DNA damage. It acts as a final checkpoint before entering the M-phase.

M-Phase: Mitosis - The Equational Division

  • Division in somatic cells
  • Produces two identical daughter cells
  • Chromosome number remains same (2n)

🎙️ Mitosis is often called equational division because the resulting daughter cells are genetically identical to the parent cell and have the same number of chromosomes. It's fundamental for growth and repair.

Mitosis: Prophase

  • Chromatin condenses into chromosomes
  • Nuclear envelope disappears
  • Spindle fibres begin to form

🎙️ Prophase is the initial stage where the diffuse chromatin material becomes tightly packed and visible as distinct chromosomes. The cell also starts dismantling its nuclear membrane and forming the mitotic spindle.

Mitosis: Metaphase

  • Chromosomes align at equatorial plate
  • Metaphase plate forms
  • Spindle fibres attach to kinetochores

🎙️ During metaphase, all the condensed chromosomes perfectly align in the center of the cell, forming the metaphase plate. This precise alignment ensures that each daughter cell receives an equal set of chromosomes.

Mitosis: Anaphase

  • Sister chromatids separate
  • Move to opposite poles of the cell
  • Each chromatid now a chromosome

🎙️ Anaphase is a rapid and critical stage where the sister chromatids, which were held together, finally pull apart. They are then considered individual chromosomes moving towards opposite ends of the cell.

Mitosis: Telophase

  • Chromosomes decondense at poles
  • Nuclear envelopes reform
  • Nucleolus and ER reappear

🎙️ Telophase essentially reverses the events of prophase. The chromosomes decondense, and new nuclear membranes form around the two sets of chromosomes, creating two distinct nuclei within the same cell.

Cytokinesis: Division of Cytoplasm

  • Plant cells: Cell plate forms

🎙️ Cytokinesis is the final step, dividing the cytoplasm and organelles to form two separate daughter cells. The mechanism differs between animal cells, which pinch off, and plant cells, which build a new wall.

Significance of Mitosis

  • Growth of multicellular organisms
  • Repair and replacement of cells
  • Asexual reproduction in some organisms

🎙️ Mitosis is indispensable for life. It ensures that an organism can grow from a single cell, repair damaged tissues, and in many simpler life forms, reproduce without a partner, maintaining genetic uniformity.

Meiosis: The Reductional Division

  • Occurs in germ cells (for gamete formation)
  • Reduces chromosome number by half
  • Produces four haploid daughter cells

🎙️ Meiosis is a specialized cell division crucial for sexual reproduction. It ensures that the chromosome number is halved, so when two gametes fuse, the offspring maintains the correct diploid chromosome count.

Meiosis I: Prophase I - The Longest Phase

  • Leptotene: Chromatin condensation
  • Zygotene: Synapsis (pairing of homologous chromosomes)
  • Pachytene: Crossing over occurs
  • Diplotene: Chiasmata visible, Diakinesis: Terminalization

🎙️ Prophase I is exceptionally long and complex, divided into five substages. The most significant event here is crossing over in Pachytene, which shuffles genetic material and creates variation.

Meiosis I: Metaphase I, Anaphase I, Telophase I

  • Metaphase I: Homologous pairs align at equator
  • Anaphase I: Homologous chromosomes separate
  • Telophase I: Two haploid cells form, each with replicated chromosomes

🎙️ In Meiosis I, it's the homologous chromosomes that separate, not sister chromatids. This reduction in chromosome number is what makes Meiosis I the 'reductional division,' leading to two haploid cells.

Meiosis II: Like Mitosis

  • No DNA replication before Meiosis II
  • Sister chromatids separate in Anaphase II
  • Results in four haploid cells (n, C)

🎙️ Meiosis II is very similar to a mitotic division. It separates the sister chromatids in each of the two cells formed after Meiosis I, ultimately yielding four haploid gametes.

Significance of Meiosis

  • Maintains chromosome number across generations
  • Introduces genetic variation (crossing over, independent assortment)
  • Essential for evolution and adaptation

🎙️ Meiosis is crucial for two main reasons: it halves the chromosome number for sexual reproduction, and more importantly, it generates genetic diversity, which is the raw material for evolution.

Comparison: Mitosis vs. Meiosis

  • Number of divisions: Mitosis (1), Meiosis (2)
  • Daughter cells: Mitosis (2 diploid), Meiosis (4 haploid)
  • Genetic variation: Mitosis (none), Meiosis (present)

🎙️ This comparison is critical for exams. Remember, mitosis is for growth and repair, producing identical cells, while meiosis is for sexual reproduction, generating genetic diversity and reducing chromosome number.

Worked Example: Chromosome & DNA Content

  • Cell in G1 (2n=4, 2C DNA)
  • After S phase (2n=4, 4C DNA)
  • After Mitosis (2n=4, 2C DNA for each daughter)
  • After Meiosis I (n=2, 2C DNA for each daughter)
  • After Meiosis II (n=2, C DNA for each daughter)

🎙️ Let's trace the changes. In S phase, DNA doubles but chromosome count stays same. In mitosis, both return to original. In meiosis I, chromosome number halves. In meiosis II, DNA content halves again.

Cell Cycle Regulation & Checkpoints

  • G1 checkpoint: Ensures cell size and resources are adequate
  • G2 checkpoint: Checks for DNA damage and replication completion
  • M checkpoint: Ensures spindle fibre attachment to chromosomes

🎙️ The cell cycle is tightly controlled by internal and external signals, primarily at checkpoints. These checkpoints prevent errors, and their failure can lead to uncontrolled cell division, like in cancer.

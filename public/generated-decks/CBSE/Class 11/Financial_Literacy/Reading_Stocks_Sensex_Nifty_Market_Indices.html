What is a Stock Market Index?

  • A statistical measure of change in a stock market.
  • Represents the performance of a 'basket' of selected stocks.
  • Acts as a barometer for the overall market or a specific sector.

🎙️ A stock market index is essentially a benchmark. It aggregates the prices of several stocks to give a single number that reflects the general trend of the market or a particular segment.

Market Barometer

Why Do We Need Indices?

  • To gauge overall market sentiment and direction.
  • To benchmark portfolio performance.
  • To understand economic health indicators.
  • For derivative trading (futures & options).

🎙️ Indices are vital tools. They help investors, analysts, and policymakers quickly understand if the market is going up or down, and how well their own investments are performing relative to the market.

Sensex: The S&P BSE Sensex

  • Full name: S&P BSE Sensex (Sensitive Index).

🎙️ Sensex is the flagship index of the Bombay Stock Exchange. The term 'Sensitive Index' highlights its responsiveness to market changes. It focuses on the largest and most actively traded companies.

How are Indices Calculated? (Overview)

  • Indices are typically 'Market-Capitalization Weighted'.
  • Companies with higher market cap have a greater impact.
  • Uses 'Free-Float Market Capitalization' for accuracy.

🎙️ It's not a simple average. The calculation method ensures that larger, more influential companies have a proportionally larger effect on the index's movement. This gives a more realistic picture of the market.

➗
Weighted Average

Market Capitalization Explained

  • Total value of a company's outstanding shares.
  • Formula: Shares Outstanding × Current Market Price per Share.
  • Indicates a company's size and value in the market.

🎙️ Market capitalization is a fundamental concept. It tells you how much a company is worth in the eyes of the stock market. This value fluctuates with the share price.

Worked Example: Market Cap

  • Company A has 100 million shares outstanding.
  • Current share price is ₹500.
  • Market Cap = 100,000,000 shares × ₹500/share = ₹50,000,000,000 (₹50 Billion).

🎙️ This example illustrates a straightforward calculation. Remember this formula, as it's key to understanding how companies are valued in the market.

Free-Float Market Capitalization

  • Only considers shares readily available for public trading.
  • Excludes shares held by promoters, government, or locked-in shares.
  • Used for index calculation to reflect actual tradable value.

🎙️ Free-float market cap is a more refined measure. It filters out shares that aren't actively traded, giving a truer picture of the company's value that can be bought and sold by the public, thus impacting the index.

🔓
Tradable Shares
17 / 30

Worked Example: Free-Float Market Cap

  • Company A: Market Cap ₹50 Billion.
  • Promoters hold 40% of shares.
  • Free-Float % = 100% - 40% = 60%.
  • Free-Float Market Cap = ₹50 Billion × 60% = ₹30 Billion.

🎙️ This shows how the free-float adjustment reduces the market cap to only the publicly available shares. Indices use this 'free-float' value for weighting components.

Understanding Index Points

  • Index value is an arbitrary number, not a currency value.
  • Movement in points indicates percentage change from previous value.
  • Sensex at 70,000 points doesn't mean each share is worth ₹70,000.

🎙️ This is a crucial point often misunderstood. Index points are just a numerical representation. What truly matters is the percentage change, as it reflects the proportional movement of the index.

📈
Relative Movement
19 / 30

Worked Example: Index Movement

  • Sensex opens at 70,000 points.
  • Closes at 70,500 points (up 500 points).
  • Percentage Change = (500 / 70,000) × 100 = 0.71%.
  • This 0.71% is the actual market gain.

🎙️ Always focus on the percentage change rather than the absolute points. A 500-point rise at 10,000 is much more significant than a 500-point rise at 70,000.

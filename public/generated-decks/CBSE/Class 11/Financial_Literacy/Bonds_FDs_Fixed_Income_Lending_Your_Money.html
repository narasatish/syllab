Fixed Deposits (FDs): Introduction

  • Deposit money for a fixed period with a bank.
  • Bank pays a fixed interest rate.
  • Principal + Interest received at maturity.

🎙️ Fixed Deposits are perhaps the simplest and most widely understood fixed income product in India. You essentially lend your money to a bank for a specific duration, and in return, the bank promises to pay you a fixed rate of interest.

FDs: Features & Benefits

  • Guaranteed returns (subject to bank solvency).
  • Low risk and capital protection.
  • Option for monthly/quarterly interest payout.
  • Tax benefits under Section 80C (for some FDs).

🎙️ The biggest appeal of FDs is their safety and predictability. You know exactly what you'll get back. They are ideal for saving for short to medium-term goals where capital preservation is key. Some FDs, like tax-saver FDs, also offer tax deductions.

FDs: Types of Interest Calculation

🎙️ Interest can be calculated in two main ways: simple or compound. Most FDs use compound interest, which means you earn interest not just on your initial deposit, but also on the interest that has already been earned. This is the power of 'interest on interest'.

Worked Example: FD Calculation

  • Question: Invest ₹50,000 for 3 years at 7% p.a. compounded annually. What is the maturity amount?
  • Solution:
  • Year 1: ₹50,000 * 0.07 = ₹3,500. Total = ₹53,500
  • Year 2: ₹53,500 * 0.07 = ₹3,745. Total = ₹57,245
  • Year 3: ₹57,245 * 0.07 = ₹4,007.15. Total = ₹61,252.15

🎙️ Let's walk through an example. Notice how the interest earned in each subsequent year is higher because it's calculated on a larger principal amount. This demonstrates the power of compounding, a concept you should be very familiar with for your exams.

Common Mistakes with FDs

  • Ignoring inflation: Returns might not beat rising costs.
  • Premature withdrawal penalties: Can reduce returns significantly.
  • Not comparing rates across banks: Always shop around.

🎙️ While FDs are safe, they aren't without pitfalls. The biggest one is often underestimating inflation, which can erode your real returns. Also, be aware of penalties if you need to break your FD before maturity, and always compare rates from different banks.

Bonds: Introduction

  • A bond is a loan made by an investor to a borrower (corporate or government).
  • The borrower promises to pay back the principal (face value) at maturity.
  • Regular interest payments (coupon payments) are made to the investor.

🎙️ Now, let's move to bonds. Think of a bond as a formal 'IOU'. When you buy a bond, you're essentially lending money to a company or government. In return, they promise to pay you interest and return your original investment at a future date.

Bonds: Key Terms

  • Face Value (Par Value): The principal amount repaid at maturity.
  • Coupon Rate: The annual interest rate paid on the face value.
  • Maturity Date: When the bond issuer repays the face value.
  • Issuer: The entity borrowing money (e.g., Govt., Company).

🎙️ These terms are crucial for understanding bonds. The face value is what you get back at the end. The coupon rate tells you how much interest you'll receive annually, usually as a percentage of the face value. The maturity date is the end date of your loan.

🎙️ While both are fixed income, there are key differences. FDs are generally with banks, while bonds can be with governments or corporations. Bonds can also be traded in a secondary market, which is not typically the case for FDs.

Types of Bonds

  • Government Bonds (G-Secs): Issued by central/state governments, lowest risk.
  • Corporate Bonds: Issued by companies, riskier than G-Secs but higher potential returns.
  • Zero-Coupon Bonds: No regular interest payments; bought at a discount, repaid at face value.

🎙️ The type of bond often indicates its risk level. Government bonds are considered extremely safe due to sovereign guarantee. Corporate bonds carry more risk, depending on the company's financial health. Zero-coupon bonds are interesting as you don't get regular payments, but profit from the difference between purchase price and face value.

Bond Yields: Understanding Returns

  • Yield: The total return an investor receives from a bond.
  • Coupon Yield: Annual coupon payment / Face Value.
  • Current Yield: Annual coupon payment / Market Price.
  • Yield to Maturity (YTM): Total return if held till maturity (complex calculation).

🎙️ Yield is a critical concept for bonds. It's not just the coupon rate. If you buy a bond in the secondary market at a price different from its face value, your actual return (yield) will be different from the coupon rate. YTM is the most comprehensive measure but involves complex financial calculations.

Worked Example: Bond Yield Calculation

  • Question: A bond with a Face Value of ₹1,000 pays a 8% coupon annually. If its current market price is ₹950, what is the Current Yield?
  • Solution:
  • Annual Coupon Payment = 8% of ₹1,000 = ₹80
  • Current Yield = (Annual Coupon Payment / Market Price) * 100
  • Current Yield = (₹80 / ₹950) * 100 = 8.42% (approx.)

🎙️ This example shows why current yield is important. Even though the coupon rate is 8%, if you buy the bond at a discount (below face value), your actual return on the money you invested (₹950) is higher. Similarly, if you buy it at a premium, your current yield would be lower.

Risks in Bonds

  • Interest Rate Risk: Bond prices fall when interest rates rise.
  • Credit Risk (Default Risk): The issuer might fail to pay interest or principal.
  • Inflation Risk: Returns might not keep pace with inflation.
  • Liquidity Risk: Difficulty selling the bond quickly at a fair price.

🎙️ While often considered safe, bonds are not risk-free. Interest rate risk is key: when new bonds offer higher rates, older bonds with lower rates become less attractive, and their market price falls. Credit risk is about the issuer's ability to pay, which is why government bonds are safer than corporate bonds.

Other Fixed Income Instruments

  • Public Provident Fund (PPF): Long-term, tax-exempt savings scheme.
  • National Savings Certificates (NSC): Fixed income, tax-saving instrument.
  • Government Securities (G-Secs): Direct bonds issued by the government.
  • Senior Citizen's Saving Scheme (SCSS): For senior citizens, higher interest rates.

🎙️ Beyond FDs and standard bonds, India offers several other fixed income options, many with tax benefits or tailored for specific demographics. PPF and NSC are very popular for long-term tax-saving goals.

Why Invest in Fixed Income?

  • Capital preservation: Protects your initial investment.
  • Regular income: Provides steady cash flow.
  • Diversification: Balances higher-risk investments (like stocks).
  • Financial planning: Helps achieve specific financial goals.

🎙️ Fixed income plays a crucial role in a balanced portfolio. It acts as a stability anchor, providing a cushion against market volatility and ensuring a steady stream of income, making it suitable for conservative investors or those nearing retirement.

Factors to Consider Before Investing

  • Investment Horizon: How long can you lock your money?
  • Risk Tolerance: How much risk are you comfortable with?
  • Liquidity Needs: Do you need access to funds easily?
  • Tax Implications: How will returns be taxed?
  • Inflation: Will your returns beat inflation?

🎙️ Before investing in any fixed income instrument, always assess these factors. Your personal financial situation and goals should drive your investment decisions. Don't just chase the highest interest rate without considering the associated risks and your own needs.

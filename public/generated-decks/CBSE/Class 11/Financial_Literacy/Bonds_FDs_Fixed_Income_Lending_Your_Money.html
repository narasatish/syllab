Introduction to Fixed Income

  • Investments that provide regular, predictable payments.
  • You lend money to a borrower (bank, government, company).
  • Borrower pays you interest and returns your principal.

🎙️ Fixed income refers to investments where you, the investor, lend money to an entity. In return, you receive fixed interest payments over a period, and your original capital is returned at the end. It's about predictable earnings.

Key Terms: Principal, Interest, Maturity

  • Principal: The initial amount you lend.
  • Interest: The 'rent' or return you earn on your principal.
  • Maturity: The date when the loan period ends and principal is returned.

🎙️ These three terms are the bedrock of understanding any fixed income instrument. Principal is your initial investment, interest is your profit, and maturity is when you get your main investment back.

Fixed Deposits (FDs): Your Bank's Promise

  • A popular and safe investment option in India.
  • You deposit a lump sum with a bank for a fixed period.
  • The bank promises a fixed interest rate for that period.

🎙️ Fixed Deposits, or FDs, are a household name in India. They are considered very safe because they are offered by banks, and in India, deposits up to a certain limit are insured by the DICGC.

How Fixed Deposits Work

  • You invest a specific amount (e.g., ₹50,000).
  • You choose a tenure (e.g., 1 year, 5 years).
  • Bank pays interest (e.g., 6% p.a.) regularly or at maturity.

🎙️ It's a straightforward process. You give your money to the bank, they hold it for an agreed time, and during that time, they pay you interest. At the end, you get your initial amount back, plus all the earned interest.

FD Features: Interest Rates and Compounding

  • Interest rates vary based on bank, tenure, and investor type (e.g., senior citizens).
  • Compounding: Interest earned also starts earning interest.
  • Higher compounding frequency (e.g., quarterly) generally means more earnings.

🎙️ The interest rate is key: it tells you how much you'll earn. Compounding is the magic here; it means your interest itself starts growing, leading to higher returns over time. Always check the compounding frequency.

Worked Example: Calculating FD Interest

  • Priya invests ₹10,000 for 1 year at 6% p.a. simple interest.
  • Interest = Principal × Rate × Time.
  • Interest = ₹10,000 × 0.06 × 1 = ₹600.

🎙️ Let's apply this to Priya's savings. If she puts ₹10,000 into an FD for one year at 6% simple interest, she will earn ₹600. So, at maturity, she gets back ₹10,600.

Bonds: Lending to Governments & Companies

  • A bond is essentially a loan you give to a government or a company.
  • They issue bonds to raise money for projects or operations.
  • You, as the investor, become a lender to them.

🎙️ While FDs are about lending to banks, bonds are about lending to larger entities like the government or big corporations. Think of it as a formal 'IOU' certificate that these entities issue.

Who Issues Bonds?

  • Central and State Governments (G-Secs for Government Securities).
  • Public Sector Undertakings (PSUs) like NHAI, PFC.
  • Private Corporations (e.g., Reliance, Tata Group).

🎙️ Bonds are issued by a wide range of entities. Government bonds are considered safest, while corporate bonds carry varying degrees of risk depending on the company's financial health. PSUs are usually a middle ground.

Bond Terminology: Face Value, Coupon, Maturity

  • Face Value (Par Value): The principal amount repaid at maturity (e.g., ₹1,000).
  • Coupon Rate: The fixed interest rate paid on the face value (e.g., 7% p.a.).
  • Maturity Date: The date when the bond issuer repays the face value.

🎙️ These are the three most critical terms for bonds. The face value is what you get back at the end, the coupon rate tells you your annual interest, and the maturity date is when the bond expires.

How Bonds Work: The Investment Cycle

  • You buy a bond (lend money to the issuer).
  • The issuer pays you regular interest (coupon payments).
  • At maturity, the issuer repays the original face value.

🎙️ The bond cycle is similar to an FD but with a few nuances. You purchase the bond, receive periodic interest, and then get your principal back. Bonds can also be traded in a secondary market before maturity.

Worked Example: Calculating Bond Coupon Payment

  • You buy a bond with a Face Value of ₹1,000.
  • The Coupon Rate is 8% p.a., paid annually.
  • Annual Coupon Payment = Face Value × Coupon Rate = ₹1,000 × 0.08 = ₹80.

🎙️ This example shows how straightforward calculating the coupon payment is. It's always based on the bond's face value, not necessarily the price you paid for it if you bought it in the secondary market.

Bond Yield vs. Coupon Rate

  • Coupon Rate: Fixed interest rate printed on the bond's face value.
  • Yield: Actual return an investor gets, considering the bond's market price.
  • If you buy a bond at a discount, your yield will be higher than the coupon rate.

🎙️ This is a key distinction. The coupon rate is static, but the 'yield' is dynamic. It changes with the bond's market price. If you buy a bond for less than its face value, your effective return, or yield, increases.

Types of Bonds: Government Bonds (G-Secs)

  • Issued by the Central or State Governments.
  • Considered the safest investment option (sovereign guarantee).
  • Lower risk often means lower interest rates compared to others.

🎙️ Government bonds, or G-Secs, are virtually risk-free in terms of default. This 'sovereign guarantee' makes them a preferred choice for conservative investors, though their returns might be modest.

Types of Bonds: Corporate Bonds

  • Issued by private companies to raise capital.
  • Carry higher risk than G-Secs, depending on the company's health.
  • Offer potentially higher interest rates to compensate for the risk.

🎙️ Corporate bonds are issued by businesses. Their risk depends on the company's creditworthiness. A highly-rated company's bond is safer than a bond from a struggling one, and the interest rate reflects this risk.

Types of Bonds: Public Sector Undertaking (PSU) Bonds

  • Issued by government-owned companies (e.g., NTPC, REC).
  • Generally safer than corporate bonds, but riskier than G-Secs.
  • Offer a balance of safety and slightly higher returns than G-Secs.

🎙️ PSU bonds occupy a middle ground. Since they are government-backed entities, they are considered relatively safe, often offering a slightly better return than pure government bonds without the higher risk of private corporate bonds.

Comparing FDs and Bonds: Key Differences

  • Issuer: FDs by banks; Bonds by Govts, PSUs, Corps.
  • Liquidity: FDs can have withdrawal penalties; Bonds can be traded.
  • Risk: FDs generally lower (DICGC insured); Bonds vary by issuer.

🎙️ This slide is crucial for understanding when to choose which instrument. FDs are simple, safe, but less liquid. Bonds offer more diversity and liquidity but come with varying risk profiles depending on who issues them.

Risk and Return: FDs vs. Bonds

  • FDs: Low risk, predictable returns, generally lower interest rates.
  • Bonds: Risk varies from very low (G-Secs) to moderate (some corporate bonds).
  • Higher risk bonds typically offer higher potential returns.

🎙️ It's the fundamental principle of investing: higher potential returns usually come with higher risk. FDs are at the lower end of the risk-return spectrum, while different types of bonds offer different balances.

Why Invest in Fixed Income Instruments?

  • Capital Preservation: Protect your initial investment.
  • Regular Income: Receive steady interest payments.
  • Diversification: Reduce overall portfolio risk.

🎙️ Fixed income is not about getting rich quickly, but about stability. It's excellent for preserving capital, generating a steady income stream, and diversifying your investment portfolio to reduce overall risk.

Common Mistakes in Fixed Income

  • Ignoring Inflation: Real return can be eroded by rising prices.
  • Premature Withdrawal: FDs incur penalties for early withdrawal.
  • Not Checking Credit Rating: For bonds, issuer's credit quality is vital.

🎙️ Students often overlook these points. Inflation can silently eat into your returns. Early FD withdrawals cost you, and for bonds, always check the issuer's credit rating to assess default risk. A low rating means higher risk.

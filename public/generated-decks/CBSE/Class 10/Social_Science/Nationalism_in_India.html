The Idea of a Nation: More Than Just Land

  • A nation is a community united by shared history, culture, or language.
  • It involves a strong sense of loyalty and belonging to one's homeland.
  • In India, this idea emerged during the anti-colonial struggle.

🎙️ Just like your family shares values and a surname, a nation is a large group of people united by common culture, history, or language. This shared identity fuels a strong sense of belonging and loyalty to their homeland.

World War I: A Catalyst for Indian Nationalism

  • Increased defence expenditure led to new taxes and customs duties.
  • Forced recruitment in rural areas caused widespread anger.
  • Economic hardship fueled discontent against British rule.

🎙️ The First World War (1914-1918) dragged India into its vortex, leading to increased defence expenditure, custom duties, and income tax. This severely burdened the common people, fueling discontent and setting the stage for nationalist movements.

Mahatma Gandhi & The Power of Satyagraha

  • Gandhi returned to India in Jan 1915 from South Africa.
  • Introduced 'Satyagraha' - a method of non-violent resistance.
  • Emphasized truth and non-violence as weapons against injustice.

🎙️ After successfully using Satyagraha against racist regimes, Gandhi brought this powerful idea to India, advocating for peaceful resistance against oppression, believing in the moral superiority of truth. He believed a true cause needed no physical force.

🧘‍♂️
Truth Force

Gandhi's Early Experiments with Satyagraha

  • Champaran (1917): Supported indigo planters against oppressive system.
  • Kheda (1917): Backed peasants demanding revenue reduction due to crop failure.
  • Ahmedabad (1918): Organized mill workers for better wages and working conditions.

🎙️ Gandhi successfully organized three local satyagraha movements, demonstrating the effectiveness of his non-violent approach. These early victories established him as a respected leader among the masses.

The Rowlatt Act (1919): An Unjust Law

  • Passed by the Imperial Legislative Council despite Indian opposition.
  • Allowed detention of political prisoners without trial for two years.
  • Aimed to curb nationalist activities and dissent.

🎙️ In 1919, the British passed the Rowlatt Act, which gave them enormous powers to repress political activities. This unjust law allowed detention of political prisoners without trial for two years, provoking widespread outrage across India.

  • On April 13, 1919, a large crowd gathered in Amritsar.
  • General Dyer ordered troops to fire on unarmed civilians.

🎙️ On 13th April 1919, a large crowd gathered at Jallianwala Bagh in Amritsar. General Dyer ordered his troops to open fire on the unarmed crowd, killing hundreds and deeply shocking the nation, intensifying the call for freedom.

Why Non-Cooperation? Gandhi's Vision for Swaraj

  • In 'Hind Swaraj' (1909), Gandhi said British rule survived due to Indian cooperation.
  • Proposed a phased movement to refuse cooperation with British institutions.
  • Believed non-cooperation would lead to 'Swaraj' (self-rule) within a year.

🎙️ In his book 'Hind Swaraj' (1909), Gandhi declared that British rule survived in India only because of Indian cooperation. He envisioned a movement where Indians would refuse to cooperate, leading to the collapse of British rule and the attainment of Swaraj.

The Khilafat Movement: Uniting Hindus & Muslims

  • Indian Muslims were angered by the harsh treatment of the Ottoman Khalifa.
  • Ali Brothers (Muhammad Ali & Shaukat Ali) formed the Khilafat Committee.
  • Gandhi saw this as an opportunity for Hindu-Muslim unity for a national movement.

🎙️ After World War I, the Ottoman Emperor (Khalifa) faced a harsh treaty. Indian Muslims, led by the Ali Brothers, formed the Khilafat Committee to defend the Khalifa, finding common cause with Gandhi's vision for a united movement.

Launching the Non-Cooperation-Khilafat Movement

  • Gandhi convinced Congress to launch a Non-Cooperation movement.
  • It aimed to support the Khilafat cause and achieve Swaraj.
  • The movement unfolded in stages, from boycotts to civil disobedience.

🎙️ At the Calcutta Congress Session in September 1920, Gandhi convinced other leaders to support a Non-Cooperation Movement in support of Khilafat and Swaraj. This united two powerful streams of protest, setting the stage for a nationwide struggle.

Non-Cooperation in Towns: Boycott & Resistance

  • Students, teachers, and lawyers boycotted government institutions.
  • Council elections were largely boycotted, except in Madras.
  • Foreign goods were boycotted, leading to a rise in Indian textile production.

🎙️ Thousands of students left government-controlled schools and colleges, headmasters and teachers resigned, and lawyers gave up their legal practices. This widespread boycott of British institutions showcased the growing resolve of the Indian people.

Students & Lawyers

NCM in the Countryside: Peasant & Tribal Uprisings

  • Awadh peasants led by Baba Ramchandra protested against high rents and begar.
  • Tribals in Gudem Hills (Andhra) led by Alluri Sitaram Raju rebelled against forest laws.
  • These movements often interpreted 'Swaraj' in their own local contexts.

🎙️ In rural areas, the NCM took on different forms. Peasants fought against exploitative landlords, while tribal communities resisted colonial forest regulations. Each group sought their own version of freedom, often interpreting 'Swaraj' in their local context.

Swaraj in the Plantations: Freedom to Move

  • For Assam plantation workers, Swaraj meant freedom to move in and out of estates.
  • They were confined under the Inland Emigration Act of 1859.
  • Thousands defied authorities, believing Gandhi Raj was coming.

🎙️ For plantation workers in Assam, 'Swaraj' meant the right to move freely in and out of the confined spaces where they were enclosed under the Inland Emigration Act of 1859. They yearned to retain a link with their villages.

  • In Feb 1922, a violent incident occurred at Chauri Chaura, Uttar Pradesh.
  • Protesters clashed with police, leading to the death of several policemen.
  • Gandhi, dismayed by the violence, called off the Non-Cooperation Movement.

🎙️ In February 1922, a violent clash at Chauri Chaura in Uttar Pradesh led to the death of several policemen. Mahatma Gandhi, dismayed by the turn towards violence, immediately called off the Non-Cooperation Movement.

The Salt March: Beginning of Civil Disobedience

  • Gandhi's letter to Viceroy Irwin listed 11 demands, including abolishing salt tax.
  • Began the Dandi March on March 12, 1930, with 78 volunteers.
  • Reached Dandi, broke the salt law, marking the start of CDM.

🎙️ Mahatma Gandhi sent a letter to Viceroy Irwin stating 11 demands, including the abolition of the salt tax, which was a vital commodity. When demands were ignored, he began the famous Dandi March in March 1930, breaking the salt law and igniting the Civil Disobedience Movement.

NCM vs. CDM: Key Differences

  • NCM aimed at non-cooperation with British institutions.
  • CDM aimed at actively breaking colonial laws (e.g., salt law).
  • CDM saw greater participation from women and industrial workers.

🎙️ While the Non-Cooperation Movement focused on boycotting British institutions, the Civil Disobedience Movement took a bolder step by actively breaking colonial laws. The scale and diversity of participation also grew significantly in the CDM.

Gandhi-Irwin Pact & Round Table Conferences

  • Gandhi-Irwin Pact (March 1931): Gandhi agreed to suspend CDM.
  • Gandhi attended the Second Round Table Conference in London.
  • Negotiations failed, leading to the resumption of CDM.

🎙️ In March 1931, Gandhi signed the Gandhi-Irwin Pact, agreeing to suspend CDM and attend the Second Round Table Conference in London. However, the negotiations failed to yield desired results, leading to the resumption of the Civil Disobedience Movement.

Dalits, Dr. Ambedkar & The Poona Pact

  • Dalit leaders, especially Dr. B.R. Ambedkar, demanded separate electorates.
  • Gandhi opposed this, fearing it would fragment Indian society.
  • The Poona Pact (1932) gave Dalits reserved seats in legislative councils.

🎙️ The question of Dalit representation was a critical challenge. While Ambedkar sought separate electorates for political empowerment, Gandhi believed in an integrated society, leading to the Poona Pact as a compromise for reserved seats.

🎙️ Beyond mass movements, the idea of a nation was also forged through cultural processes. Images like 'Bharat Mata', the rediscovery of Indian folklore, and symbols like the tricolor flag helped in creating a shared sense of collective belonging among diverse communities.

What is Federalism? Defining the System

  • A system of government where power is divided.
  • Divided between a central authority and various constituent units.
  • Two or more levels (tiers) of government.

🎙️ Formally, federalism is a system where power is split between a general government for the entire country and regional governments. Both levels have their own areas of authority, ensuring that governance is both unified and responsive to local needs.

Power Division

Key Feature 1: Two or More Levels of Government

  • The central government for the entire country.
  • State or provincial governments for regions.
  • India has three tiers: Union, State, and Local (Panchayats/Municipalities).

🎙️ The most fundamental feature is the existence of multiple levels of government. In India, we started with two, but after the 73rd and 74th Constitutional Amendments, we effectively have three distinct tiers, each with its own responsibilities.

Key Feature 2: Distinct Jurisdictions

  • Each level of government has its own specific powers.
  • Areas of legislation, taxation, and administration.
  • Powers are clearly specified in the Constitution.

🎙️ Just like a doctor and a lawyer have different areas of expertise, different levels of government have distinct jurisdictions. The Constitution clearly outlines what each level can do, preventing overlaps and conflicts. For example, defence is a Union subject, while agriculture is a State subject.

Key Feature 3: Constitutional Guarantee of Powers

  • The existence and authority of each government level are guaranteed by the Constitution.
  • No single level can unilaterally change the fundamental provisions.
  • This ensures stability and prevents power grabs.

🎙️ The Constitution is the supreme law, acting as a safeguard for federalism. It explicitly lays down the powers of both the Union and State governments, making it difficult for one level to undermine the authority of the other. This constitutional backing is crucial for the system's longevity.

Key Feature 4: Courts as Arbitrators

  • The judiciary (especially the Supreme Court) interprets the Constitution.
  • Resolves disputes between different levels of government.
  • Acts as an umpire to ensure powers are exercised within constitutional limits.

🎙️ When there are disagreements over power distribution, the courts step in. The Supreme Court of India plays a pivotal role in interpreting the Constitution and settling disputes between the Union and State governments, upholding the spirit of federalism.

Key Feature 5: Financial Autonomy

  • Each level of government has its own sources of revenue.
  • This ensures financial independence and ability to perform duties.
  • Taxes like income tax (Union) and land revenue (State) are examples.

🎙️ For any government to function effectively, it needs funds. Federalism ensures that both the Union and State governments have their own designated sources of income through various taxes. This financial autonomy prevents one level from being entirely dependent on the other.

Key Feature 6: Consent for Constitutional Changes

  • Fundamental provisions of the Constitution cannot be unilaterally changed.
  • Requires the consent of both levels of government.
  • Ensures that the federal structure is protected from easy alteration.

🎙️ To amend core aspects of India's federal structure, both the Parliament and a majority of State Legislatures must agree. This dual consent mechanism safeguards the balance of power, preventing either the Centre or the States from imposing changes on the other.

Key Feature 7: Dual Objectives of Federalism

  • To accommodate regional diversity.
  • It's a balance between central authority and regional autonomy.

🎙️ Federalism isn't just about dividing power; it's about achieving two crucial goals simultaneously. It aims to maintain national unity while respecting and preserving the vast cultural and linguistic diversities within the country. This delicate balance is key to India's strength.

Reorganising India: The Language Map (Linguistic States)

  • After independence, the demand for states based on language grew.
  • The States Reorganisation Commission (1953) recommended creating states like Andhra Pradesh.
  • This ensured people speaking the same language lived in the same state.

🎙️ The creation of linguistic states was a bold experiment. Initially, many feared it would lead to disintegration, but it actually strengthened India's unity by accommodating diversity. For example, Andhra Pradesh was the first state formed on a linguistic basis in 1953, setting a precedent for others.

Beyond Hindi: India's Language Mosaic

  • The Indian Constitution did not declare any language as the national language.
  • Hindi was identified as the official language, but states could choose their own.
  • There are 22 Scheduled Languages recognised by the Constitution.

🎙️ India's language policy is another example of federal accommodation. While Hindi is the official language, the Constitution ensures that no single language dominates. This respect for linguistic diversity has prevented conflicts and promoted harmony, unlike in countries like Sri Lanka.

Balancing Act: Centre-State Relations

However, the era of coalition governments, where no single party had a majority, forced the Centre to share power and respect state autonomy more, leading to a stronger federal structure.

Power to the People: Why Local Matters (Decentralisation)

  • Need for direct participation of citizens in governance.

🎙️ Even with state governments, the sheer size and diversity of India meant that many local issues remained unaddressed. This led to the idea of decentralisation – taking power away from the Centre and States and giving it to local governments, closer to the people. It's about empowering communities.

A Landmark Shift: The 1992 Constitutional Amendment

  • Made it mandatory to hold regular elections to local government bodies.
  • Reserved seats for Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, and Other Backward Classes.
  • Reserved at least one-third of all positions for women.

🎙️ The 1992 Amendment was a game-changer for decentralisation. It transformed local self-governance from a voluntary principle to a constitutional mandate, ensuring democratic representation and financial resources for local bodies. This was a monumental step towards true grassroots democracy.

Grassroots Democracy: The Panchayati Raj System

  • Rural local self-government, present in every village.
  • Gram Panchayat: A council with ward members (Panch) and a Sarpanch (President).
  • Gram Sabha: All adult residents of the village, meeting at least twice a year.

🎙️ The Panchayati Raj system brings democracy right to the village level. The Gram Panchayat is the decision-making body, while the Gram Sabha acts as an oversight mechanism, holding the Panchayat accountable. It's where citizens can directly participate in local development.

Urban Governance: Cities in Charge (Municipalities)

  • Municipalities govern urban areas (towns).
  • Municipal Corporations govern big cities (e.g., Mumbai, Delhi).
  • Headed by a Mayor, elected by representatives from wards.

🎙️ Just like villages have Panchayats, towns and cities have Municipalities and Municipal Corporations. These bodies are responsible for urban services like water supply, sanitation, and local infrastructure. They ensure that urban challenges are addressed by local elected representatives.

Friction Points: When Governments Disagree (Centre-State Disputes)

  • Disagreements over revenue sharing (fiscal federalism).
  • Inter-state water disputes (e.g., Cauvery water dispute between Karnataka and Tamil Nadu).
  • Misuse of central power (e.g., Article 356 to dismiss state governments).

🎙️ While federalism is beneficial, it's not without challenges. Disputes often arise over financial resources or shared natural resources like water. The misuse of emergency powers by the Centre has also been a contentious issue, sometimes straining Centre-State relations.

When the Centre Takes Over: Emergency Provisions

  • The Constitution provides for emergency powers (Articles 352, 356, 360).
  • During emergencies, the federal structure can become unitary.
  • Article 356 (President's Rule) allows the Centre to take over state administration.

🎙️ India's federal system has a unique feature: it can transform into a unitary one during emergencies. While intended for national security, Article 356 has often been criticised for its political misuse, leading to debates about the balance of power between the Centre and States.

Watch Out! Common Pitfalls in Understanding Federalism

  • Confusing 'unitary' with 'federal' systems.
  • Forgetting the role of the judiciary as an umpire.
  • Not understanding the significance of the 1992 Amendment.
  • Thinking India is a 'true' federation (it's a 'holding together' federation).

🎙️ Students often make mistakes by not grasping the nuances of federalism. Remember, India is a 'holding together' federation, not a 'coming together' one, which explains the strong central government. Also, don't underestimate the impact of the 1992 amendment on local governance.

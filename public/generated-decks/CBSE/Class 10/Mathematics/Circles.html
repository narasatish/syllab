Revisiting Circles: The Basics

  • A circle is a collection of all points in a plane that are at a fixed distance from a fixed point.
  • The fixed point is called the centre.
  • The fixed distance is called the radius.

🎙️ Before we jump into new concepts, let's quickly recall the definition of a circle. Remember, every point on the boundary of a circle is exactly the same distance from its centre. This distance is what we call the radius.

Lines & Circles: Friends or Strangers?

  • A line and a circle can interact in three main ways.
  • They can completely miss each other.
  • They can cut through the circle.
  • Or, they can just touch the circle at one point.

🎙️ Imagine drawing a line on a paper that already has a circle. There are only three possible outcomes for how that line can relate to the circle. Let's look at each of these possibilities in detail.

🎙️ In this first case, the line is completely outside the circle. It travels past the circle without ever making contact. We say that such a line is non-intersecting with the circle.

What is a Tangent? (Formal Definition)

  • A tangent to a circle is a line that intersects the circle at only one point.
  • This point is called the 'point of contact'.
  • It's like a line 'grazing' the circle's boundary.

🎙️ So, to formally define it, a tangent is a line that meets the circle at exactly one unique point. It's a very precise interaction. This concept is fundamental in many areas of mathematics and physics, so understanding it well is important.

The Right Angle Rule: Tangent ⊥ Radius

  • Theorem 10.1: The tangent at any point of a circle is perpendicular to the radius through the point of contact.
  • This means they form a 90-degree angle!
  • This is a very important property.

🎙️ This is our first major property about tangents! If you draw a radius to the exact point where the tangent touches the circle, that radius and the tangent line will always meet at a perfect right angle, 90 degrees. This property is incredibly useful for solving problems.

Analogy: The Ladder Against the Wall

  • Imagine a ladder leaning against a perfectly round pillar.
  • For the ladder to be stable and just 'touch' the pillar at one point (like a tangent), it must be perpendicular to the imaginary line (radius) from the pillar's centre to that point.
  • Any other angle would make it slide or cut into the pillar.

🎙️ Think of a ladder against a cylindrical pillar. For the ladder to just touch the pillar at one point, it needs to be positioned such that it forms a right angle with the line extending from the pillar's centre to that contact point. If it's not perpendicular, it would either slip or lean against it over a larger area, not just a single point.

Why Tangent ⊥ Radius: A Quick Look

  • 1. Assume the tangent is not perpendicular to the radius at the point of contact.
  • 2. Then there must be a shorter distance from the centre to the tangent line.
  • 3. This shorter distance would mean the line intersects the circle at two points, making it a secant, not a tangent.
  • 4. This contradicts our definition of a tangent, so it must be perpendicular.

🎙️ The formal proof involves showing that the radius is the shortest distance from the centre to the tangent line. Since the shortest distance from a point to a line is always along the perpendicular, the radius must be perpendicular to the tangent at the point of contact. This logic ensures the property holds true.

1Assume not ⊥
2Shorter distance
3Becomes secant
4Contradiction!

Case A: Point Inside the Circle

  • If the point lies inside the circle, can you draw a tangent?
  • Any line passing through an interior point will always intersect the circle at two points.
  • Therefore, no tangent can be drawn to a circle from a point inside it.

🎙️ Imagine a point right in the middle of a circle. If you try to draw a line through that point, it will always cut through the circle twice. It can never just 'touch' the circle at one point. So, from an interior point, you can't draw any tangent.

Case B: Point On the Circle

  • If the point lies on the circle itself, how many tangents can be drawn?
  • Exactly one tangent can be drawn to a circle at any point on the circle.
  • This tangent is perpendicular to the radius through that point.

🎙️ If the point is directly on the circumference of the circle, you can draw exactly one unique tangent through that point. This is the 'point of contact' we discussed earlier, and remember, the radius to this point will be perpendicular to the tangent.

Equal Partners: Tangent Lengths from External Point

  • Theorem 10.2: The lengths of tangents drawn from an external point to a circle are equal.
  • If P is an external point, and PA and PB are tangents to a circle with centre O, then PA = PB.
  • This property is extremely useful in problem-solving.

🎙️ This is our second major theorem! When you draw two tangents from the same external point to a circle, the segments from the external point to the points of contact on the circle will always have equal lengths. This is a very elegant and powerful property.

🎙️ Think of an island in a river. If you're standing on the bank and want to build two paths (tangents) to just touch the island's edge, both paths will naturally be the same length because of the symmetry of the circle. This helps us visualize why the tangent lengths are equal.

Why Tangent Lengths Are Equal: A Quick Proof

  • 1. Draw radii OA and OB to the points of contact A and B.
  • 2. Connect the external point P to the centre O.
  • 3. Triangles OAP and OBP are right-angled triangles (from Theorem 10.1).
  • 4. Using RHS congruence (Right angle, Hypotenuse OP, Side OA=OB=radius), we prove ΔOAP ≅ ΔOBP.
  • 5. By CPCTC (Corresponding Parts of Congruent Triangles are Congruent), PA = PB.

🎙️ The proof for this theorem relies on congruent triangles. By drawing radii to the points of contact and connecting the external point to the centre, we form two right-angled triangles. We can then prove these triangles are congruent using the RHS criterion, which directly leads to the conclusion that the tangent lengths are equal.

1Radii to contact
2Connect P to O
3Right triangles
4RHS Congruence
5PA = PB (CPCTC)

Common Mistakes to Avoid!

  • Confusing Secant and Tangent: Remember, a secant cuts through (two points), a tangent just touches (one point).
  • Forgetting 90°: Always remember radius ⊥ tangent at the point of contact.
  • Incorrectly applying Pythagoras: Ensure you're using the hypotenuse correctly in right-angled triangles.
  • Assuming equality: Only tangent lengths from the SAME external point are equal.

🎙️ Students often make a few common errors. It's crucial to clearly distinguish between a secant and a tangent. Also, don't forget the 90-degree angle property – it's key to solving many problems involving Pythagoras. And always double-check your application of Theorem 10.2.

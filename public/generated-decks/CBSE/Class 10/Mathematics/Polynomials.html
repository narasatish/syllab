What Exactly is a Polynomial?

  • An algebraic expression with variables and constants.
  • Variables have only non-negative integer powers.
  • Operations include addition, subtraction, multiplication.

🎙️ A polynomial is essentially an algebraic expression, but with a special condition: the powers of the variables must always be whole numbers, like 0, 1, 2, 3, and so on. No fractions or negative powers allowed!

Building Blocks: Terms, Coefficients, Variables, Constants

  • Terms: Parts separated by + or - signs.
  • Coefficients: Numerical part multiplying a variable.
  • Variables: Letters representing unknown values (e.g., x, y).
  • Constants: Numbers with fixed values (e.g., 5, -2).

🎙️ Think of a polynomial as a building. Its 'bricks' are terms, variables, coefficients, and constants. For example, in '3x² + 2x - 5', '3x²', '2x', and '-5' are terms. '3' and '2' are coefficients, 'x' is the variable, and '-5' is a constant.

Polynomials in Real Life: Area Calculation

  • Used to calculate areas of shapes.
  • Example: Area of a rectangle is length × breadth.
  • If length = (x+2) and breadth = (x), Area = x(x+2) = x² + 2x.

🎙️ Polynomials aren't just for textbooks! Imagine you're designing a room. If the length and breadth are given as expressions involving 'x', you'd use polynomials to find the area. The expression 'x² + 2x' is a polynomial that describes the area.

Understanding the Degree of a Polynomial

  • The highest power of the variable in a polynomial.
  • Helps classify polynomials.
  • Determines the maximum number of zeroes a polynomial can have.

🎙️ The degree is like the 'rank' of a polynomial. It's the highest exponent you see on any variable in the entire expression. For example, in '5x³ + 2x² - 7', the highest power of 'x' is 3, so its degree is 3.

Finding the Degree: Let's Practice!

  • 7z³ + 4z² - z + 10: Degree is 3 (highest power of z is 3).

Linear Polynomials: Straight Lines

  • Form: ax + b, where a ≠ 0.
  • Degree is 1.
  • Its graph is always a straight line.

🎙️ Linear polynomials are the simplest type. Their general form is 'ax + b', where 'a' cannot be zero. When you plot a linear polynomial on a graph, you'll always get a straight line. Think of equations like y = 2x + 3.

What are the Zeroes of a Polynomial?

  • A value of the variable that makes the polynomial equal to zero.
  • Also known as 'roots' of the polynomial equation.
  • A polynomial can have at most 'degree' number of zeroes.

🎙️ The 'zeroes' of a polynomial are incredibly important. They are the specific values you can substitute for the variable that will make the entire polynomial expression evaluate to zero. Finding zeroes is a fundamental task in algebra.

Finding Zeroes: A Simple Example

  • Consider P(x) = x - 3.
  • To find the zero, set P(x) = 0.
  • x - 3 = 0 => x = 3.
  • So, 3 is the zero of P(x).

🎙️ Let's walk through an example. If we have the polynomial P(x) = x - 3, to find its zero, we simply set P(x) equal to zero. Solving the equation x - 3 = 0 gives us x = 3. This means if you substitute 3 into the polynomial, you get 0.

2Solve for x
3The value of x is the zero

Graphical Meaning of Zeroes

  • The zeroes are the x-coordinates where the graph intersects the x-axis.
  • A linear polynomial (degree 1) intersects the x-axis at one point.
  • A quadratic polynomial (degree 2) can intersect at most two points.

🎙️ Graphically, the zeroes are very easy to spot! They are simply the points where the polynomial's graph crosses or touches the x-axis. If a graph crosses the x-axis at x=2, then 2 is a zero of that polynomial. This visual understanding is very powerful.

Sum and Product of Zeroes (Quadratic)

  • Sum of zeroes (α + β) = -b/a
  • Product of zeroes (αβ) = c/a
  • These formulas are crucial for CBSE Class 10!

🎙️ Here are the two main formulas you need to remember. The sum of the zeroes is equal to the negative of the coefficient of x, divided by the coefficient of x². And the product of the zeroes is the constant term divided by the coefficient of x². Make sure to memorize these!

Applying the Relationship: Worked Example

  • Polynomial: x² + 5x + 6
  • Here, a=1, b=5, c=6.
  • Sum of zeroes = -b/a = -5/1 = -5.
  • Product of zeroes = c/a = 6/1 = 6.

🎙️ Let's apply these formulas to an example: x² + 5x + 6. First, identify a, b, and c. Here, a=1, b=5, and c=6. Now, just plug these values into our formulas. The sum of zeroes is -5, and the product of zeroes is 6. Simple, right?

Division Algorithm for Polynomials

  • Just like numbers, polynomials can also be divided.
  • Dividend = Divisor × Quotient + Remainder.
  • The remainder's degree is less than the divisor's degree.

🎙️ The division algorithm for polynomials is very similar to the one we use for numbers. When you divide one polynomial (the dividend) by another (the divisor), you get a quotient and a remainder. The key rule is that the degree of the remainder must always be less than the degree of the divisor.

How to Divide Polynomials: Step-by-Step

  • Arrange terms in descending order of degree.
  • Divide the first term of the dividend by the first term of the divisor.
  • Multiply the divisor by this result and subtract.
  • Bring down the next term and repeat until degree of remainder < divisor.

🎙️ Polynomial long division can seem tricky at first, but it's a systematic process. Always start by arranging your polynomials in descending powers of the variable. Then, you repeatedly divide the leading terms, multiply, and subtract until you can no longer divide. Practice makes perfect here!

1Arrange
2Divide first terms
3Multiply & Subtract
4Repeat

🎙️ One fascinating real-world application of polynomials is in projectile motion. When you throw a ball or launch a rocket, its path can be modeled by a quadratic polynomial. These equations help scientists and engineers calculate how high something will go or where it will land.

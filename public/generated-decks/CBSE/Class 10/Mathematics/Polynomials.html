What are Polynomials?

  • An algebraic expression with variables and coefficients.
  • Variables have non-negative integer exponents (whole numbers).
  • Combined using addition, subtraction, multiplication.

🎙️ Simply put, a polynomial is like a mathematical sentence made up of variables, numbers, and operations. The key rule is that the powers of the variables must always be whole numbers, like 0, 1, 2, 3, and so on, never fractions or negative numbers.

Building Blocks of a Polynomial

  • Term: Each part separated by + or - (e.g., 3x²).
  • Coefficient: The numerical factor of a term (e.g., 3 in 3x²).
  • Variable: The letter representing an unknown value (e.g., x in 3x²).

🎙️ Think of a polynomial as a LEGO model. Each LEGO brick is a 'term'. The 'color' of the brick could be its coefficient, and the 'shape' could be the variable with its power. Understanding these parts is crucial.

What is the Degree of a Polynomial?

  • The highest power of the variable in a polynomial.
  • Determines the type and behavior of the polynomial.
  • Example: In 5x³ + 2x - 7, the degree is 3.

🎙️ The degree is like the 'rank' of a polynomial. It tells us the highest power a variable reaches in the expression. This 'rank' is very important as it classifies polynomials and helps us understand their graphs and properties.

Classifying Polynomials by Degree

  • Linear: Degree 1 (e.g., 2x + 5).
  • Quadratic: Degree 2 (e.g., 3x² - x + 1).
  • Cubic: Degree 3 (e.g., x³ + 4x² - 2).

🎙️ Just like we have different types of vehicles, polynomials are classified by their degree. A degree 1 polynomial is linear, degree 2 is quadratic, and degree 3 is cubic. Each type has unique characteristics, especially when graphed.

Classifying Polynomials by Number of Terms

  • Monomial: One term (e.g., 5x²).
  • Binomial: Two terms (e.g., 3x + 7).
  • Trinomial: Three terms (e.g., x² - 2x + 1).

🎙️ Besides degree, we can also classify polynomials by how many terms they have. 'Mono' means one, 'bi' means two, and 'tri' means three. So, a monomial has one term, a binomial has two, and a trinomial has three terms.

Understanding Zeroes of a Polynomial

  • A 'zero' is a value of the variable that makes the polynomial equal to zero.
  • Also known as 'roots' of the polynomial.
  • If P(k) = 0, then 'k' is a zero of P(x).

🎙️ The zeroes of a polynomial are incredibly important. They are the specific numbers you can substitute for the variable that will make the entire polynomial expression equal to zero. Think of it as hitting the bullseye!

Zeroes Geometrically: Linear Polynomials

  • A linear polynomial (ax + b) has exactly one zero.
  • The graph is a straight line.
  • The zero is the x-coordinate where the line crosses the x-axis.

🎙️ When you graph a linear polynomial, you get a straight line. The point where this line intersects the x-axis is the geometric representation of its zero. Since a straight line crosses the x-axis at most once, a linear polynomial has at most one zero.

Zeroes Geometrically: Quadratic Polynomials

  • A quadratic polynomial (ax² + bx + c) can have at most two zeroes.
  • The graph is a parabola (U-shaped curve).
  • Zeroes are the x-coordinates where the parabola crosses the x-axis.

🎙️ For quadratic polynomials, the graph is a curve called a parabola. This parabola can intersect the x-axis at two points, one point, or no points. Each intersection point represents a zero of the polynomial. Thus, a quadratic polynomial has at most two zeroes.

Zeroes Geometrically: Cubic Polynomials

  • A cubic polynomial can have at most three zeroes.
  • The graph is a curve with up to two turns.
  • Zeroes are the x-coordinates where the curve crosses the x-axis.

🎙️ Cubic polynomials have graphs that can be more complex, often looking like an 'S' shape or a wavy line. These curves can intersect the x-axis at most three times, meaning a cubic polynomial can have at most three zeroes.

Example: Finding Zeroes of a Linear Polynomial

  • Problem: Find the zero of P(x) = 2x - 6.
  • Step 1: Set P(x) = 0.
  • Step 2: Solve for x.

🎙️ To find the zero of any polynomial, the basic idea is to set the entire expression equal to zero and then solve for the variable. For a linear polynomial, this is a straightforward algebraic equation.

1Set P(x) = 0
22x - 6 = 0
32x = 6
4x = 3

Example: Finding Zeroes of a Quadratic Polynomial

  • Problem: Find the zeroes of P(x) = x² - 5x + 6.
  • Step 1: Set P(x) = 0.
  • Step 2: Factorize the quadratic expression.
  • Step 3: Set each factor to zero and solve.

🎙️ For quadratic polynomials, factoring is a common method to find zeroes. Once factored, we use the property that if a product of terms is zero, at least one of the terms must be zero.

1Set x² - 5x + 6 = 0
2(x - 2)(x - 3) = 0
3x - 2 = 0 or x - 3 = 0
4x = 2 or x = 3

Zeroes and Coefficients: Quadratic Polynomials

  • For ax² + bx + c, let zeroes be α and β.
  • Sum of zeroes (α + β) = -b/a.
  • Product of zeroes (αβ) = c/a.

🎙️ This is a very important concept! For quadratic polynomials, there's a direct relationship between the zeroes and the coefficients. Knowing these formulas allows us to find the sum and product of zeroes without actually finding the zeroes themselves.

Example: Verifying Zeroes-Coefficients Relationship

  • Polynomial: x² - 5x + 6. Zeroes are 2 and 3.
  • Coefficients: a=1, b=-5, c=6.
  • Sum of zeroes: 2 + 3 = 5. From formula: -(-5)/1 = 5. (Matches!)
  • Product of zeroes: 2 × 3 = 6. From formula: 6/1 = 6. (Matches!)

🎙️ Let's put the relationship to test with our previous example. We found the zeroes to be 2 and 3. By applying the formulas and comparing them with the actual sum and product, we can see that the relationship holds true.

1Identify a, b, c
2Calculate Sum (α+β)
3Calculate Product (αβ)
4Compare with -b/a, c/a

Zeroes and Coefficients: Cubic Polynomials

  • For ax³ + bx² + cx + d, let zeroes be α, β, γ.
  • Sum of zeroes (α + β + γ) = -b/a.
  • Sum of products of zeroes taken two at a time (αβ + βγ + γα) = c/a.
  • Product of zeroes (αβγ) = -d/a.

🎙️ The relationship extends to cubic polynomials as well, though with more terms. These formulas are incredibly useful for constructing polynomials when zeroes are given, or for finding sums and products without solving for individual zeroes.

Division Algorithm for Polynomials

  • If P(x) and G(x) are two polynomials, G(x) ≠ 0.
  • We can find Q(x) (quotient) and R(x) (remainder).
  • Such that P(x) = G(x) × Q(x) + R(x).
  • Where R(x) = 0 or degree of R(x) < degree of G(x).

🎙️ Just like how we divide numbers, we can also divide polynomials. The division algorithm for polynomials states that we can always find a quotient and a remainder, much like in regular division. This is fundamental for finding factors and zeroes.

Example: Dividing Polynomials

  • Problem: Divide P(x) = x² + 3x + 2 by G(x) = x + 1.
  • Step 1: Perform long division.
  • Step 2: Find Quotient Q(x) and Remainder R(x).

🎙️ Polynomial long division follows a similar process to numerical long division. We aim to eliminate the highest degree term in each step until the remainder's degree is less than the divisor's degree, or the remainder is zero.

1Set up long division (x²+3x+2)/(x+1)
2x(x+1) = x²+x
3Subtract: (x²+3x+2) - (x²+x) = 2x+2
42(x+1) = 2x+2
5Subtract: (2x+2) - (2x+2) = 0
6Quotient: x+2, Remainder: 0

Recap: What We've Learned

  • Polynomials are algebraic expressions with whole number exponents.

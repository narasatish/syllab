What is a Sequence?

  • Each number is called a 'term'.

🎙️ A sequence is simply a collection of numbers arranged in a definite order, according to some rule. Think of it like a line of students arranged by height, or a list of even numbers. Each number in the list is a 'term'.

What makes a sequence an Arithmetic Progression?

  • A sequence where the difference between consecutive terms is constant.
  • This constant difference is called the 'Common Difference'.
  • It's like climbing stairs with equal step heights.

🎙️ An Arithmetic Progression, or AP, is a special type of sequence. The magic here is that if you subtract any term from its succeeding term, you always get the same number. This constant number is very important!

The 'Common Difference' (d)

  • Denoted by 'd'.
  • Calculated by: any term - previous term (e.g., a₂ - a₁).
  • Can be positive, negative, or zero.

🎙️ The 'common difference', denoted by 'd', is the heart of an AP. To find 'd', just subtract any term from the term that immediately follows it. Remember, 'd' must be the same throughout the entire sequence for it to be an AP.

The General Form of an AP

  • If 'a' is the first term and 'd' is the common difference.
  • The AP can be written as: a, a+d, a+2d, a+3d, ...
  • This pattern helps us find any term.

🎙️ Just like we have a general form for linear equations, APs also have a general structure. If you know the first term ('a') and the common difference ('d'), you can write out the entire progression. This general form is super useful!

Finding any term: The nth Term of an AP

  • We can find any term without listing all of them.
  • Formula: aₙ = a + (n-1)d
  • Where aₙ is the nth term, 'a' is the first term, 'd' is the common difference, and 'n' is the term number.

🎙️ Imagine an AP with 100 terms! It would be tedious to list them all to find the 99th term. Luckily, we have a powerful formula! This formula, aₙ = a + (n-1)d, allows us to jump directly to any term we want.

Understanding the nth Term Formula

  • a₁ = a
  • a₂ = a + d
  • a₃ = a + 2d
  • Notice the pattern: for aₙ, we add 'd' (n-1) times.

🎙️ Let's break down the formula. The first term is 'a'. To get to the second term, you add 'd' once. To get to the third term, you add 'd' twice. So, to get to the 'n'th term, you add 'd' (n-1) times. Simple, right?

11st term (a₁) = a
22nd term (a₂) = a + 1d
33rd term (a₃) = a + 2d
4nth term (aₙ) = a + (n-1)d

Worked Example: Let's find the 10th term!

  • AP: 2, 5, 8, 11...
  • Here, a = 2, d = 3, n = 10.
  • a₁₀ = a + (10-1)d
  • a₁₀ = 2 + (9)3 = 2 + 27 = 29.

🎙️ Consider the AP: 2, 5, 8, 11... We need to find the 10th term. First, identify 'a' (which is 2) and 'd' (which is 3). Now, substitute these values into our nth term formula. The 10th term of this AP is 29.

Finite vs. Infinite Arithmetic Progressions

  • Finite AP: Has a limited number of terms.
  • Infinite AP: Continues indefinitely, no last term.
  • Example of Finite: 2, 4, 6, ..., 20 (ends)
  • Example of Infinite: 1, 3, 5, 7, ... (continues)

🎙️ APs can be classified into two types: finite and infinite. A finite AP has a definite number of terms, meaning it has a last term. An infinite AP, on the other hand, goes on forever, indicated by '...' at the end.

Adding them all up: Sum of an AP

  • Sometimes we need the total of all terms.
  • Imagine summing up your daily savings.
  • This is called the 'Sum of the first n terms', denoted by Sₙ.

🎙️ What if you wanted to know your total savings after 30 days? You wouldn't want to add each day's amount individually! Fortunately, there's a clever way to find the sum of terms in an AP, which we call Sₙ.

➕➕➕➕➕
Adding Terms
18 / 30

Unlocking the Sum Formula (Part 1)

  • Let Sₙ = a + (a+d) + ... + (l-d) + l (Equation 1)
  • Now, write the sum in reverse order:
  • Sₙ = l + (l-d) + ... + (a+d) + a (Equation 2)

🎙️ This formula was famously derived by a young mathematician named Gauss. He wrote the sum of an AP in two ways: one forward and one backward. This clever trick helps us derive the sum formula easily.

1S = a + (a+d) + ... + l
2S = l + (l-d) + ... + a
19 / 30

Unlocking the Sum Formula (Part 2)

  • Add Equation 1 and Equation 2 term by term.
  • 2Sₙ = (a+l) + (a+l) + ... (n times)
  • 2Sₙ = n(a+l)
  • So, Sₙ = n/2 (a+l), where 'l' is the last term (aₙ).

🎙️ When you add the forward and backward sums, something magical happens! Each pair of terms adds up to (a+l). Since there are 'n' terms, we get 'n' such pairs. This leads us directly to the elegant formula Sₙ = n/2 (a+l).

💡
The Sum Formula
20 / 30

The Sum Formula using 'd'

  • We know that l = aₙ = a + (n-1)d.
  • Substitute 'l' into Sₙ = n/2 (a+l).
  • Sₙ = n/2 [a + {a + (n-1)d}]
  • Sₙ = n/2 [2a + (n-1)d].

🎙️ Sometimes, you might not know the last term 'l' directly. But you always know 'a' and 'd'! By substituting the formula for 'l' (which is aₙ) into our sum formula, we get another extremely useful form: Sₙ = n/2 [2a + (n-1)d].

Worked Example: Let's find the sum of the first 20 terms!

  • AP: 1, 4, 7, 10...
  • Here, a = 1, d = 3, n = 20.
  • S₂₀ = 20/2 [2(1) + (20-1)3]
  • S₂₀ = 10 [2 + 19 × 3] = 10 [2 + 57] = 10 × 59 = 590.

🎙️ Let's apply the sum formula. For the AP 1, 4, 7, 10..., we want the sum of the first 20 terms. We have 'a' = 1, 'd' = 3, and 'n' = 20. Plug these values into the formula Sₙ = n/2 [2a + (n-1)d] to get the sum.

The Arithmetic Mean

  • If a, b, c are in AP, then 'b' is the Arithmetic Mean of 'a' and 'c'.
  • Formula: b = (a + c) / 2.
  • It's the middle term that maintains the common difference.

🎙️ The concept of Arithmetic Mean is closely related to APs. If you have three numbers in an AP, the middle number is the arithmetic mean of the other two. It's simply the average of the two extreme terms.

Beware of Common Mistakes!

  • Confusing 'n' with 'n-1' in formulas.
  • Sign errors when calculating 'd' (a₂ - a₁).
  • Mixing up aₙ (nth term) and Sₙ (sum of n terms).
  • Calculation errors with negative numbers.

🎙️ Students often make a few common errors. Always double-check if you're using 'n' or 'n-1' correctly, especially in the nth term formula. Pay close attention to signs when finding 'd', and clearly distinguish between finding a specific term and finding the sum of terms.

Quick Recap: What did we learn?

What is an Equation?

  • A mathematical statement showing two expressions are equal.
  • It always contains an '=' sign.
  • Example: 2 + 3 = 5 or x + 7 = 10.

🎙️ Think of an equation like a balanced scale. Whatever is on one side must be equal in 'weight' or 'value' to what's on the other side. The equal sign is the pivot that keeps everything balanced.

What is a Linear Equation?

  • An equation where the highest power of the variable is 1.
  • When plotted on a graph, it forms a straight line.
  • Example: 2x + 5 = 11 (power of x is 1).

🎙️ The word 'linear' comes from 'line'. This means if you were to draw a picture of this equation, it would always be a straight line, never a curve. This is because the variable isn't squared or cubed, it's just 'x' to the power of one.

Linear Equation in One Variable

  • Contains only one unknown quantity (variable).
  • General form: ax + b = 0, where a ≠ 0.
  • Example: 3x - 6 = 0. Here, 'x' is the only variable.

🎙️ A linear equation in one variable is the simplest type. You've probably solved many of these before! It has just one letter, like 'x' or 'y', whose value you need to find. There's usually only one unique solution for 'x'.

Real-life Example: One Variable

  • Problem: 'If I add 5 to twice my age, I get 25.' What is my age?
  • Let my age be 'x'.
  • Equation: 2x + 5 = 25.
  • Solution: 2x = 20, so x = 10. My age is 10 years.

🎙️ See how easily we can turn a sentence into an equation? By representing the unknown age with 'x', we can set up the equation and solve it to find the exact age. This shows the practical use of equations.

Variables: The Unknowns

  • Their values can change or vary.

🎙️ Variables are like placeholders for numbers we don't know yet. In algebra, we use letters to represent these numbers. Understanding the difference between variables and constants is key to forming correct equations.

Linear Equation in Two Variables

  • Contains two unknown quantities, usually 'x' and 'y'.
  • General form: ax + by + c = 0, where a, b, c are real numbers and a, b ≠ 0.
  • Example: 2x + 3y = 7.

🎙️ Now, let's add another layer of complexity! Sometimes, we have two different things we don't know, and they are related. That's when we use two variables, like 'x' and 'y'. Both 'x' and 'y' have a power of 1, keeping it 'linear'.

Real-life Example: Two Variables

  • Problem: 'The cost of 2 pens and 3 notebooks is ₹60.'
  • Let cost of one pen be 'x' and one notebook be 'y'.
  • Equation: 2x + 3y = 60.
  • Here, we have two unknowns, 'x' and 'y'.

🎙️ Imagine you go shopping. If you buy different items, each with an unknown price, you'll need two variables. This equation shows the total cost based on the number of pens and notebooks you buy. Can you find unique values for x and y from just this one equation? Not really, because many pairs of x and y could make 60!

Solution of a Linear Equation in Two Variables

  • A pair of values (x, y) that satisfies the equation.
  • Meaning, when you substitute 'x' and 'y' into the equation, both sides become equal.
  • A single linear equation in two variables has infinitely many solutions.

🎙️ Because there are two variables, a solution isn't just one number, but a pair of numbers – one for 'x' and one for 'y'. For example, in 2x + 3y = 7, (2, 1) is a solution because 2(2) + 3(1) = 4 + 3 = 7. But there are many other pairs too, like (3.5, 0) or (5, -1).

Pair of Linear Equations in Two Variables

  • When we have two linear equations involving the same two variables.
  • We need a pair of equations to find a unique solution for 'x' and 'y'.
  • Example: x + y = 5 and x - y = 1.

🎙️ This is the core of our chapter! To get a single, specific answer for both 'x' and 'y', we usually need two different clues or conditions. Each clue gives us one equation, and together, they form a 'pair' of linear equations.

Methods to Solve a Pair of Linear Equations

  • We have different techniques to find the unique solution (x, y).
  • Mainly divided into two categories: Graphical Method and Algebraic Methods.
  • Each method has its own advantages.

🎙️ Just like there are many ways to reach a destination, there are different methods to solve a pair of linear equations. We'll explore both graphical and algebraic approaches. The graphical method gives us a visual understanding, while algebraic methods are usually more precise.

Graphical Method: Introduction

  • Each linear equation in two variables represents a straight line on a graph.
  • The solution to a pair of equations is the point where their lines intersect.
  • We plot both lines and observe their relationship.

🎙️ Imagine drawing both lines on a graph paper. The point where they cross each other, if they do, is the solution! Why? Because that point lies on both lines, meaning its (x, y) coordinates satisfy both equations simultaneously. There are three main possibilities for how two lines can behave.

Plotting lines

Graphical Method: Intersecting Lines

  • The two lines cross each other at exactly one point.
  • This indicates a unique solution for the pair of equations.
  • Condition: a₁/a₂ ≠ b₁/b₂.

🎙️ When two lines intersect, they meet at only one spot. This means there's just one pair of (x, y) values that works for both equations. This is the most common and often desired outcome when solving problems.

❌
One common point
15 / 25

Graphical Method: Parallel Lines

  • The two lines never meet, no matter how far they are extended.
  • This means there is no common point, hence no solution.
  • Condition: a₁/a₂ = b₁/b₂ ≠ c₁/c₂.

🎙️ Think of railway tracks – they run side by side but never cross. If your two equations result in parallel lines, it means there's no (x, y) pair that can satisfy both conditions at the same time. So, there's no solution to that system of equations.

🛤️
Never meeting

Graphical Method: Coincident Lines

  • The two lines lie exactly on top of each other, overlapping completely.
  • This means every point on one line is also on the other line.
  • There are infinitely many solutions.
  • Condition: a₁/a₂ = b₁/b₂ = c₁/c₂.

🎙️ Coincident lines are essentially the same line, just perhaps written in different forms. Since they share all their points, there are endless solutions! Any point on that line is a solution to both equations.

Algebraic Methods: Overview

  • These methods use mathematical operations to solve equations.
  • They are generally more precise than graphical methods.
  • Three main methods: Substitution, Elimination, and Cross-Multiplication.

🎙️ Algebraic methods are like solving puzzles using a set of rules and steps. We manipulate the equations to isolate the variables and find their exact values. We will focus on Substitution and Elimination in this lesson.

Algebraic Method: Substitution

  • Express one variable from one equation in terms of the other variable.
  • Substitute this expression into the second equation.
  • Solve the resulting equation for the single variable.
  • Substitute back to find the value of the first variable.

🎙️ The word 'substitution' means replacing something. Here, we replace one variable with an equivalent expression from the other equation. This reduces the problem to a single variable equation, which we already know how to solve!

🔄
Replacing a value

Worked Example: Substitution Method

  • Solve: x + y = 5 (1) and x - y = 1 (2)
  • From (1), x = 5 - y.
  • Substitute x in (2): (5 - y) - y = 1 => 5 - 2y = 1 => 2y = 4 => y = 2.
  • Substitute y = 2 in x = 5 - y: x = 5 - 2 => x = 3.
  • Solution: (x, y) = (3, 2).

🎙️ Let's walk through this step-by-step. First, we isolated 'x' from the first equation. Then, we plugged that expression for 'x' into the second equation. This gave us an equation with only 'y', which we solved. Finally, we used the value of 'y' to find 'x'. Simple, right?

Algebraic Method: Elimination

  • Make the coefficients of one variable equal in both equations.
  • Add or subtract the equations to eliminate that variable.
  • Solve the resulting equation for the single variable.
  • Substitute back to find the value of the eliminated variable.

🎙️ Elimination means getting rid of something. Here, we try to get rid of one of the variables by making its coefficients the same in both equations. Then, by adding or subtracting the equations, that variable disappears, leaving us with a simpler equation to solve.

✂️
Cutting out a variable

Worked Example: Elimination Method

  • Solve: 2x + 3y = 11 (1) and 2x - 2y = -4 (2)
  • Notice '2x' has same coefficient.
  • Subtract (2) from (1): (2x + 3y) - (2x - 2y) = 11 - (-4) => 5y = 15 => y = 3.
  • Substitute y = 3 in (1): 2x + 3(3) = 11 => 2x + 9 = 11 => 2x = 2 => x = 1.
  • Solution: (x, y) = (1, 3).

🎙️ In this example, '2x' already had the same coefficient. So, subtracting the equations directly eliminated 'x'. We then solved for 'y' and substituted it back into one of the original equations to find 'x'. This method is often quicker when coefficients are easy to match.

Recap: Key Takeaways

  • A pair of linear equations in two variables can have one, no, or infinitely many solutions.
  • Graphical method helps visualize solutions as intersecting, parallel, or coincident lines.
  • Algebraic methods (Substitution, Elimination) provide precise solutions.
  • These concepts are vital for solving real-world problems involving two unknowns.

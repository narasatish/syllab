  • A mathematical statement showing equality.
  • Contains an '=' sign.
  • Balances two expressions.

🎙️ An equation is fundamentally a statement that two mathematical expressions are equal. Think of it like a balanced scale, where what's on one side must exactly match what's on the other. The equal sign is the key element that defines an equation.

Understanding Linear Equations

  • Highest power of any variable is 1.
  • No variables multiplied together (like xy).
  • Graphs as a straight line.

🎙️ A linear equation is special because its variables appear only with an exponent of one. This means you won't see terms like x-squared or y-cubed. The most important characteristic is that when you plot a linear equation on a graph, it always forms a perfect straight line.

Linear Equation in Two Variables

  • Involves two unknown quantities (e.g., x and y).
  • General form: ax + by + c = 0.
  • a, b, c are real numbers; a and b are not both zero.

🎙️ Now, let's add another unknown! A linear equation in two variables means we have two different quantities we need to find, commonly represented by 'x' and 'y'. The general way to write it is ax + by + c = 0, where 'a' and 'b' cannot both be zero, ensuring it remains a linear equation.

Introducing a Pair of Linear Equations

  • Sometimes one equation isn't enough.
  • We need two related equations.
  • These form a 'system' of equations.

🎙️ What if we have two unknowns, but only one equation? It's impossible to find unique values! That's when we need a 'pair' of linear equations. These are two equations that work together to help us find the values of both unknown variables.

General Form of a Pair of Linear Equations

  • Equation 1: a₁x + b₁y + c₁ = 0
  • Equation 2: a₂x + b₂y + c₂ = 0
  • a₁, b₁, c₁, a₂, b₂, c₂ are real numbers.

🎙️ A pair of linear equations has a specific general form. We use subscripts to differentiate between the coefficients of the first equation (a₁, b₁, c₁) and the second equation (a₂, b₂, c₂). Remember, in both equations, the coefficients 'a' and 'b' cannot both be zero simultaneously.

Graphical Method: What Does it Mean?

  • Each linear equation forms a straight line.
  • We plot both lines on the same graph.
  • The solution is where the lines meet.

🎙️ In the graphical method, we represent each equation as a line on a coordinate plane. The point where these two lines cross each other is the solution to our pair of equations. This point's (x, y) coordinates satisfy both equations simultaneously.

Graphical Method: Intersecting Lines

  • The two lines cross at exactly one point.
  • This point is the unique solution (x, y).
  • The system is 'consistent' and has a unique solution.

🎙️ When you graph two linear equations and they intersect, it means they share exactly one common point. This point is the one and only solution to the system. Such a system is called 'consistent' because a solution exists.

Graphical Method: Coincident Lines

  • The two equations represent the exact same line.
  • One line lies completely on top of the other.
  • Infinitely many solutions (every point on the line).

🎙️ Sometimes, when you graph two equations, you might find that they are actually the same line! This means every single point on that line is a common solution to both equations. Therefore, there are infinitely many solutions, and this system is also considered 'consistent'.

🟰

Graphical Method: Parallel Lines

  • The two lines never meet, no matter how far they extend.
  • They maintain the same distance apart.
  • No common point means 'no solution'.

🎙️ In this case, the two lines you plot will be parallel to each other. Just like railway tracks, they will never intersect. Since there's no common point, there is no value of (x, y) that satisfies both equations simultaneously. This system is called 'inconsistent' because it has no solution.

Worked Example: Graphical Method (Intersecting)

  • Equations: x + y = 5 and x - y = 1.
  • Find points for each line (e.g., for x+y=5: (0,5), (5,0)).
  • Plot points and draw lines on a graph paper.

🎙️ Let's solve x + y = 5 and x - y = 1 graphically. For the first equation, if x=0, y=5; if y=0, x=5. So, (0,5) and (5,0) are two points. For the second, if x=0, y=-1; if y=0, x=1. So, (0,-1) and (1,0) are two points. When you plot these and draw the lines, they will intersect at the point (3, 2). So, x=3, y=2 is the solution.

Method 1: Substitution

  • Express one variable in terms of the other from one equation.
  • Substitute this expression into the second equation.
  • Solve the resulting single-variable equation.
  • Substitute back to find the other variable.

🎙️ The substitution method is like replacing one piece of information with another. You pick one equation, rearrange it to get one variable by itself (e.g., x = ...). Then, you take that entire expression and 'substitute' it into the other equation, which simplifies it to an equation with only one variable, making it solvable.

18 / 25

Worked Example: Substitution Method

  • Equations: x + y = 5 (1) and x - y = 1 (2).
  • From (1), x = 5 - y.
  • Substitute 'x' in (2): (5 - y) - y = 1.
  • Solve for y, then find x.

🎙️ Let's use our example: x + y = 5 and x - y = 1. From the first equation, we can write x = 5 - y. Now, replace 'x' in the second equation with '5 - y'. This gives us (5 - y) - y = 1. Solving this, we get 5 - 2y = 1, so 2y = 4, and y = 2. Substitute y=2 back into x = 5 - y, and we find x = 3. The solution is (3, 2).

Method 2: Elimination

  • Make coefficients of one variable equal (by multiplying equations).
  • Add or subtract the equations to 'eliminate' that variable.
  • Solve the resulting single-variable equation.
  • Substitute back to find the other variable.

🎙️ The elimination method aims to get rid of one variable by making its coefficients numerically equal in both equations. You might need to multiply one or both equations by a constant. Once the coefficients are the same (or opposite), you either add or subtract the equations to 'eliminate' that variable, leaving you with an equation in just one variable.

Worked Example: Elimination Method

  • Equations: x + y = 5 (1) and x - y = 1 (2).
  • Notice 'y' coefficients are +1 and -1.
  • Add (1) and (2): (x+y) + (x-y) = 5+1.
  • Solve for x, then find y.

🎙️ Using x + y = 5 and x - y = 1 again. Observe that the 'y' terms have opposite coefficients (+1y and -1y). If we add the two equations directly, the 'y' terms will cancel out: (x+y) + (x-y) = 5+1, which simplifies to 2x = 6. This gives x = 3. Substitute x=3 into either original equation (e.g., 3 + y = 5) to find y = 2. The solution is (3, 2).

Method 3: Cross-Multiplication

  • A formulaic method for general form equations.
  • Coefficients are 'cross-multiplied' in a specific pattern.
  • Directly gives the values of x and y.

🎙️ The cross-multiplication method is a more direct way to find the solution, especially useful for competitive exams. It uses a specific formula derived from the general form of the equations. You arrange the coefficients in a particular pattern and perform cross-multiplications to calculate x and y. It's a bit like a shortcut once you understand the pattern.

Worked Example: Cross-Multiplication Method

  • Equations: x + y - 5 = 0 (a₁=1, b₁=1, c₁=-5)
  • And x - y - 1 = 0 (a₂=1, b₂=-1, c₂=-1).
  • Apply the formula: x / (b₁c₂ - b₂c₁) = y / (c₁a₂ - c₂a₁) = 1 / (a₁b₂ - a₂b₁).

🎙️ Let's apply the cross-multiplication formula to x+y-5=0 and x-y-1=0. Plugging in the coefficients, we get x / ((1)(-1) - (-1)(-5)) = y / ((-5)(1) - (-1)(1)) = 1 / ((1)(-1) - (1)(1)). This simplifies to x / (-1 - 5) = y / (-5 + 1) = 1 / (-1 - 1), so x / -6 = y / -4 = 1 / -2. From this, x = -6 / -2 = 3 and y = -4 / -2 = 2. The solution is (3, 2).

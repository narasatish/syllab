Linear Equation in Two Variables

  • An equation with two variables (e.g., x and y).
  • Highest power of each variable is 1.
  • Standard form: ax + by + c = 0, where a, b, c are real numbers and a ≠ 0, b ≠ 0.

🎙️ A linear equation in two variables is like a balanced scale with two different types of items. Its standard form helps us identify 'a', 'b', and 'c' easily. Remember, 'a' and 'b' cannot be zero simultaneously.

Solutions of One Linear Equation

  • A single linear equation in two variables has infinitely many solutions.
  • Each solution is an ordered pair (x, y).
  • Example: x + y = 5. Solutions: (1,4), (2,3), (0,5), (6,-1), etc.

🎙️ If you only have one linear equation with two variables, there are endless combinations of 'x' and 'y' that can satisfy it. Think of it as many points lying on a single straight line on a graph.

Pair of Linear Equations in Two Variables

  • Two linear equations with the same two variables.
  • They represent two lines on a graph.
  • Standard form: a₁x + b₁y + c₁ = 0, a₂x + b₂y + c₂ = 0.

🎙️ When we have two such equations together, we call them a 'pair'. This pair helps us find a unique solution that satisfies both equations simultaneously, if one exists. This is our main focus today.

Visualizing the Pair: Two Paths

  • A pair of linear equations means we are looking at two lines on the same graph.
  • The solution is where these two lines interact.

🎙️ Imagine two roads on a map. Each road is an equation. Where these roads meet, or how they run next to each other, tells us about the solutions to our pair of equations.

Intersecting Lines: One Unique Solution

  • The two lines cross each other at exactly one point.
  • This point (x, y) is the unique solution to the pair of equations.
  • The system is called 'consistent'.

🎙️ Just like two roads that cross, intersecting lines have one specific point in common. This single point represents the unique 'x' and 'y' values that satisfy both equations.

✖️

Parallel Lines: No Solution

  • The two lines never cross each other, no matter how far they extend.
  • They run side-by-side at a constant distance.
  • There is no common point, so no solution.
  • The system is called 'inconsistent'.

🎙️ Think of railway tracks. They run parallel and never meet. Similarly, if our equations represent parallel lines, there's no point that satisfies both equations simultaneously, meaning no solution.

⏸️

Coincident Lines: Infinitely Many Solutions

  • The two lines lie exactly on top of each other.
  • Every point on one line is also on the other line.
  • They share infinitely many common points, so infinitely many solutions.
  • The system is called 'consistent and dependent'.

🎙️ If two lines are coincident, they are essentially the same line! Every point on one line is a solution for the other, leading to an infinite number of common solutions. They are dependent on each other.

Predicting Solutions from Coefficients

  • Compare ratios of coefficients (a₁/a₂, b₁/b₂, c₁/c₂).
  • Intersecting: a₁/a₂ ≠ b₁/b₂ (Unique solution).
  • Parallel: a₁/a₂ = b₁/b₂ ≠ c₁/c₂ (No solution).
  • Coincident: a₁/a₂ = b₁/b₂ = c₁/c₂ (Infinitely many solutions).

🎙️ This is a super important trick! Without even drawing, you can tell what kind of solution your pair of equations will have just by comparing the ratios of their coefficients. Memorize these conditions!

Solving Graphically: Step-by-Step

  • 1. Find at least two solutions (x, y) for each equation.
  • 2. Plot these points on a graph paper.
  • 3. Draw a straight line passing through the points for each equation.
  • 4. Observe the lines to find the solution.

🎙️ The graphical method is visual and intuitive. For each equation, you only need two points to draw a straight line. The magic happens when you draw both lines on the same graph.

Example: Intersecting Lines (Unique Solution)

  • Equations: x + y = 3 and x - y = 1.
  • Points for x+y=3: (0,3), (3,0).
  • Points for x-y=1: (0,-1), (1,0).

Example: Parallel Lines (No Solution)

  • Equations: x + 2y - 4 = 0 and 2x + 4y - 12 = 0.
  • Ratios: a₁/a₂ = 1/2, b₁/b₂ = 1/2, c₁/c₂ = 1/3.
  • Since a₁/a₂ = b₁/b₂ ≠ c₁/c₂, lines are parallel.
  • No intersection point.

🎙️ Here, if you plot these lines, you'll notice they never meet. The ratio test confirms this: the 'a' and 'b' ratios are equal, but not the 'c' ratio, indicating parallel lines and thus no solution.

Example: Coincident Lines (Infinitely Many Solutions)

  • Equations: x - 2y + 3 = 0 and 3x - 6y + 9 = 0.
  • Ratios: a₁/a₂ = 1/3, b₁/b₂ = 1/3, c₁/c₂ = 1/3.
  • Since a₁/a₂ = b₁/b₂ = c₁/c₂, lines are coincident.
  • Every point on the line is a solution.

🎙️ In this case, one equation is just a multiple of the other. When plotted, they will perfectly overlap. This means every point on that line is a solution, giving us infinitely many solutions.

Beyond Graphs: Algebraic Methods

  • Graphical method can be time-consuming and sometimes inaccurate for exact fractional solutions.
  • Algebraic methods provide precise solutions.
  • Three main methods: Substitution, Elimination, Cross-Multiplication.

🎙️ While graphical methods are great for understanding, they might not always give us perfectly accurate answers, especially with complex numbers. Algebraic methods are our go-to for exact and efficient solutions.

Algebraic Method 1: Substitution

  • 1. Express one variable in terms of the other from one equation.
  • 2. Substitute this expression into the second equation.
  • 3. Solve the resulting linear equation in one variable.
  • 4. Substitute the found value back into the expression from step 1 to find the other variable.

🎙️ The substitution method is like replacing one unknown with its equivalent from another statement. It simplifies a two-variable problem into a one-variable problem, which you already know how to solve.

1Isolate
2Substitute
3Solve
4Find other
22 / 30

Substitution Method: Example

  • Solve: x + y = 5 (1) and 2x - 3y = 4 (2).
  • From (1), x = 5 - y.
  • Substitute into (2): 2(5 - y) - 3y = 4. => 10 - 5y = 4.
  • 5y = 6 => y = 6/5. Substitute y=6/5 into x=5-y => x = 19/5.
  • Solution: (19/5, 6/5).

🎙️ See how we effectively reduced the problem to a single variable? This method is very powerful, especially when one variable can be easily expressed in terms of the other.

Algebraic Method 2: Elimination

  • 1. Multiply one or both equations to make coefficients of one variable numerically equal.
  • 2. Add or subtract the equations to eliminate that variable.
  • 3. Solve the resulting equation in one variable.
  • 4. Substitute the value into either original equation to find the other variable.

🎙️ Elimination is like balancing the scales and then removing a pair of equal weights. We manipulate the equations so that one variable disappears when we combine them, making it easier to solve.

1Equalize coefficients
2Add/Subtract
3Solve one variable
4Find other variable
24 / 30

Elimination Method: Example

  • Solve: 3x + 4y = 10 (1) and 2x - 2y = 2 (2).
  • Multiply (2) by 2: 4x - 4y = 4 (3).
  • Add (1) and (3): 7x = 14 => x = 2.
  • Substitute x=2 into (2): 4 - 2y = 2 => 2y = 2 => y = 1.
  • Solution: (2,1).

🎙️ Notice how multiplying the second equation by 2 made the 'y' coefficients opposites. Then, adding the equations eliminated 'y', leaving us with a simple equation for 'x'.

Algebraic Method 3: Cross-Multiplication

  • For a₁x + b₁y + c₁ = 0 and a₂x + b₂y + c₂ = 0.
  • x / (b₁c₂ - b₂c₁) = y / (c₁a₂ - c₂a₁) = 1 / (a₁b₂ - a₂b₁).
  • This formula directly gives x and y values.
  • Remember the coefficients in order!

🎙️ The cross-multiplication method is a powerful shortcut, especially for competitive exams. It's a direct formula derived from the elimination method. Just be careful with the signs and the order of coefficients!

1Identify coefficients
2Apply formula
3Calculate x, y
27 / 30

Cross-Multiplication Method: Example

  • Solve: 2x + y - 5 = 0 and 3x + 2y - 8 = 0.
  • a₁=2, b₁=1, c₁=-5; a₂=3, b₂=2, c₂=-8.
  • x / (1(-8) - 2(-5)) = y / ((-5)(3) - (-8)(2)) = 1 / (2(2) - 3(1)).
  • x / 2 = y / 1 = 1 / 1 => x = 2, y = 1.
  • Solution: (2,1).

🎙️ As you can see, once you correctly identify the coefficients and plug them into the formula, the solution comes out directly. Practice makes perfect with this method to avoid errors.

Watch Out! Common Mistakes

  • Sign errors, especially with negative numbers.
  • Incorrectly plotting points on the graph.
  • Forgetting to check consistency conditions (a₁/a₂, b₁/b₂, c₁/c₂).
  • Not writing equations in standard form (ax + by + c = 0) before applying formulas.
  • Calculation errors during multiplication/division.

🎙️ It's easy to make small errors in this chapter. Always double-check your signs, especially when moving terms across the equals sign. Practicing carefully will help you avoid these pitfalls.

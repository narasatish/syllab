Euclid's Division Lemma: The Foundation

  • Given positive integers 'a' and 'b'.
  • There exist unique integers 'q' and 'r'.
  • Such that a = bq + r, where 0 ≤ r < b.

🎙️ Euclid's Division Lemma is a fundamental statement about division. It tells us that for any two positive integers, 'a' (dividend) and 'b' (divisor), we can always find a unique quotient 'q' and remainder 'r'. The remainder 'r' will always be less than the divisor 'b' and greater than or equal to zero.

Real-life Use: Sharing Sweets!

  • You have 7 sweets and 3 friends.
  • Each friend gets 2 sweets (q=2).
  • 1 sweet is left over (r=1).

🎙️ Let's apply Euclid's Lemma to a simple scenario. If you have 7 sweets and want to share them equally among 3 friends, each friend gets 2 sweets. You'll be left with 1 sweet, which is less than the number of friends. Here, a=7, b=3, q=2, r=1, fitting the form 7 = 3*2 + 1.

Euclid's Division Algorithm: Finding HCF

  • A technique to compute the Highest Common Factor (HCF).
  • Based on Euclid's Division Lemma.
  • Repeatedly apply the lemma until remainder is zero.

🎙️ Euclid's Division Algorithm is a systematic procedure to find the HCF of two positive integers. It uses the lemma repeatedly. You divide, then use the divisor and remainder for the next step, continuing until the remainder becomes zero. The divisor at that final step is your HCF.

1Apply Lemma (a=bq+r)
2If r=0, b is HCF
3If r≠0, let a=b, b=r
4Repeat until r=0

Worked Example: HCF of 135 and 225

  • Step 1: 225 = 135 × 1 + 90
  • Step 2: 135 = 90 × 1 + 45
  • Step 3: 90 = 45 × 2 + 0

🎙️ Let's find the HCF of 135 and 225 using the algorithm. First, 225 divided by 135 gives a remainder of 90. Next, we divide 135 by 90, getting a remainder of 45. Finally, 90 divided by 45 gives a remainder of 0. The divisor at this stage, 45, is the HCF.

Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic: Prime Factorization

  • Every composite number can be uniquely expressed.
  • As a product of prime numbers.
  • The order of prime factors doesn't matter.

🎙️ This theorem is incredibly powerful! It states that any composite number, no matter how large, can be broken down into a unique set of prime numbers multiplied together. Think of prime numbers as the basic building blocks of all other numbers, and this theorem guarantees their unique composition.

Worked Example: Prime Factorization of 140

  • 140 = 2 × 70
  • 70 = 2 × 35
  • 35 = 5 × 7
  • So, 140 = 2 × 2 × 5 × 7 = 2² × 5 × 7

🎙️ Let's factorize 140. We start by dividing by the smallest prime, 2. We continue until we can no longer divide by 2, then move to the next prime, 5, and finally 7. This gives us the unique prime factorization of 140 as 2 multiplied by itself twice, then multiplied by 5 and by 7.

HCF and LCM: Using Prime Factors

  • HCF: Product of smallest power of common prime factors.
  • LCM: Product of highest power of all prime factors.
  • A systematic way to find HCF and LCM.

🎙️ The Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic makes finding HCF and LCM much simpler. For HCF, you look for the prime factors common to both numbers and take the lowest power of each. For LCM, you consider all prime factors present in either number and take the highest power of each. This method is very efficient.

Worked Example: HCF & LCM of 6 and 20

  • Prime factors of 6 = 2¹ × 3¹
  • Prime factors of 20 = 2² × 5¹
  • HCF(6, 20) = 2¹ = 2
  • LCM(6, 20) = 2² × 3¹ × 5¹ = 4 × 3 × 5 = 60

🎙️ Let's find the HCF and LCM of 6 and 20. First, prime factorize both numbers. For HCF, the only common prime factor is 2, and its lowest power is 2¹. For LCM, we take the highest powers of all prime factors: 2², 3¹, and 5¹. This gives us HCF as 2 and LCM as 60.

Important Relation: Product of Numbers = HCF × LCM

  • For any two positive integers 'a' and 'b'.
  • a × b = HCF(a, b) × LCM(a, b).
  • This relation holds true only for two numbers.

🎙️ This is a very useful relationship! For any two positive integers, the product of the numbers themselves is equal to the product of their HCF and LCM. This formula provides a quick way to find one value if the other three are known. Remember, this specific relation applies only to two numbers.

Proving Irrationality: By Contradiction

  • Assume the number is rational (p/q form).
  • Derive a contradiction to a known fact.
  • Conclude that the initial assumption was false.

🎙️ To prove that a number like √2 is irrational, we use a method called 'proof by contradiction'. We start by assuming the opposite – that √2 is rational. We then follow logical steps, which inevitably lead to a statement that contradicts a basic mathematical truth. This contradiction proves our initial assumption was wrong, thus establishing that √2 must be irrational.

Decimal Expansions: Terminating or Non-Terminating?

  • Rational numbers have decimal expansions that are either terminating or non-terminating repeating.
  • Irrational numbers have non-terminating non-repeating decimal expansions.
  • This helps classify numbers without converting to p/q form.

🎙️ The decimal representation of a number gives us a clear clue about whether it's rational or irrational. Rational numbers will either have decimals that end (like 0.5) or decimals that repeat a pattern (like 0.333...). Irrational numbers, however, have decimals that go on forever without any repeating pattern. This distinction is key.

When do Decimals Terminate?

  • A rational number p/q (in simplest form) has a terminating decimal expansion.
  • If the prime factorization of its denominator 'q' is of the form 2ⁿ5ᵐ.
  • Where 'n' and 'm' are non-negative integers.

🎙️ There's a special condition for when a rational number's decimal expansion will terminate. If you write the rational number as a fraction p/q in its simplest form, and the denominator 'q' only has prime factors of 2 or 5 (or both), then its decimal will terminate. Otherwise, it will be non-terminating and repeating.

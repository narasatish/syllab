Rational Numbers: Fractions and Decimals

🎙️ Rational numbers are numbers that can be expressed as a fraction. This means they can be precisely written as a ratio of two integers. Their decimal forms either stop (terminate) or repeat a pattern.

Euclid's Division Lemma (EDL): A Fundamental Tool

  • Given positive integers 'a' and 'b', there exist unique integers 'q' and 'r'.
  • Such that a = bq + r, where 0 ≤ r < b.
  • It's like the division algorithm we learned in primary school.

🎙️ Euclid's Division Lemma is a foundational concept. It simply states that when you divide one positive integer by another, you always get a unique quotient and a unique remainder that is smaller than the divisor. It's the formal way of writing division.

➗
Dividend = Divisor × Quotient + Remainder

Euclid's Division Algorithm (EDA): Finding HCF

  • A technique to compute the Highest Common Factor (HCF) of two positive integers.
  • Repeatedly apply Euclid's Division Lemma.
  • The HCF is the divisor when the remainder becomes zero.

🎙️ Euclid's Division Algorithm is a step-by-step process that uses the Lemma to find the HCF of two numbers efficiently. You keep dividing the divisor by the remainder until the remainder is zero. The last non-zero divisor is your HCF.

1Apply a = bq + r
2If r = 0, b is HCF
3If r ≠ 0, set a=b, b=r
4Repeat until r = 0
15 / 25

Worked Example: HCF using EDA

  • Find the HCF of 135 and 225.
  • Step 1: 225 = 135 × 1 + 90.
  • Step 2: 135 = 90 × 1 + 45.
  • Step 3: 90 = 45 × 2 + 0. HCF is 45.

🎙️ Let's work through an example. To find the HCF of 135 and 225, we apply the algorithm. We see that 45 is the divisor when the remainder finally becomes zero, so 45 is the HCF.

The Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic (FTA)

  • Every composite number can be expressed (factorized) as a product of primes.
  • This factorization is unique, apart from the order of its prime factors.
  • It's like the 'building blocks' of numbers.

🎙️ This theorem is incredibly powerful! It tells us that every number larger than 1 is either a prime number itself or can be broken down into a unique set of prime numbers multiplied together. Think of prime numbers as the basic atoms of all numbers.

Applications of FTA: HCF and LCM

  • HCF = Product of the smallest power of each common prime factor.
  • LCM = Product of the greatest power of each prime factor involved.
  • For any two positive integers a, b: HCF(a,b) × LCM(a,b) = a × b.

🎙️ The FTA provides a systematic way to find the HCF and LCM of numbers. HCF uses only the common prime factors with their lowest powers, while LCM uses all prime factors with their highest powers. The relationship HCF × LCM = a × b is a very useful property.

Worked Example: HCF/LCM using FTA

  • Find HCF and LCM of 6 and 20.
  • Prime factors: 6 = 2 × 3, 20 = 2² × 5.
  • HCF(6,20) = 2¹ = 2 (common factor, smallest power).
  • LCM(6,20) = 2² × 3¹ × 5¹ = 4 × 3 × 5 = 60 (all factors, greatest power).

🎙️ Let's apply the FTA to find the HCF and LCM of 6 and 20. First, we find their prime factorizations. Then, we identify common factors for HCF and all factors with their highest powers for LCM. Notice that 2 × 60 = 120 and 6 × 20 = 120, verifying the property.

Revisiting Irrational Numbers: Proof by Contradiction

  • Many irrational numbers (like √2, √3, √5) can be proven irrational.
  • We assume the number is rational, then show this leads to a contradiction.
  • This contradiction proves our initial assumption was false.

🎙️ Now we'll formally prove that certain numbers are irrational. The method of 'proof by contradiction' is very powerful in mathematics. We start by assuming the opposite of what we want to prove, and if that assumption leads to a logical inconsistency, then our original statement must be true.

❌
Contradiction

Worked Example: Proving √5 is Irrational

  • Assume √5 is rational, so √5 = a/b (a, b are coprime integers, b≠0).
  • Squaring both sides: 5 = a²/b² => a² = 5b² (a² is divisible by 5).
  • If a² is divisible by 5, then 'a' must be divisible by 5 (a=5c).
  • Substitute a=5c: (5c)² = 5b² => 25c² = 5b² => 5c² = b² (b² is divisible by 5).
  • If b² is divisible by 5, then 'b' must be divisible by 5.
  • Contradiction: 'a' and 'b' are both divisible by 5, but we assumed they were coprime. Thus, √5 is irrational.

🎙️ This proof demonstrates the method. By assuming √5 is rational, we end up concluding that 'a' and 'b' share a common factor (5), which contradicts our initial assumption that they are coprime. Therefore, our initial assumption must be wrong, and √5 is indeed irrational.

Decimal Expansions of Rational Numbers

  • Terminating decimals: The decimal part ends (e.g., 1/2 = 0.5).
  • Non-terminating repeating decimals: The decimal part repeats a pattern (e.g., 1/3 = 0.333...).
  • A rational number p/q has a terminating decimal expansion if the prime factorization of q contains only powers of 2 or 5 (or both).

🎙️ When you convert a rational number to its decimal form, it will either stop after a few digits or continue infinitely but with a repeating block of digits. This property is directly linked to the prime factors of the denominator of the fraction.

Decimal Expansions of Irrational Numbers

  • Non-terminating non-repeating decimals.
  • The decimal part goes on forever without any repeating pattern.
  • Examples: √2 = 1.41421356..., π = 3.14159265...

🎙️ In contrast to rational numbers, irrational numbers have decimal expansions that are truly endless and unpredictable. There's no repeating pattern, making each digit unique in its infinite sequence. This is a defining characteristic of irrational numbers.

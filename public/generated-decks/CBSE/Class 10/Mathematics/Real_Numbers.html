Decimal Expansions of Rational Numbers

  • Terminating decimals: end after finite digits (e.g., 0.5, 0.25).
  • Non-terminating repeating decimals: digits repeat in a pattern (e.g., 0.333..., 0.142857...).

🎙️ When you divide the numerator by the denominator of a rational number, you'll get a decimal. This decimal will either stop (terminate), like 1/2 = 0.5, or it will go on forever with a repeating pattern, like 1/3 = 0.333... These are the two distinct types of decimal expansions for rational numbers.

Euclid's Division Lemma: Sharing Sweets Fairly

  • Imagine you have 10 sweets and 3 friends.
  • How many sweets does each friend get, and how many are left over?
  • 10 = 3 × 3 + 1 (Each gets 3, 1 left).

🎙️ Euclid's Division Lemma is simply a formal way of stating what you do every day when you divide something. It's like sharing sweets: you divide them as evenly as possible, and whatever is left over is the remainder. This simple idea forms the basis of a powerful mathematical tool.

Euclid's Division Lemma (EDL) - The Formal Statement

  • Given positive integers 'a' and 'b'.
  • There exist unique integers 'q' and 'r'.
  • Such that a = bq + r, where 0 ≤ r < b.
  • 'a' is dividend, 'b' is divisor, 'q' is quotient, 'r' is remainder.

🎙️ So, for any two positive integers, 'a' (the number being divided) and 'b' (the number dividing it), we can always find a unique quotient 'q' and a unique remainder 'r'. The remainder 'r' will always be less than the divisor 'b' and greater than or equal to zero. This is the heart of EDL.

Example: Using Euclid's Division Lemma

  • Let a = 17 and b = 5.
  • We divide 17 by 5.
  • 17 = 5 × 3 + 2.
  • Here, q = 3 (quotient) and r = 2 (remainder).
  • Notice 0 ≤ 2 < 5, which satisfies the condition.

🎙️ Let's put EDL into practice. If we take 'a' as 17 and 'b' as 5, when we divide 17 by 5, we get a quotient of 3 and a remainder of 2. This perfectly fits the EDL equation: 17 equals 5 times 3 plus 2. The remainder 2 is indeed between 0 and 5.

2Divide a by b

Euclid's Division Algorithm: Finding HCF

  • A technique to compute the HCF of two positive integers.
  • Repeatedly apply EDL until remainder is zero.
  • The divisor at the last step (when r=0) is the HCF.

🎙️ The beauty of EDL is that it forms the basis for Euclid's Division Algorithm, a powerful method to find the Highest Common Factor (HCF) of two numbers. We keep applying the lemma, making the previous remainder the new divisor, until we get a remainder of zero. The divisor at that final step is our HCF.

Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic (FTA) - The Formal Statement

  • Every composite number can be expressed as a product of primes.
  • This factorization is unique.
  • The order of prime factors doesn't matter (e.g., 2×3 is same as 3×2).

🎙️ So, if you take any composite number, say 12, you can break it down into 2 x 2 x 3. No matter how you try, you'll always end up with two 2s and one 3 as its prime factors. This uniqueness is what makes the theorem so fundamental and powerful in number theory.

Example: Prime Factorization

  • Find the prime factors of 36.
  • 36 = 2 × 18
  • 18 = 2 × 9
  • 9 = 3 × 3
  • So, 36 = 2 × 2 × 3 × 3 = 2² × 3².

🎙️ Let's factorize 36. We start by dividing by the smallest prime, 2. We continue until we can't divide by 2 anymore, then move to the next prime, 3, and so on. This systematic process ensures we find all the prime factors, and we can write them in exponential form for neatness.

1Divide by smallest prime
2Continue until all prime
3Write as product

Application of FTA: HCF and LCM

  • HCF (Highest Common Factor): Product of the smallest power of each common prime factor.
  • LCM (Least Common Multiple): Product of the greatest power of each prime factor involved.
  • For two numbers a, b: HCF(a,b) × LCM(a,b) = a × b.

🎙️ The FTA is super useful for finding HCF and LCM. For HCF, we look at only the prime factors common to both numbers and take the smallest power. For LCM, we consider all prime factors from both numbers and take the greatest power. There's also a handy formula relating HCF, LCM, and the product of the two numbers.

🎙️ Just as there are rational numbers, there are also numbers that cannot be expressed as simple fractions. These are irrational numbers. Their decimal forms go on forever without any repeating pattern. Think of them as numbers that defy neat fractional representation.

Proving Irrationality: The Case of √2

  • We often use 'Proof by Contradiction'.
  • Assume √2 is rational (can be written as p/q in simplest form).
  • Derive a contradiction (e.g., p and q are both even, contradicting simplest form).
  • Therefore, our initial assumption was false, and √2 is irrational.

🎙️ Proving a number is irrational is a common type of problem. We use a method called 'proof by contradiction'. We assume the opposite (that it *is* rational), follow logical steps, and if we reach a statement that is clearly false, then our initial assumption must have been wrong. This proves the number is indeed irrational.

1Assume Rational
2Square Both Sides
3Find Contradiction
4Conclude Irrational

Operations with Irrational Numbers

  • Sum/Difference of a rational and an irrational is irrational (e.g., 2 + √3).
  • Product/Quotient of a non-zero rational and an irrational is irrational (e.g., 5√2).
  • Sum/Difference/Product/Quotient of two irrationals can be rational or irrational (e.g., √2 + (-√2) = 0 (rational), √2 × √2 = 2 (rational)).

🎙️ What happens when you combine rational and irrational numbers? Generally, if you add, subtract, multiply, or divide a rational number (other than zero for multiplication/division) with an irrational number, the result is irrational. However, when you combine two irrational numbers, the outcome can be either rational or irrational, depending on the specific numbers.

Decimal Expansions & Denominators (Theorem)

  • A rational number x = p/q (q ≠ 0) has a terminating decimal expansion.
  • IF and only IF the prime factorization of q is of the form 2ⁿ5ᵐ, where n and m are non-negative integers.
  • Otherwise, it has a non-terminating repeating decimal expansion.

🎙️ There's a cool trick to know if a rational number will have a terminating decimal without actually dividing! Just look at the prime factors of its denominator (q). If q only has prime factors of 2 or 5 (or both), then the decimal will terminate. If it has any other prime factor, it will be non-terminating and repeating.

Common Mistakes to Avoid!

  • Confusing HCF and LCM applications.
  • Incorrectly identifying 'q' in Euclid's Division Lemma.
  • Assuming √9 is irrational (it's 3, which is rational!).
  • Not writing prime factors in ascending order for consistency.

🎙️ Even experienced students make these simple errors. Always double-check your HCF/LCM calculations. Remember that √9 is a perfect square. And when proving irrationality, ensure your steps lead to a clear contradiction. Practice makes perfect in avoiding these pitfalls.

What is an Equation?

  • A mathematical statement showing two expressions are equal.
  • Contains an equals sign (=).
  • Often involves variables (like x, y).

🎙️ Think of an equation like a balanced scale. Whatever is on one side must be exactly equal to what's on the other. It's a way to find unknown values, represented by variables.

Linear Equations: A Quick Recap

  • Standard form: ax + b = 0 (where a ≠ 0).

🎙️ You've already studied linear equations in earlier classes. Remember, 'linear' means it forms a straight line when graphed, and the variable 'x' has a power of 1.

What is a Quadratic Polynomial?

  • A polynomial with degree 2.
  • Example: x² + 5x + 6.
  • The term 'quad' means square, referring to the x² term.

🎙️ A quadratic polynomial is special because its highest power is 2. The word 'quadratic' comes from the Latin word 'quadratus' meaning square, which refers to the 'x-squared' term.

Defining a Quadratic Equation

  • When a quadratic polynomial is set to zero.
  • Standard form: ax² + bx + c = 0.
  • Here, a, b, c are real numbers, and a ≠ 0.

🎙️ So, when we take a quadratic polynomial and make it equal to zero, it becomes a quadratic equation. The 'a' cannot be zero, otherwise, the x² term vanishes, and it becomes a linear equation!

📝
Standard Form

Real-Life Example: Area of a Plot

  • A rectangular plot has an area of 300 m².
  • Its length is 1 meter more than twice its breadth.
  • How can we find the dimensions?

🎙️ Imagine you want to buy a plot of land. If we let the breadth be 'x' meters, then the length would be (2x+1) meters. Since Area = Length × Breadth, we get x(2x+1) = 300. This simplifies to 2x² + x - 300 = 0, which is a quadratic equation!

Checking if an Equation is Quadratic: Example 1

  • Is (x+1)² = 2(x-3) a quadratic equation?
  • Expand both sides of the equation.
  • Simplify and check the highest power of x.

🎙️ Let's try an example. First, expand the left side using the (a+b)² formula. Then, distribute on the right side. Bring all terms to one side and simplify. We see the highest power of 'x' is 2, so yes, it is a quadratic equation.

1(x+1)² = x² + 2x + 1
22(x-3) = 2x - 6
3x² + 2x + 1 = 2x - 6
4x² + 7 = 0

Checking if an Equation is Quadratic: Example 2

  • Is x(x+1) + 8 = (x+2)(x-2) a quadratic equation?
  • Expand and simplify both sides.
  • Look for the x² term.

🎙️ For this example, expand x(x+1) and use the (a+b)(a-b) identity for the right side. When we bring all terms to one side, the x² terms cancel out. This leaves us with a linear equation, not a quadratic one.

1LHS: x² + x + 8
2RHS: x² - 4 (using a²-b²)
3x² + x + 8 = x² - 4
4x + 12 = 0

Roots of a Quadratic Equation

  • The values of the variable that satisfy the equation.
  • Also called 'solutions' or 'zeros'.
  • A quadratic equation has at most two roots.

🎙️ The roots are the specific numbers you can substitute for 'x' that make the equation true. Since the highest power is 2, a quadratic equation can have up to two distinct roots, or sometimes one repeated root, or no real roots.

Method 1: Finding Roots by Factorisation

  • Express the quadratic polynomial as a product of two linear factors.
  • Set each factor to zero to find the roots.
  • Relies on the 'zero product rule'.

🎙️ Factorisation is a common method. We break down the quadratic expression into two simpler linear expressions multiplied together. If their product is zero, then at least one of them must be zero, which helps us find the roots.

✂️
Breaking Apart
13 / 25

Worked Example 1 (Factorisation)

  • Solve: x² - 3x - 10 = 0
  • Find two numbers that multiply to -10 and add to -3.
  • Split the middle term and factor by grouping.

🎙️ Here, the two numbers are -5 and 2. We rewrite the middle term (-3x) as (-5x + 2x). Then, we group the terms and factor out common parts. Finally, we set each factor to zero to get our two roots.

1x² - 5x + 2x - 10 = 0
2x(x - 5) + 2(x - 5) = 0
3(x - 5)(x + 2) = 0
4x - 5 = 0 or x + 2 = 0
5x = 5 or x = -2
14 / 25

Worked Example 2 (Factorisation)

  • Solve: 2x² + x - 6 = 0
  • Multiply 'a' and 'c' (2 * -6 = -12).
  • Find two numbers that multiply to -12 and add to 1.

🎙️ In this example, the numbers are 4 and -3. We split the middle term 'x' into '4x - 3x'. Then, we group and factor. This method is called 'splitting the middle term' and is very effective for factorisation.

12x² + 4x - 3x - 6 = 0
22x(x + 2) - 3(x + 2) = 0
3(2x - 3)(x + 2) = 0
42x - 3 = 0 or x + 2 = 0
5x = 3/2 or x = -2

Method 2: Completing the Square

  • Transform the equation into the form (x + k)² = d.
  • Makes one side a perfect square trinomial.
  • Allows finding roots by taking square roots.

🎙️ This method is a bit more involved but very powerful. We manipulate the equation to create a perfect square on one side, which then allows us to easily isolate 'x' by taking the square root of both sides. It's like building a perfect square visually!

📐
Perfect Square
18 / 25

Worked Example (Completing the Square)

  • Solve: 2x² - 7x + 3 = 0
  • Divide by 'a' (2) to make x² coefficient 1.
  • Add (b/2)² to both sides to complete the square.

🎙️ First, divide the entire equation by 2. Then, move the constant term to the right. To complete the square, we add (half of the coefficient of x, squared) to both sides. This creates a perfect square trinomial on the left. Then, take the square root of both sides and solve for x.

1x² - (7/2)x + 3/2 = 0
2x² - (7/2)x = -3/2
3x² - (7/2)x + (7/4)² = -3/2 + (7/4)²
4(x - 7/4)² = -3/2 + 49/16 = 25/16
5x - 7/4 = ±5/4 => x = 12/4 or 2/4
6x = 3 or x = 1/2

Method 3: The Quadratic Formula

  • A universal formula to find roots of ax² + bx + c = 0.
  • Roots are given by: x = [-b ± √(b² - 4ac)] / 2a.
  • Works for all quadratic equations.

🎙️ This is perhaps the most famous and powerful method. The quadratic formula is derived by applying the method of completing the square to the general form ax² + bx + c = 0. It's a direct way to find the roots, no matter how complex the numbers are.

➗
Universal Solution

Worked Example (Quadratic Formula)

  • Solve: 2x² + x - 6 = 0
  • Identify a, b, and c.
  • Substitute values into the quadratic formula.

🎙️ Here, a=2, b=1, and c=-6. Carefully substitute these values into the formula. Pay close attention to the signs, especially inside the square root. Calculate the term under the square root first, then simplify to find the two roots.

1a=2, b=1, c=-6
2x = [-1 ± √(1² - 4(2)(-6))] / (2*2)
3x = [-1 ± √(1 + 48)] / 4
4x = [-1 ± √49] / 4
5x = [-1 ± 7] / 4
6x = 6/4 or x = -8/4
7x = 3/2 or x = -2

Nature of Roots: The Discriminant

  • The expression b² - 4ac is called the discriminant (D).
  • It determines the nature of the roots.
  • No need to solve the equation completely.

🎙️ The part under the square root in the quadratic formula, b² - 4ac, is very important. We call it the discriminant because it 'discriminates' or tells us about the type of roots without actually solving for them.

🤔
Deciding Factor
22 / 25

Cases of the Discriminant (D)

  • D > 0: Two distinct real roots.
  • D = 0: Two equal real roots (one repeated root).
  • D < 0: No real roots (roots are imaginary).

🎙️ If D is positive, you get two different real numbers as roots. If D is exactly zero, the ± part vanishes, giving one repeated real root. If D is negative, you cannot take the square root of a negative number in real numbers, so there are no real roots.

D = 0
D < 0

Real-Life Example: Ball Thrown Upwards

  • Height of a ball: h = 5t - t² (t is time).
  • When does the ball hit the ground (h=0)?
  • 0 = 5t - t² => t² - 5t = 0.

🎙️ Quadratic equations model projectile motion. If we set the height 'h' to zero, we are looking for the time 't' when the ball is on the ground. This gives us a quadratic equation t(t-5) = 0, which means t=0 (start) or t=5 (lands after 5 seconds).

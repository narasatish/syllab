What is Coordinate Geometry?

  • A branch of mathematics that uses coordinates to study geometric figures.
  • Connects algebra and geometry.
  • Helps locate points, lines, and shapes in a plane.

🎙️ Coordinate geometry is essentially a bridge between algebra and geometry. Instead of just drawing shapes, we use numbers, called coordinates, to define their exact positions and properties. This powerful tool allows us to solve geometric problems using algebraic methods, making complex ideas much clearer.

The Cartesian System: X and Y Axes

  • A plane defined by two perpendicular lines: X-axis and Y-axis.
  • They intersect at the origin (0,0).
  • Horizontal line is X-axis, vertical line is Y-axis.

🎙️ The foundation of coordinate geometry is the Cartesian system, named after René Descartes. It's simply a grid formed by two number lines crossing each other at a right angle. The horizontal line is our X-axis, and the vertical line is our Y-axis. Their meeting point is very special, called the origin.

Plotting Points: Ordered Pairs (x,y)

  • Every point in the plane is represented by an ordered pair (x,y).
  • 'x' is the x-coordinate (abscissa), distance from Y-axis.
  • 'y' is the y-coordinate (ordinate), distance from X-axis.

🎙️ To locate any point in this plane, we use an ordered pair of numbers, (x,y). The first number, 'x', tells us how far left or right to move from the origin along the X-axis. The second number, 'y', tells us how far up or down to move along the Y-axis. Just like giving directions, the order matters!

Quadrants: Dividing the Plane

  • The X and Y axes divide the plane into four regions.
  • These regions are called Quadrants I, II, III, and IV.
  • Signs of coordinates change in each quadrant.

🎙️ When the X and Y axes intersect, they create four distinct sections, much like dividing a pie into four slices. We call these quadrants, and they are numbered counter-clockwise starting from the top-right. The signs of the x and y coordinates tell us exactly which quadrant a point lies in.

Deriving the Distance Formula

  • Consider two points P(x1, y1) and Q(x2, y2).
  • Form a right-angled triangle using these points and a horizontal/vertical line.
  • Apply Pythagoras Theorem: (Hypotenuse)² = (Base)² + (Height)².

🎙️ The distance formula isn't just a magic trick; it comes directly from a concept you already know: the Pythagorean theorem! By drawing a right-angled triangle with the line segment connecting our two points as the hypotenuse, we can easily see how the differences in x-coordinates and y-coordinates form the legs of the triangle. This makes the formula very intuitive.

Distance Formula: Worked Example 1

  • Find the distance between A(2,3) and B(5,7).

🎙️ Let's put the distance formula into practice with an example. We have two points, A at (2,3) and B at (5,7). First, we identify our x1, y1, x2, and y2 values. Then, we carefully substitute these into the formula and perform the calculations step-by-step. As you can see, the distance between A and B is exactly 5 units.

1Identify (x1,y1) and (x2,y2)
2Apply formula: √[(x2-x1)² + (y2-y1)²]
3Substitute values: √[(5-2)² + (7-3)²]
4Calculate: √[3² + 4²] = √[9+16] = √25
5Result: 5 units

Section Formula: Internal Division

  • Point P(x,y) divides line segment A(x1,y1) and B(x2,y2).
  • Divides internally in the ratio m1:m2.
  • Formula: x = (m1x2 + m2x1) / (m1 + m2), y = (m1y2 + m2y1) / (m1 + m2).

🎙️ When a point divides a line segment internally, it means the point lies *between* the two endpoints. The ratio m1:m2 tells us how it divides the segment. The formula might look a bit complex at first, but it's logical: it's a weighted average of the x-coordinates and y-coordinates, based on the given ratio. Practice makes it easy to remember and apply.

Section Formula: Worked Example 1

  • Find the coordinates of the point which divides the line segment joining (4,-3) and (9,7) in the ratio 3:2 internally.

🎙️ Let's solve a problem using the Section Formula. We have two points and a ratio of 3:2. First, carefully identify all the values for x1, y1, x2, y2, m1, and m2. Then, substitute these values into the x-coordinate formula and the y-coordinate formula separately. Calculate each part, and you'll find the coordinates of the dividing point.

1Identify (x1,y1), (x2,y2), m1, m2
2Apply x = (m1x2 + m2x1) / (m1 + m2)
3x = (3*9 + 2*4) / (3+2) = (27+8)/5 = 35/5 = 7
4Apply y = (m1y2 + m2y1) / (m1 + m2)
5y = (3*7 + 2*-3) / (3+2) = (21-6)/5 = 15/5 = 3
6Result: Point is (7,3)

Midpoint Formula: A Special Case

  • The midpoint divides a line segment into two equal parts.
  • It's a special case of the section formula where m1:m2 is 1:1.
  • Formula: x = (x1 + x2) / 2, y = (y1 + y2) / 2.

🎙️ The midpoint is a very common point we need to find. It's simply the point exactly in the middle of a line segment. This is a special, simpler version of the section formula where the ratio is 1:1. You just average the x-coordinates and average the y-coordinates. It's super handy for many problems!

Area of a Triangle Formula

  • For a triangle with vertices (x1,y1), (x2,y2), (x3,y3).
  • Area = 1/2 |x1(y2 - y3) + x2(y3 - y1) + x3(y1 - y2)|
  • The absolute value ensures a positive area.

🎙️ This is the formula for finding the area of a triangle when you know the coordinates of its three vertices. It looks a bit long, but there's a pattern to it: x1 times the difference of y2 and y3, plus x2 times the difference of y3 and y1, and so on. Remember to take the absolute value at the end, as area must always be positive.

Area of a Triangle: Worked Example 1

  • Find the area of a triangle with vertices A(1,1), B(4,5), C(7,2).

🎙️ Let's apply the area formula to a triangle with given vertices. First, assign the coordinates to x1, y1, and so on. Then, carefully substitute these values into the formula. Pay close attention to the signs, especially when subtracting. After simplifying, remember to take the absolute value and then multiply by half to get the final area.

1Identify (x1,y1), (x2,y2), (x3,y3)
2Apply formula: 1/2 |x1(y2-y3) + x2(y3-y1) + x3(y1-y2)|
3Substitute: 1/2 |1(5-2) + 4(2-1) + 7(1-5)|
4Calculate: 1/2 |1(3) + 4(1) + 7(-4)|
5Simplify: 1/2 |3 + 4 - 28| = 1/2 |-21|
6Result: 10.5 square units

Collinearity: Are Points on a Line?

  • Three or more points are collinear if they lie on the same straight line.
  • If points are collinear, the area of the triangle formed by them is zero.
  • This is a key test for collinearity in coordinate geometry.

🎙️ An important application of the area of a triangle formula is to check for collinearity. If three points lie on the same straight line, they cannot form a triangle with any area. Therefore, if the area calculated using the formula turns out to be zero, it confirms that the three points are indeed collinear. This is a very elegant way to test if points are aligned.

Recap: Key Formulas

  • Distance Formula: √[(x2-x1)² + (y2-y1)²]
  • Section Formula: x=(m1x2+m2x1)/(m1+m2), y=(m1y2+m2y1)/(m1+m2)
  • Midpoint Formula: x=(x1+x2)/2, y=(y1+y2)/2
  • Area of Triangle: 1/2 |x1(y2-y3) + x2(y3-y1) + x3(y1-y2)|

🎙️ Let's quickly review the main formulas we've learned today. The Distance Formula helps us measure lengths. The Section Formula allows us to find a point that divides a segment in a certain ratio, with the Midpoint Formula being a special case. And finally, the Area of a Triangle formula helps us calculate the space enclosed by three points. These are your essential tools for Coordinate Geometry.

Plotting Points: (x, y) Coordinates

  • Every point is described by an ordered pair (x, y).
  • 'x' tells us how far horizontally from the origin.
  • 'y' tells us how far vertically from the origin.

🎙️ Think of it like giving directions: 'Go 3 steps right, then 2 steps up.' That's (3, 2)! The 'x' coordinate always comes first, then the 'y'.

  • Numbered I, II, III, IV in an anti-clockwise direction.

🎙️ Each quadrant has a special combination of positive and negative x and y coordinates. It's important to know which quadrant a point lies in.

The Secret Behind the Distance Formula

  • We can form a right-angled triangle between two points.
  • The distance is the hypotenuse of this triangle.
  • Pythagoras Theorem: a² + b² = c².

🎙️ By drawing horizontal and vertical lines from our two points, we create a perfect right triangle, and then Pythagoras comes to our rescue!

📐
Right Triangle
13 / 30

The Distance Formula Revealed!

  • For points A(x1, y1) and B(x2, y2):
  • Distance AB = √((x2 - x1)² + (y2 - y1)²)

🎙️ This formula looks a bit long, but it's just an application of Pythagoras theorem. Remember to square the differences and then take the square root of their sum.

Example: Finding Distance Between Two Points

  • Find the distance between P(2, 3) and Q(5, 7).
  • Step 1: Identify (x1, y1) and (x2, y2).
  • Step 2: Substitute values into the formula.
  • Step 3: Calculate and simplify.

🎙️ Here, x1=2, y1=3, x2=5, y2=7. So, (5-2)² + (7-3)² = 3² + 4² = 9 + 16 = 25. The distance is √25 = 5 units.

Special Cases: Distance from Origin/Axes

  • Distance from Origin (0,0) to (x,y): √(x² + y²)
  • Distance of (x,y) from X-axis: |y|
  • Distance of (x,y) from Y-axis: |x|

🎙️ These are shortcuts! If one of the points is the origin, the formula simplifies. And distance from an axis is simply the absolute value of the other coordinate.

Dividing a Line Segment: The Section Formula

  • Imagine cutting a cake into specific ratios.
  • The Section Formula helps find the point that divides a line segment in a given ratio.

🎙️ This is useful when you know the two endpoints of a line and you want to find a point that lies *on* that line, dividing it into two parts with a specific ratio.

✂️
Cutting a Line
19 / 30

Internal Division: Point Inside

  • Point P(x, y) divides the line segment joining A(x1, y1) and B(x2, y2) internally in the ratio m1:m2.
  • P(x,y) = ((m1x2 + m2x1)/(m1+m2), (m1y2 + m2y1)/(m1+m2))

🎙️ The ratio m1:m2 means that the distance AP is to PB as m1 is to m2. Be careful with the subscripts, m1 multiplies x2 and y2, and m2 multiplies x1 and y1.

Midpoint Formula: Exactly Halfway!

  • The midpoint is a special case of the Section Formula where the ratio is 1:1.
  • Midpoint M(x, y) = ((x1+x2)/2, (y1+y2)/2)

🎙️ When m1 = m2 = 1, the formula simplifies to just averaging the x-coordinates and averaging the y-coordinates. It's the easiest way to find the center of a segment.

The Area of a Triangle Formula

  • For a triangle with vertices (x1, y1), (x2, y2), (x3, y3):
  • Area = 1/2 |x1(y2 - y3) + x2(y3 - y1) + x3(y1 - y2)|
  • The absolute value ensures area is always positive.

🎙️ This formula might look complex, but it follows a cyclic pattern (1,2,3 then 2,3,1 then 3,1,2). Remember to take the absolute value at the end.

Collinear Points Revisited (Area Method)

  • If three points are collinear (lie on the same straight line), they cannot form a triangle.
  • Therefore, the area of the triangle formed by collinear points is 0.

🎙️ This is a powerful alternative to the distance formula for checking collinearity. If your area calculation results in zero, the points are collinear!

Common Mistakes: Learn from Others!

  • Sign errors (especially with negative coordinates).
  • Mixing up (x1, y1) and (x2, y2) in formulas.
  • Forgetting to take square root in distance formula.
  • Incorrectly applying ratios in section formula (m1x2 vs m2x1).
  • Calculation errors in complex formulas.

🎙️ These are the usual traps students fall into. Always write down the formula first, substitute carefully, and double-check your calculations. Practice makes perfect!

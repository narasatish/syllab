What Exactly is Probability?

  • It's a measure of how likely an event is to occur.
  • Expressed as a number between 0 and 1.
  • 0 means impossible, 1 means certain.

🎙️ Think of probability as a way to quantify uncertainty. When we say there's a 'high chance' or 'low chance' of something, probability gives us a precise number for that feeling. It helps us make informed decisions.

Key Terms: Experiment & Outcome

  • An 'Experiment' is an action with well-defined results.
  • Example: Tossing a coin, rolling a die.
  • An 'Outcome' is a possible result of an experiment.
  • Example: Getting a 'Head' when tossing a coin.

🎙️ Before we dive into calculations, let's understand some basic vocabulary. An experiment is simply any activity we perform, and an outcome is just one of the many ways that experiment can turn out.

Key Terms: Sample Space

  • The 'Sample Space' (S) is the set of all possible outcomes of an experiment.
  • Example: Tossing a coin, S = {Head, Tail}.
  • Example: Rolling a die, S = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}.

🎙️ The sample space is like a complete list of everything that can possibly happen in an experiment. It's important to identify all outcomes correctly, as this forms the base for our probability calculations.

Key Terms: Event

  • An 'Event' (E) is one or more outcomes of an experiment.
  • It is a subset of the sample space.
  • Example: Getting a 'Head' in a coin toss.
  • Example: Getting an 'even number' when rolling a die: {2, 4, 6}.

🎙️ An event is what we are specifically interested in. It could be a single outcome, like rolling a '3', or a group of outcomes, like rolling an 'odd number'. We calculate the probability of an event.

The Probability Formula

  • Probability of an Event E, denoted as P(E).
  • P(E) = (Number of Favorable Outcomes) / (Total Number of Possible Outcomes).
  • This formula is the heart of probability calculations.

🎙️ This is the most important formula you'll learn today! To find the probability of any event, you just need to count how many ways that event can happen and divide it by the total number of ways the experiment can turn out.

➗
Favorable / Total

Example: Tossing a Coin

  • Experiment: Tossing a coin.
  • Sample Space (S) = {Head, Tail}. Total outcomes = 2.
  • Event (E): Getting a Head. Favorable outcomes = 1 (Head).
  • P(Head) = 1/2.

🎙️ Let's apply our formula to a simple coin toss. There are two equally likely outcomes: Heads or Tails. If we want to find the probability of getting a Head, there's only one favorable outcome out of two total possibilities, so it's 1/2.

Example: Rolling a Die

  • Experiment: Rolling a standard six-sided die.
  • Sample Space (S) = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}. Total outcomes = 6.
  • Event (E): Getting a '4'. Favorable outcomes = 1 (the number 4).
  • P(Getting a 4) = 1/6.

🎙️ Similarly, when you roll a die, there are six possible outcomes. If you want to know the probability of rolling a '4', there's only one '4' on the die, so it's 1 out of 6. This applies to any specific number you want to roll.

Range of Probability

  • The probability of any event E is always between 0 and 1, inclusive.
  • 0 ≤ P(E) ≤ 1.
  • A probability of 0 means the event is impossible.
  • A probability of 1 means the event is certain to happen.

🎙️ It's crucial to remember that a probability value can never be negative or greater than 1. If your calculation gives you a value outside this range, you know there's been a mistake. This range helps us verify our answers.

Impossible Event

  • An event that cannot happen has a probability of 0.
  • Number of favorable outcomes = 0.
  • Example: Rolling a '7' on a standard six-sided die.
  • P(Getting a 7) = 0/6 = 0.

🎙️ An impossible event is something that simply cannot occur under the given conditions. For instance, you can't roll a '7' on a die that only has numbers 1 through 6. The number of favorable outcomes is zero.

🚫

Sure Event (Certain Event)

  • An event that is certain to happen has a probability of 1.
  • Number of favorable outcomes = Total number of outcomes.
  • Example: Getting a number less than '7' on a standard die roll.
  • P(Number < 7) = 6/6 = 1.

🎙️ Conversely, a sure event is something that will definitely happen. If you roll a die, you are guaranteed to get a number less than 7. In this case, all outcomes are favorable, so the probability is 1.

Complementary Events

  • If E is an event, then 'not E' (or E') is its complementary event.
  • It means the event E does NOT occur.
  • The sum of probabilities of an event and its complement is always 1.
  • P(E) + P(not E) = 1.

🎙️ Complementary events are like two sides of a coin: either an event happens, or it doesn't. If you know the probability of an event, you can easily find the probability of it not happening by subtracting from 1. This is a very useful property.

↔️
Opposite events
14 / 25

Example: Complementary Event

  • Experiment: Rolling a die.
  • Event (E): Getting a '4'. P(E) = 1/6.
  • Complementary Event (not E): Not getting a '4'.
  • P(not E) = 1 - P(E) = 1 - 1/6 = 5/6.

🎙️ Let's use our die example again. We know the probability of getting a '4' is 1/6. So, the probability of NOT getting a '4' would be 1 minus 1/6, which is 5/6. This means any other number (1, 2, 3, 5, 6) could be rolled.

Types of Events: Elementary Event

  • An 'Elementary Event' is an event with only one possible outcome.
  • Example: Getting a 'Head' when tossing a coin.
  • Example: Getting a '3' when rolling a die.

🎙️ An elementary event is the simplest kind of event, representing just one specific result from our experiment. Each individual outcome in the sample space is an elementary event.

Types of Events: Compound Event

  • A 'Compound Event' is an event with more than one possible outcome.
  • Example: Getting an 'even number' when rolling a die {2, 4, 6}.
  • Example: Getting 'at least one Head' when tossing two coins {HT, TH, HH}.

🎙️ A compound event is made up of two or more elementary events. When we talk about rolling an even number, we're considering the outcomes 2, 4, and 6 together as a single event.

Worked Example: Marbles in a Bag

  • A bag contains 3 red marbles and 5 blue marbles.
  • Total marbles = 3 + 5 = 8.
  • P(drawing a red marble) = 3/8.
  • P(drawing a blue marble) = 5/8.

🎙️ Let's solve a practical problem. Imagine a bag with different colored marbles. To find the probability of drawing a red marble, we count the red marbles and divide by the total number of marbles in the bag. The same logic applies to blue marbles.

Worked Example: Playing Cards

  • A standard deck has 52 playing cards.
  • There are 26 red cards and 26 black cards.
  • P(drawing a red card) = 26/52 = 1/2.
  • P(drawing an Ace) = 4/52 = 1/13.

🎙️ Playing cards are a classic example in probability. A standard deck has 52 cards, divided equally into red and black. Knowing the composition of the deck helps us calculate probabilities for drawing specific cards or types of cards.

What is Light?

  • Form of energy that enables us to see.
  • Travels in straight lines (rectilinear propagation).
  • Doesn't need a medium to travel (can travel through vacuum).

🎙️ Light is a fundamental part of our daily lives, allowing us to perceive the world. It's a form of energy that moves incredibly fast, always in straight paths, and can even travel through empty space.

  • Responsible for us seeing objects.

🎙️ Imagine throwing a ball at a wall – it bounces back, right? Light does something similar when it hits a surface. This phenomenon is called reflection, and it's how we see everything that doesn't produce its own light.

Laws of Reflection

  • Angle of incidence (i) equals angle of reflection (r).
  • Incident ray, reflected ray, and normal lie in the same plane.
  • These laws apply to all types of reflecting surfaces.

🎙️ There are two simple rules that light always follows when it reflects. The first is that the angle at which light hits a surface is always equal to the angle at which it bounces off. The second rule tells us that all the relevant rays and the normal stay in the same flat plane.

Reflection in Everyday Life

  • Mirrors use reflection to show our image.
  • Smooth, polished surfaces reflect light best.
  • Even rough surfaces reflect, but scatter light (diffuse reflection).

🎙️ The most common example of reflection is a mirror. When you look into a mirror, the light from your face reflects off its smooth surface and enters your eyes, creating your image. This is also why we can see objects around us – light reflects off them and comes to our eyes.

Types of Mirrors

  • Plane Mirrors: Flat reflecting surface.
  • Spherical Mirrors: Curved reflecting surface.
  • Spherical mirrors can be concave or convex.

🎙️ Mirrors aren't all the same! We primarily categorize them into two types: plane mirrors, which are flat, and spherical mirrors, which have a curved surface. Spherical mirrors are further divided based on whether their inner or outer surface reflects light.

Images Formed by Plane Mirrors

  • Image is always virtual and erect.
  • Image is same size as object.
  • Image is laterally inverted (left appears right).
  • Image distance equals object distance.

🎙️ When you look into a plane mirror, you see an image that seems to be behind the mirror (virtual), upright (erect), and exactly your size. However, notice that your left hand appears as your right hand – that's called lateral inversion! The image also appears as far behind the mirror as you are in front of it.

Spherical Mirrors: Concave & Convex

  • Concave Mirror: Reflecting surface curves inwards (like a spoon's inner side).
  • Convex Mirror: Reflecting surface curves outwards (like a spoon's outer side).
  • Used in many applications, from torches to vehicle rearview mirrors.

🎙️ Spherical mirrors are curved. A concave mirror focuses light inwards, making it useful for things like flashlights. A convex mirror spreads light outwards, which is why it's used in vehicle rearview mirrors to give a wider view.

Important Terms for Spherical Mirrors

  • Pole (P): Center of the reflecting surface.
  • Centre of Curvature (C): Center of the sphere from which the mirror is cut.
  • Principal Focus (F): Point where parallel rays converge (concave) or appear to diverge (convex).
  • Radius of Curvature (R) & Focal Length (f): R = 2f.

🎙️ To understand how spherical mirrors work, we need to know some key points. The pole is the mirror's center. The center of curvature is like the center of the original sphere. The principal focus is a very important point where light rays meet or appear to meet after reflection.

Refraction of Light

  • Bending of light as it passes from one medium to another.
  • Caused by change in speed of light in different media.
  • Example: A spoon appearing bent in a glass of water.

🎙️ Now, let's explore another fascinating phenomenon: refraction. This is when light doesn't bounce back, but rather passes through a substance and bends. This bending happens because light changes its speed as it moves from one medium, like air, to another, like water.

Laws of Refraction (Snell's Law)

  • Incident ray, refracted ray, and normal lie in the same plane.
  • The ratio of sine of angle of incidence to sine of angle of refraction is constant (Snell's Law).
  • This constant is called the refractive index.

🎙️ Just like reflection, refraction also follows specific laws. The first law is similar, stating that the rays and normal are in the same plane. The second, known as Snell's Law, describes the mathematical relationship between the angles of incidence and refraction, which is always constant for a given pair of media.

Refractive Index

  • Measure of how much light bends when entering a medium.
  • Ratio of speed of light in vacuum to speed of light in the medium.
  • Higher refractive index means light bends more, and speed is slower.
  • No units (dimensionless quantity).

🎙️ The refractive index tells us how 'optically dense' a material is. A higher refractive index means light slows down more and bends more when it enters that material. For example, light travels slower and bends more in glass than in water.

Refraction in Everyday Life

  • A pencil partially immersed in water appears bent.
  • The bottom of a swimming pool appears shallower.
  • Twinkling of stars is due to atmospheric refraction.

🎙️ You've probably seen a pencil in a glass of water look broken or bent – that's refraction! The light rays coming from the submerged part of the pencil bend as they leave the water and enter the air, making it appear in a different position to our eyes. This phenomenon explains many everyday optical illusions.

Lenses: Convex and Concave

  • Lenses are transparent materials that refract light.
  • Convex Lens: Thicker in the middle, converges light (converging lens).
  • Concave Lens: Thinner in the middle, diverges light (diverging lens).

🎙️ Just as mirrors reflect, lenses refract. Lenses are transparent pieces of glass or plastic that are shaped to bend light in specific ways. Convex lenses bring light rays together, while concave lenses spread them out, which is why they are used in spectacles and many optical instruments.

👓👓

Important Terms for Lenses

  • Optical Centre (O): Central point of the lens.
  • Principal Focus (F): Point where parallel rays converge (convex) or appear to diverge (concave) after refraction.
  • Focal Length (f): Distance from optical centre to principal focus.
  • Two principal foci (F1 and F2) on either side of the lens.

🎙️ Similar to mirrors, lenses also have important reference points. The optical center is the lens's geometric center. The principal focus is where parallel light rays meet or appear to meet after passing through the lens. Lenses have two principal foci, one on each side.

Power of a Lens

  • Measure of the converging or diverging ability of a lens.
  • Defined as the reciprocal of its focal length (P = 1/f).
  • Unit is Dioptre (D), where 1D = 1m⁻¹.
  • Convex lens has positive power, concave lens has negative power.

🎙️ The power of a lens tells us how strongly it can converge or diverge light. It's simply the inverse of the focal length, measured in dioptres. A lens with a shorter focal length has greater power. Convex lenses have positive power, and concave lenses have negative power, which is important for correcting vision.

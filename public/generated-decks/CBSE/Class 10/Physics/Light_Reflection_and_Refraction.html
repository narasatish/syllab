Laws Governing Reflection

  • 1. The angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection.
  • 2. The incident ray, reflected ray, and normal all lie in the same plane.

🎙️ These two laws are fundamental to understanding how mirrors work. The angle at which light strikes a surface is exactly the same as the angle at which it leaves.

Regular vs. Diffuse Reflection

  • Regular reflection: from smooth surfaces (e.g., mirror), forms clear images.
  • Diffuse reflection: from rough surfaces (e.g., wall), scatters light, no clear image.

🎙️ Imagine looking at your face in a shiny new steel plate versus an old, dented one. The smooth plate gives a clear image (regular), while the rough one doesn't (diffuse).

Image Formation by Plane Mirrors

  • Plane mirrors form virtual, erect, and laterally inverted images.
  • The image distance equals the object distance.
  • The image is formed behind the mirror.

🎙️ When you stand in front of a plane mirror, your right hand appears as your left hand in the reflection. This 'left-right' reversal is called lateral inversion.

↔️
Lateral Inversion

Characteristics of Images from Plane Mirrors

  • Virtual: Cannot be obtained on a screen.
  • Erect: Upright, not inverted.
  • Same size as the object.
  • Laterally inverted.
  • Same distance behind the mirror as the object is in front.

🎙️ Remember, the image you see in a bathroom mirror is always upright and the same size as you, but it's not a 'real' image you can project onto a screen.

  • They are part of a hollow sphere.
  • They can be concave or convex.

🎙️ Unlike flat plane mirrors, spherical mirrors are curved. Think of the shiny back of a spoon – that's a spherical mirror in action!

Concave vs. Convex Mirrors

  • Concave Mirror: Reflecting surface is curved inwards (like a cave).
  • Convex Mirror: Reflecting surface is curved outwards (like a bulging eye).

🎙️ The inward curve of a concave mirror focuses light, while the outward curve of a convex mirror spreads it out. This difference gives them distinct uses.

Key Terms for Spherical Mirrors (Part 1)

  • Pole (P): Geometric centre of the mirror's surface.
  • Centre of Curvature (C): Centre of the sphere from which the mirror is cut.
  • Radius of Curvature (R): Radius of the sphere from which the mirror is cut.

🎙️ These terms help us precisely describe the shape and properties of spherical mirrors. Imagine the mirror being cut from a larger glass ball.

📍

Key Terms for Spherical Mirrors (Part 2)

  • Principal Axis: Line joining the Pole and Centre of Curvature.
  • Principal Focus (F): Point where parallel rays converge (concave) or appear to diverge (convex).
  • Focal Length (f): Distance between Pole and Principal Focus (f = R/2).

🎙️ The principal focus is a very important point as it dictates how light rays behave after reflection, either converging or diverging.

Rules for Drawing Ray Diagrams

  • 1. Ray parallel to Principal Axis passes through F (or appears to).
  • 2. Ray passing through F (or directed towards F) becomes parallel.
  • 3. Ray passing through C (or directed towards C) reflects back along the same path.
  • 4. Ray incident at P reflects symmetrically.

🎙️ These four rules are your toolkit for accurately drawing ray diagrams, which are essential for understanding image formation by spherical mirrors.

Image Formation by Convex Mirror

  • No matter where the object is, the image is always between P and F.
  • They provide a wider field of view.

🎙️ Convex mirrors are much simpler; they consistently produce a specific type of image. This predictability makes them ideal for certain safety applications.

Laws Governing Refraction

  • 1. Incident ray, refracted ray, and normal lie in the same plane.
  • 2. Snell's Law: Ratio of sine of angle of incidence to sine of angle of refraction is constant (refractive index).

🎙️ Just like reflection, refraction also follows specific laws. Snell's Law is crucial as it quantitatively describes how much light bends when moving between two media.

📏
Snell's Law

Refractive Index: Measure of Bending

  • The refractive index (n) indicates how much light slows down and bends in a medium.
  • It's the ratio of the speed of light in vacuum to the speed of light in the medium.
  • Denser media generally have higher refractive indices.

🎙️ The refractive index is like a 'bending power' for a material. A higher refractive index means light bends more when it enters that material.

Air (n=1.0)
Water (n=1.33)
Glass (n=1.5)

Refraction Through a Glass Slab

  • Light bends twice: once entering the slab, once exiting it.
  • The emergent ray is parallel to the incident ray but laterally displaced.
  • This displacement depends on the slab's thickness and refractive index.

🎙️ When light passes through a glass slab, it doesn't just bend once. It bends at both surfaces, but surprisingly, the final ray is parallel to the initial one, just shifted a bit.

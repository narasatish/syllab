What is Currency?

  • A system of money in general use in a particular country.
  • It's a medium of exchange, a store of value, and a unit of account.
  • Facilitates trade and transactions.

🎙️ Simply put, currency is the official money of a country. It helps us buy and sell things, saves value for later, and gives us a standard way to price goods and services.

Why Different Currencies?

  • National sovereignty and economic independence.
  • Allows countries to control their own monetary policy.
  • Reflects the economic strength and stability of a nation.

🎙️ Having different currencies is a sign of a country's independence. It allows governments to manage their own economy, set interest rates, and respond to local economic conditions.

What is an Exchange Rate?

  • The value of one currency in terms of another currency.
  • It tells you how much of one currency you can get for another.
  • Changes constantly based on market forces.

🎙️ The exchange rate is like a price tag for money. It tells you, for example, how many Indian Rupees you need to give to get one US Dollar, or vice versa.

↔️
Currency conversion

Understanding Exchange Rate Quotes

  • Often quoted as '1 foreign currency = X amount of domestic currency'.
  • Example: 1 US Dollar (USD) = 83 Indian Rupees (INR).
  • This means you need 83 Rupees to buy 1 Dollar.

🎙️ When you see an exchange rate like '1 USD = 83 INR', it's telling you the price of one US Dollar in Indian Rupees. This is the most common way to quote rates in India.

Real-Life Example: USD to INR Exchange

  • Current rate: 1 USD = ₹83.
  • If you have $100, you can get 100 * ₹83 = ₹8,300.
  • If you have ₹830, you can buy ₹830 / 83 = $10.

🎙️ Let's say you're an Indian student who received $100 from a relative in the US. At an exchange rate of ₹83 per dollar, you would convert that to ₹8,300 in India.

Factors Affecting Exchange Rates: Demand & Supply

  • Like any other good, currency value is affected by demand and supply.
  • High demand for a currency usually increases its value.
  • High supply (more people selling) usually decreases its value.

🎙️ Think of it like vegetables in a market. If everyone wants tomatoes, their price goes up. Similarly, if more people want to buy US Dollars, the dollar's value against other currencies will likely increase.

⚖️
Balancing forces

Factors Affecting Exchange Rates: Interest Rates

  • Higher interest rates attract foreign investors.
  • Investors buy the local currency to invest, increasing its demand.
  • This can strengthen the local currency.

🎙️ When a country offers higher interest rates on its investments, foreign investors are more likely to put their money there. To do this, they first need to buy that country's currency, boosting its value.

🏦
Attracting investments

Factors Affecting Exchange Rates: Political Stability

  • Countries with stable governments and economies attract more investment.
  • This increases demand for their currency.
  • Unstable conditions can lead to currency depreciation.

🎙️ No one wants to invest in a risky place. If a country has a stable political environment and a strong economy, investors feel safer. This confidence translates into higher demand for its currency.

Worked Example 1: Buying Foreign Currency

  • You need 500 Euros for your trip to France.
  • Current rate: 1 Euro = ₹90.
  • Total Rupees needed = 500 Euros * ₹90/Euro = ₹45,000.

🎙️ Let's put this into practice. If you're going to Europe and need 500 Euros, and the rate is 90 Rupees per Euro, you simply multiply the amount of Euros you need by the exchange rate to find out how many Rupees you'll spend.

1Identify need: 500€
2Find rate: 1€ = ₹90
3Multiply: 500 * 90
4Result: ₹45,000

Worked Example 2: Converting Back to Home Currency

  • You returned from the UK with 50 British Pounds (£).
  • Current rate: 1 Pound = ₹105.
  • Total Rupees you'll get = 50 Pounds * ₹105/Pound = ₹5,250.

🎙️ Similarly, if you come back home with some foreign currency, say 50 British Pounds, you'd multiply that by the current exchange rate to convert it back into Indian Rupees.

Types of Exchange Rate Systems: Fixed

  • Government or central bank sets the exchange rate.
  • Rate is maintained by buying/selling foreign currency.
  • Offers stability but limits monetary policy flexibility.

🎙️ In a fixed exchange rate system, the government decides and maintains the value of its currency against another currency or a basket of currencies. This brings stability but means the central bank has less freedom with interest rates.

🔒
Rate is locked
18 / 25

Types of Exchange Rate Systems: Floating

  • Exchange rate is determined purely by market forces (demand & supply).
  • Most major currencies operate under this system (e.g., INR, USD).
  • Offers flexibility but can lead to volatility.

🎙️ A floating exchange rate, which India largely follows, means the value of the Rupee changes constantly based on how much demand there is for it versus how much is available. This allows for flexibility but can also mean more ups and downs.

🌊
Market driven

Impact of Exchange Rates: For Importers

  • Importers buy goods from other countries.
  • A stronger Rupee (e.g., 1 USD = ₹80 instead of ₹83) means they pay less Rupees for the same dollar value of goods.
  • Good for importers, as imported goods become cheaper.

🎙️ If you're an Indian business importing electronics from the US, a stronger Rupee means you need fewer Rupees to buy the same amount of Dollars. This makes your imports cheaper and can lower prices for consumers.

🚢
Bringing goods in

Impact of Exchange Rates: For Exporters

  • Exporters sell goods to other countries.
  • A weaker Rupee (e.g., 1 USD = ₹85 instead of ₹83) means they get more Rupees for the same dollar value of goods.
  • Good for exporters, as their products become more competitive abroad.

🎙️ Conversely, if you're an Indian company exporting textiles to the US, a weaker Rupee means that when your US customer pays in dollars, you get more Rupees back. This makes your exports more attractive in the international market.

Impact of Exchange Rates: For Tourists

  • When traveling abroad: A stronger Rupee means your money goes further.
  • When foreign tourists visit India: A weaker Rupee means India is a cheaper destination for them.
  • Exchange rates affect your travel budget significantly.

🎙️ For us, as tourists, a stronger Rupee is great when we travel abroad because we can buy more foreign currency with our Rupees. For foreign tourists coming to India, a weaker Rupee makes their trip more affordable, potentially boosting tourism.

Safety Tips for Currency Exchange

  • Always exchange currency at authorized dealers like banks or licensed money changers.
  • Compare rates from different sources.
  • Avoid exchanging money in unauthorized places (black market).

🎙️ When you need to exchange money, always prioritize safety and legality. Using authorized channels protects you from counterfeit currency and ensures you get a fair and transparent rate.

Recap: Key Takeaways

  • Currency is the money of a country.
  • Exchange rate is the value of one currency against another.
  • Demand, supply, interest rates, and stability affect rates.
  • Rates impact importers, exporters, and travelers.

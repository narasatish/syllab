Meet the Family: What is a Currency?

  • The specific form of money used in a country.
  • Each country has its own unique currency.
  • It has a name, symbol, and specific notes/coins.
  • Example: Indian Rupee (₹) in India.

🎙️ Just like every family has a unique surname, every country has its own unique currency. It's the official 'money' of that nation, recognised and accepted within its borders.

Defining the Exchange Rate

  • The rate at which one currency can be exchanged for another.
  • It expresses the value of one currency in relation to another.
  • Example: 1 US Dollar (USD) = 83 Indian Rupees (INR).
  • This rate constantly changes based on various factors.

🎙️ Formally, the exchange rate is the numerical relationship between two currencies. It's the magic number that tells us how much of one currency we get for another.

Let's Convert! INR to USD

  • If 1 USD = ₹83
  • To buy 1 USD, you need to give ₹83.
  • To buy 100 USD, you need to give ₹83 x 100 = ₹8300.
  • This is how Rohan would convert his rupees for his trip!

🎙️ This simple calculation is the core of currency exchange. It tells you exactly how much local currency you need to acquire a certain amount of foreign currency for your needs.

The Bank's Business: Buy & Sell Rates

  • Banks don't buy and sell at the exact same rate.
  • They have a 'buying rate' (lower) and a 'selling rate' (higher).
  • The difference is their profit margin for the service.
  • Always check both rates when converting!

🎙️ Just like any business, banks and money changers also make a profit. They buy foreign currency from you at a slightly lower rate and sell it to you at a slightly higher rate. This small difference is their income.

Why Rates Change: Demand & Supply

  • High demand for a currency (e.g., USD) makes it stronger.
  • Low demand makes it weaker.
  • More people wanting to buy US goods = higher demand for USD.
  • Like any other commodity, its price (exchange rate) fluctuates.

🎙️ The most fundamental reason for exchange rate changes is the simple economic principle of demand and supply. If many people want a certain currency, its value goes up.

Why Rates Change: Economic Health

  • A stable economy attracts foreign investment.
  • Investors buy that country's currency, increasing demand.
  • Political stability, low inflation, and strong growth strengthen a currency.
  • Uncertainty or crises can weaken a currency.

🎙️ A country's overall economic health plays a huge role. If a country is doing well, more people want to invest there, which means they need to buy that country's currency, making it stronger.

Two Main Systems: Fixed vs. Floating

  • Fixed Exchange Rate: Government or central bank sets the rate.
  • Floating Exchange Rate: Market forces (demand/supply) determine the rate.
  • Most major currencies today use a floating rate system.
  • India uses a managed floating exchange rate system.

🎙️ While most currencies today 'float' freely, it's good to know there are different ways countries manage their currency's value. India's system tries to balance market forces with some central bank intervention.

Global Business: Imports & Exports

  • Indian exporters sell goods abroad, earning foreign currency.
  • Indian importers buy goods from abroad, paying foreign currency.
  • A weaker Rupee makes Indian exports cheaper (good for exporters).
  • A stronger Rupee makes imports cheaper (good for importers).

🎙️ For businesses, exchange rates are critical. They determine the profitability of selling goods abroad or the cost of buying raw materials from other countries.

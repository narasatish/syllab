What are Commodities?

  • Basic goods used in commerce.
  • Interchangeable with other goods of the same type.
  • Raw materials for industries.

🎙️ Think of commodities as the building blocks of our economy. They are essential raw materials that industries transform into finished products, from the food we eat to the fuel that powers our vehicles.

📦
Raw Materials
3 / 25

Types of Commodities: Hard vs. Soft

  • Hard: Mined from the earth (metals, energy).
  • Soft: Grown or livestock (agricultural products).

🎙️ We can broadly categorize commodities into two main types. Hard commodities are extracted from the ground, like minerals and fossil fuels, while soft commodities are cultivated or raised, like crops and livestock.

Hard (e.g., Oil, Gold)
Soft (e.g., Wheat, Cotton)

Example: Gold — A Precious Metal

  • A highly valued yellow metal.
  • Used for jewelry, investments, and industrial purposes.
  • Known for its rarity and durability.

🎙️ Gold is perhaps the most famous hard commodity. Its beauty, rarity, and resistance to corrosion have made it a symbol of wealth and power for centuries, and it remains a popular investment today.

Symbol of Wealth

Example: Crude Oil — The Black Gold

  • A fossil fuel, source of energy.
  • Refined into petrol, diesel, plastics.
  • Global prices impact many industries.

🎙️ Crude oil is another critical hard commodity. It powers our vehicles, heats our homes, and is a raw material for countless products. Its price fluctuations can significantly affect economies worldwide.

Energy Source

Example: Wheat — A Staple Crop

  • A major cereal grain.
  • Used to make bread, pasta, and other food products.
  • Harvests and weather affect its supply.

🎙️ Moving to soft commodities, wheat is a prime example. It's a fundamental food source for billions, and its availability and price depend heavily on agricultural conditions, like weather and soil quality.

Why Do People Trade Commodities?

  • For profit (speculation).
  • To manage risk (hedging).
  • To secure supplies for businesses.

🎙️ People trade commodities for several reasons. Some aim to make money from price changes, while others use it to protect their businesses from future price volatility, ensuring stable costs or revenues.

🤝
Buying & Selling

Understanding Supply and Demand

  • High demand + Low supply = Higher prices.
  • Low demand + High supply = Lower prices.
  • Basic economic principle for all commodities.

🎙️ Just like any other good, commodity prices are fundamentally determined by the balance between how much is available (supply) and how much people want (demand). This interplay constantly shifts prices.

Demand (Want)
Supply (Available)

Factors Affecting Gold Prices

  • Global economic uncertainty.
  • Inflation fears.
  • Interest rate changes.
  • Demand for jewelry and investment.

🎙️ Gold is often seen as a 'safe haven' asset. Its price tends to rise when there's economic instability or inflation, as people seek to protect their wealth from currency devaluation or market crashes.

📈
Price Movements

Factors Affecting Oil Prices

  • Geopolitical events (wars, conflicts).
  • Global economic growth.
  • Production levels by oil-producing nations.
  • Weather events.

🎙️ Oil prices are highly sensitive to global events. Political instability in major oil-producing regions or changes in global economic activity can have a significant impact on its supply and demand, thus affecting prices.

🌍
Global Impact

Factors Affecting Agricultural Prices

  • Weather patterns (droughts, floods).
  • Crop diseases and pests.
  • Government policies (subsidies).
  • Global population growth.

🎙️ For soft commodities like wheat or corn, weather is a huge factor. A bad monsoon in India, for example, can drastically affect food prices and availability across the country due to reduced harvests.

🌧️
Climate Effects

Where Are Commodities Traded?

  • Specialized marketplaces called Commodity Exchanges.
  • Buyers and sellers meet electronically or physically.
  • Examples: MCX (India), CME Group (USA).

🎙️ Commodities aren't just bought and sold in local markets. Large-scale trading happens on dedicated exchanges, facilitating global transactions and ensuring fair and transparent pricing for these essential goods.

🏢
Exchange Building

Trading with Futures Contracts

  • An agreement to buy/sell a commodity at a future date.
  • Price is agreed upon today.
  • Helps manage price risk for producers and consumers.

🎙️ Futures contracts are key to commodity trading. They allow businesses to lock in prices for future transactions, providing stability and predictability in an otherwise volatile market.

🗓️
Future Date

Real Example: A Farmer's Hedge

  • A farmer expects to harvest wheat in 3 months.
  • Fears prices might fall by harvest time.
  • Sells a wheat futures contract today to lock in a price.

🎙️ Imagine a farmer who wants to ensure a certain income for his crop. By selling a futures contract today, he can 'hedge' against potential price drops before his harvest, securing his earnings.

Real Example: A Biscuit Manufacturer's Hedge

  • A biscuit company needs sugar in 6 months.
  • Fears prices might rise.
  • Buys a sugar futures contract today to lock in a price.

🎙️ On the flip side, a company that uses commodities as raw materials can use futures to protect against price increases. A biscuit maker might secure sugar prices this way to keep their production costs stable.

Why is Carbon So Special?

  • Atomic Number 6
  • Non-metal
  • Forms millions of compounds

🎙️ Carbon has an atomic number of 6, meaning it has 6 protons. Its position in the periodic table gives it unique properties, allowing it to form more compounds than any other element.

The Power of Sharing: Covalent Bonds

  • Carbon shares electrons
  • Forms strong bonds
  • Achieves stable electron configuration

🎙️ Unlike ionic bonds where electrons are transferred, carbon forms covalent bonds by sharing its valence electrons with other atoms. This sharing allows both atoms to achieve a stable electron configuration, making these bonds very strong.

🤝
Sharing is Caring

Carbon's Tetravalency

  • Four valence electrons
  • Can form four bonds
  • Bonds with C, H, O, N, S, Halogens

🎙️ Carbon has four electrons in its outermost shell, giving it a valency of four, also known as tetravalency. This means a single carbon atom can form four covalent bonds with other atoms, acting like a central hub.

✋
Four Arms of Carbon

Self-Linking Property: Catenation

  • Carbon links with other carbon atoms
  • Forms long chains, branches, rings
  • Leads to large molecules

🎙️ Catenation is the unique ability of carbon atoms to link with other carbon atoms through covalent bonds. This property allows carbon to form incredibly long chains, branched structures, and even closed rings, creating a vast diversity of compounds.

Carbon Chains

Catenation in Plastics

  • Polyethylene is a polymer
  • Long chains of carbon atoms
  • Makes plastic strong and flexible

🎙️ Think about plastic bottles or bags. They are made of polymers like polyethylene, which are essentially very long chains of carbon atoms linked together. This extensive catenation gives plastics their characteristic strength and flexibility.

Alkanes: Saturated & Stable

  • Carbon atoms linked by single bonds
  • General formula: CnH2n+2
  • Less reactive due to single bonds

🎙️ Alkanes are hydrocarbons where all carbon-carbon bonds are single bonds, meaning they are 'saturated' with hydrogen atoms. They are generally quite stable and less reactive compared to other hydrocarbons because of these strong single bonds.

✅
All Single Bonds

  • Simplest alkane (CH4)
  • Main component of natural gas
  • Used as fuel for cooking and heating

🎙️ Methane, with the formula CH4, is the simplest alkane and a primary component of natural gas. We use it every day in our homes for cooking and heating, demonstrating the practical application of these saturated hydrocarbons.

Alkenes & Alkynes: Unsaturated & Reactive

  • Alkenes: at least one C=C double bond
  • Alkynes: at least one C≡C triple bond
  • More reactive due to multiple bonds

🎙️ Unsaturated hydrocarbons contain at least one carbon-carbon double bond (alkenes) or triple bond (alkynes). These multiple bonds make them more reactive than alkanes, allowing them to participate in a wider range of chemical reactions.

  • Simplest alkene (C2H4)
  • Naturally produced by fruits
  • Initiates and regulates ripening

🎙️ Ethene, also known as ethylene, is a simple alkene that plays a crucial role in agriculture. It's a natural plant hormone that helps ripen fruits, turning green bananas yellow and making them sweet.

Ethyne: Acetylene for Welding

  • Commonly known as acetylene
  • Used in oxy-acetylene torches for welding

🎙️ Ethyne, or acetylene, is the simplest alkyne and is widely used in industrial applications. When mixed with oxygen and ignited, it produces an extremely hot flame, making it ideal for welding and cutting metals.

Meet Carbon: The Element!

  • Symbol: C
  • Atomic Number: 6
  • Electronic Configuration: 2, 4

🎙️ Carbon is a non-metal element with the symbol 'C'. Its atomic number is 6, meaning it has 6 protons and 6 electrons. This electronic configuration, with 4 valence electrons, is key to its unique bonding behaviour.

Where Do We Find Carbon?

  • In Earth's crust: as minerals like carbonates.
  • In the atmosphere: as carbon dioxide (CO2).
  • In all living things: the backbone of organic molecules.

🎙️ Carbon is incredibly abundant! You can find it in rocks and minerals, in the air we breathe as carbon dioxide, and most importantly, it forms the basis of all life on Earth, from tiny bacteria to giant trees and us!

How Does Carbon Bond?

  • Carbon forms covalent bonds.
  • It shares electrons with other atoms.
  • This allows it to achieve stability.

🎙️ Unlike metals that lose electrons or non-metals that gain electrons, Carbon prefers to share. This type of sharing is called covalent bonding, and it's what makes Carbon compounds so stable and diverse.

🤝
Sharing Electrons
5 / 25

Why Covalent Bonds for Carbon?

  • It has 4 valence electrons.
  • Cannot easily lose 4 electrons (needs too much energy).
  • Cannot easily gain 4 electrons (nucleus cannot hold 10 electrons).

🎙️ With 4 valence electrons, Carbon is in a unique position. Losing all 4 would require immense energy, and gaining 4 would create an unstable ion. Sharing electrons is the most energetically favourable way for Carbon to complete its octet and become stable.

🔗
Covalent Link

Sharing is Caring: Covalent Bonds

  • Atoms come close to each other.
  • They share one or more pairs of electrons.
  • This shared pair belongs to both atoms.

🎙️ Imagine two friends, each needing something the other has. Instead of one giving it all to the other, they decide to share it. In the same way, atoms share electron pairs to fill their outermost shells, forming a strong covalent bond.

Example: Methane (CH4)

  • Simplest hydrocarbon.
  • One Carbon atom, four Hydrogen atoms.
  • Each C-H bond is a covalent bond.

🎙️ Methane, often called natural gas, is a perfect example of covalent bonding in Carbon. The central Carbon atom shares one electron with each of the four Hydrogen atoms, forming four strong covalent bonds and completing its octet.

Same Element, Different Forms: Allotropes

  • Allotropes are different structural forms of the same element.
  • They have different physical properties.
  • But their chemical properties are similar.

🎙️ Think of it like different outfits for the same person. Allotropes are different arrangements of the same Carbon atoms, leading to vastly different appearances and physical characteristics, even though they are chemically the same element.

🔄
Different Forms

Allotropes of Carbon: Diamond

  • Hardest known natural substance.
  • Each carbon atom bonded to four others.
  • Used in jewellery and cutting tools.

🎙️ Diamond is a beautiful and extremely strong allotrope of Carbon. Its rigid, three-dimensional network of strong covalent bonds makes it incredibly hard and gives it its characteristic sparkle, making it valuable for both adornment and industrial use.

💎
Sparkling Diamond
10 / 25

Allotropes of Carbon: Graphite

  • Soft, slippery, and good conductor of electricity.
  • Carbon atoms arranged in hexagonal layers.
  • Used in pencil leads and lubricants.

🎙️ Graphite is the exact opposite of diamond in terms of physical properties, even though both are made of pure Carbon. Its layered structure, with weak forces between layers, allows it to be slippery and conduct electricity, which is why it's used in pencils.

✏️
Pencil Lead

Allotropes of Carbon: Fullerenes

  • Spherical molecules, like a football.
  • Example: C60 (Buckminsterfullerene).
  • Used in nanotechnology and medicine.

🎙️ Fullerenes are a more recently discovered allotrope of Carbon, shaped like hollow spheres or tubes. The most famous is C60, resembling a football. These structures have exciting potential in advanced technologies due to their unique properties.

⚽
Buckminsterfullerene

Carbon's Special Power: Catenation

  • Ability of carbon atoms to link with other carbon atoms.
  • Forms long chains, branched chains, and rings.
  • Leads to a huge number of carbon compounds.

🎙️ Catenation is Carbon's superpower! It's the unique ability of Carbon atoms to bond with each other repeatedly. This self-linking property allows Carbon to form incredibly long and complex structures, which is why there are millions of carbon compounds.

Carbon's Four Arms: Tetravalency

  • Carbon has a valency of four.
  • It can form bonds with four other atoms.
  • These can be other carbon atoms or other elements.

🎙️ Remember Carbon has 4 valence electrons? This means it can form four covalent bonds. This 'tetravalency' allows carbon to connect to many different atoms in various ways, further contributing to the vast diversity of carbon compounds.

Single, Double, Triple Bonds

  • Single bond: sharing one pair of electrons (C-C).
  • Double bond: sharing two pairs of electrons (C=C).
  • Triple bond: sharing three pairs of electrons (C≡C).

🎙️ Carbon isn't limited to just single bonds! It can also share two pairs of electrons to form a double bond, or even three pairs to form a triple bond. This flexibility in bonding further increases the variety of structures Carbon can create.

Meet Hydrocarbons: Carbon & Hydrogen

  • Compounds made only of Carbon and Hydrogen.
  • Simplest organic compounds.
  • Examples: Methane, Ethane, Propane.

🎙️ Hydrocarbons are fundamental organic compounds, forming the basis of fuels like petrol and LPG. They are the simplest type of carbon compounds, consisting solely of carbon and hydrogen atoms connected by covalent bonds.

⛽
Fuel
16 / 25

Saturated Hydrocarbons: Alkanes

  • Contain only carbon-carbon single bonds.
  • All valencies of carbon are 'saturated' with hydrogen.
  • Also known as alkanes (e.g., Ethane, Propane).

🎙️ In saturated hydrocarbons, every carbon atom forms only single bonds with other atoms, typically hydrogen. This means they are 'full' and cannot add any more hydrogen atoms, making them generally less reactive.

🔒
Fully Bonded
17 / 25

Unsaturated Hydrocarbons: Alkenes & Alkynes

  • Contain at least one carbon-carbon double or triple bond.
  • Can add more hydrogen atoms.
  • Alkenes (C=C) and Alkynes (C≡C).

🎙️ Unsaturated hydrocarbons, on the other hand, have double or triple bonds between carbon atoms. These multiple bonds allow them to react and add more atoms, such as hydrogen, making them more reactive than saturated hydrocarbons.

🔓
More Bonds Possible

Cyclic Hydrocarbons: Rings of Carbon

  • Carbon atoms form closed rings.
  • Can be saturated (e.g., Cyclohexane) or unsaturated (e.g., Benzene).
  • Have distinct properties due to their ring structure.

🎙️ Beyond straight and branched chains, carbon atoms can also form fascinating ring structures. These cyclic hydrocarbons can be saturated or unsaturated and are found in many important compounds, including some aromatic ones like benzene.

Functional Groups: Changing Properties

  • Atoms or groups of atoms attached to a carbon chain.
  • Determine the chemical properties of the compound.
  • Even a small change can create a new compound.

🎙️ Functional groups are like special attachments that give carbon compounds their unique chemical personalities. Even if the carbon chain is similar, a different functional group can completely change how the compound reacts and behaves.

🧪
Chemical Change

Common Functional Groups

  • Alcohol (-OH): e.g., Ethanol (drinking alcohol).
  • Carboxylic Acid (-COOH): e.g., Ethanoic acid (vinegar).
  • These groups give distinct properties.

🎙️ Here are two common examples: the hydroxyl group (-OH) makes a compound an alcohol, while the carboxyl group (-COOH) makes it a carboxylic acid. Notice how these small groups drastically alter the compound's characteristics and uses.

Chemical Reactions: The Definition

  • Process where reactants transform into products.
  • New substances with new properties are formed.
  • Atoms rearrange, but no atoms are lost or gained.

🎙️ Formally, a chemical reaction is a process that involves the rearrangement of the atomic structure of a substance. Old bonds break, and new bonds form, leading to entirely new materials.

How to Write a Chemical Equation

  • 1. Write formulas for reactants and products.
  • 2. Use '+' between multiple reactants/products.
  • 3. Use '→' to separate reactants and products.

🎙️ To write a chemical equation, first identify all reactants and products and write their correct chemical formulas. Then, place a plus sign between multiple reactants and products. Finally, use an arrow to show the reaction direction.

Skeletal vs. Balanced Equations

  • Skeletal Equation: Only shows formulas, not balanced.
  • Example: H₂ + O₂ → H₂O
  • Balanced Equation: Equal number of atoms on both sides.

🎙️ A skeletal equation simply lists the correct formulas but doesn't necessarily show the correct proportions. A balanced equation, however, ensures that the number of atoms of each element is the same on both the reactant and product sides.

The Law of Conservation of Mass

  • Mass can neither be created nor destroyed.
  • Total mass of reactants = Total mass of products.
  • Balancing ensures this law is obeyed.

🎙️ Balancing chemical equations is crucial because of the Law of Conservation of Mass. This fundamental law states that matter cannot be created or destroyed in a chemical reaction, only rearranged. Therefore, the total mass and number of atoms must be equal on both sides.

⚖️

Balancing Equations: Hit & Trial Method

  • 1. Write unbalanced equation.
  • 2. Count atoms of each element.
  • 3. Balance elements one by one (metals, non-metals, H, O).
  • 4. Check final balance.

🎙️ The hit and trial method is a common way to balance equations. Start by writing the unbalanced equation, then count the atoms of each element on both sides. Systematically balance them, usually starting with metals, then non-metals, and finally hydrogen and oxygen. Always verify your final count.

1Write Eq.
2Count Atoms
3Balance
4Verify

Balancing Example: Mg + O₂ → MgO

  • Reactants: Mg=1, O=2
  • Products: Mg=1, O=1
  • Balance O: Mg + O₂ → 2MgO
  • Now balance Mg: 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO

🎙️ Let's balance the reaction of Magnesium with Oxygen. First, count the atoms. Oxygen is unbalanced. By placing a '2' before MgO, we balance oxygen. This unbalances magnesium, so we add a '2' before Mg on the reactant side. Now, all atoms are balanced!

Balancing Example: Fe + H₂O → Fe₃O₄ + H₂

  • 1. Balance Fe: 3Fe + H₂O → Fe₃O₄ + H₂
  • 2. Balance O: 3Fe + 4H₂O → Fe₃O₄ + H₂
  • 3. Balance H: 3Fe + 4H₂O → Fe₃O₄ + 4H₂
  • Final check: All atoms are balanced.

🎙️ This one is a bit more complex. Start with Iron, then Oxygen, and finally Hydrogen. Remember to adjust coefficients for entire molecules. After each step, recount to ensure the balance is maintained. Practice makes perfect!

Building Up & Breaking Down

  • Combination: A + B → AB (Two or more react to form one).
  • Decomposition: AB → A + B (One breaks down into two or more).
  • Opposite processes, often involve energy changes.

🎙️ Combination reactions are like building blocks, where simpler substances combine to form a more complex one. Decomposition reactions are the opposite, where a single compound breaks down into simpler substances. Both are fundamental types.

The "Swap" Reaction: Displacement

  • A + BC → AC + B
  • More reactive element displaces less reactive one.
  • Example: Fe + CuSO₄ → FeSO₄ + Cu

🎙️ In a displacement reaction, a more reactive element kicks out a less reactive one from its compound. Think of it as one player replacing another on a team. Iron is more reactive than copper, so it displaces copper from copper sulfate.

Oxidation and Reduction: The Redox Duo

  • Oxidation: Gain of Oxygen / Loss of Hydrogen / Loss of Electrons.
  • Reduction: Loss of Oxygen / Gain of Hydrogen / Gain of Electrons.
  • They always occur simultaneously (Redox reactions).

🎙️ Oxidation and reduction are two sides of the same coin. Oxidation means gaining oxygen, losing hydrogen, or losing electrons. Reduction is the opposite: losing oxygen, gaining hydrogen, or gaining electrons. They always happen together in what we call redox reactions.

Redox reactions involve electron transfer between reactants. Oxidation = loss of electrons

Who's Doing What? Agents

  • Oxidizing Agent: Causes oxidation, gets reduced itself.
  • Reducing Agent: Causes reduction, gets oxidized itself.
  • They facilitate the transfer of oxygen/hydrogen/electrons.

🎙️ An oxidizing agent is the substance that makes another substance get oxidized, and in doing so, it gets reduced. Conversely, a reducing agent helps another substance get reduced, and it itself gets oxidized. They are like helpers in the redox process.

Oxidation in Action: Rancidity

  • Spoilage of oily/fatty foods due to oxidation.

🎙️ Rancidity is another everyday effect of oxidation, specifically affecting food containing fats and oils. We can prevent this by adding antioxidants, refrigerating food, or storing it in nitrogen gas.

Avoid These Common Mistakes!

  • Not balancing equations correctly.
  • Confusing displacement with double displacement.
  • Mixing up oxidation and reduction definitions.
  • Forgetting state symbols in final equations.

🎙️ Students often make a few key errors. Always double-check your balancing, especially with complex equations. Remember, displacement involves one element replacing another, while double displacement involves an exchange of ions. Be clear on the definitions of oxidation and reduction.

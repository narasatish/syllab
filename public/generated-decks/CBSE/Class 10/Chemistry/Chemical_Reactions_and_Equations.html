What are Chemical Reactions?

  • Processes where new substances form.
  • Involve breaking and making bonds.
  • Reactants change into products.

🎙️ A chemical reaction is basically when one set of chemicals turns into another set. Think of it like ingredients turning into a cake – you can't easily turn the cake back into flour and eggs!

Signs of a Chemical Reaction (1/2)

  • Change in State (solid, liquid, gas)
  • Change in Colour

🎙️ How do we know a chemical reaction has happened? One way is if the physical state changes, like a liquid becoming a gas. Another clear sign is a change in colour, like when iron rusts and turns reddish-brown.

Signs of a Chemical Reaction (2/2)

  • Evolution of a Gas (bubbles)
  • Change in Temperature (hotter or colder)
  • Formation of a Precipitate (solid forms in liquid)

🎙️ Other signs include seeing bubbles, which means a gas is being produced. Sometimes, the container might feel hot or cold, indicating a temperature change. And if a solid suddenly appears in a liquid, that's called a precipitate.

Representing Chemical Reactions

  • A shorthand way to show reactions.
  • Uses chemical formulas.
  • Shows reactants and products.

🎙️ Instead of writing long sentences, chemists use a special language called chemical equations. It's like a recipe but for chemicals, showing exactly what goes in and what comes out.

The Need for Balancing Equations

  • Obeys the Law of Conservation of Mass.
  • Mass cannot be created or destroyed.
  • Number of atoms must be equal on both sides.

🎙️ Nature loves balance! The Law of Conservation of Mass tells us that atoms don't just disappear or appear out of nowhere. So, in a chemical reaction, the number of each type of atom must be the same before and after the reaction.

⚖️
Equal Atoms

Balancing by Hit and Trial Method

  • Start with the most complex formula.
  • Balance metals, then non-metals.
  • Balance hydrogen and oxygen last.
  • Check all atoms.

🎙️ The hit and trial method is like solving a puzzle. You start by balancing the atoms that appear in the fewest places or are part of the most complex molecules, usually leaving hydrogen and oxygen for the end.

Balancing Example: Iron and Water

  • Fe + H2O → Fe3O4 + H2

🎙️ Let's balance this equation. First, balance Iron: 3Fe. Then Oxygen: 4H2O. Finally Hydrogen: 4H2. The balanced equation is 3Fe + 4H2O → Fe3O4 + 4H2.

Balancing Example: Nitrogen and Hydrogen

  • N2 + H2 → NH3

🎙️ Here, we have nitrogen and hydrogen forming ammonia. Start with Nitrogen: N2 on left, so 2NH3 on right. Now balance Hydrogen: 3H2 on left for 6 H atoms. So, N2 + 3H2 → 2NH3 is balanced.

Combination Reaction

  • Two or more reactants combine.
  • Form a single product.
  • A + B → AB.

🎙️ Imagine two friends meeting and becoming a single team. In a combination reaction, two or more simpler substances join hands to form one more complex substance.

Joining Together

Combination Reaction: Burning of Coal

  • Carbon (C) combines with Oxygen (O2).
  • Forms Carbon Dioxide (CO2).
  • C(s) + O2(g) → CO2(g).

🎙️ When you burn coal, the carbon in the coal reacts with oxygen from the air. This is a classic combination reaction, forming carbon dioxide gas and releasing energy.

Decomposition Reaction

  • A single reactant breaks down.
  • Forms two or more simpler products.
  • AB → A + B.

🎙️ This is the opposite of combination. Here, one big molecule breaks down into smaller, simpler ones. Think of a big chocolate bar breaking into smaller pieces.

💔
Breaking Apart

Ways to Decompose

  • Thermal Decomposition (by heat)
  • Electrolytic Decomposition (by electricity)
  • Photolytic Decomposition (by light)

🎙️ Decomposition reactions need energy to break bonds. This energy can come from heat (like heating limestone), electricity (like splitting water), or even light (like silver bromide in photography).

  • A + BC → AC + B.

🎙️ Imagine a strong person replacing a weaker person in a team. In chemistry, a more reactive element takes the place of a less reactive element in a compound.

Displacement: Iron in Copper Sulphate

  • Iron (Fe) is more reactive than Copper (Cu).
  • Fe displaces Cu from CuSO4 solution.
  • Fe(s) + CuSO4(aq) → FeSO4(aq) + Cu(s).

🎙️ When you put an iron nail into a blue copper sulphate solution, the iron slowly displaces the copper. The nail gets a reddish-brown coating of copper, and the solution turns light green due to iron sulphate.

Double Displacement Reaction

  • Exchange of ions between two compounds.
  • Forms two new compounds.
  • AB + CD → AD + CB.

🎙️ Here, two compounds essentially swap their 'partners' or ions. It's like two dance couples exchanging partners to form two new couples.

Double Displacement: Precipitation

  • Barium Chloride + Sodium Sulphate.
  • Forms Barium Sulphate (white precipitate) + Sodium Chloride.
  • BaCl2(aq) + Na2SO4(aq) → BaSO4(s) + 2NaCl(aq).

🎙️ A common type of double displacement reaction is a precipitation reaction, where one of the products is an insoluble solid called a precipitate. Mixing these two clear solutions instantly forms a white, cloudy solid.

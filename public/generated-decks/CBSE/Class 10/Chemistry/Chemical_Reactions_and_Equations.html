Reactants and Products

🎙️ Instead of writing long sentences, chemists use a concise language called chemical equations. They use symbols and formulas, making it easy to understand what's reacting and what's being formed. It's like a universal language for chemical changes.

Why Balance Chemical Equations?

  • To satisfy the Law of Conservation of Mass.
  • Mass can neither be created nor destroyed.
  • Number of atoms of each element must be same on both sides.

🎙️ Balancing equations is crucial because of a fundamental law in chemistry: the Law of Conservation of Mass. This law states that atoms are merely rearranged, not created or destroyed, during a chemical reaction. So, the total number of atoms of each element must be equal on both the reactant and product sides.

Balancing Equations: Step 1

  • Write the unbalanced equation.
  • Draw boxes around each formula (do not change them).
  • List elements and count atoms on both sides.

🎙️ Our first step is to write down the unbalanced equation. It's helpful to draw boxes around the chemical formulas so that we remember not to change the subscripts within them. Then, make a table listing each element and its atom count on both reactant and product sides.

Balancing Equations: Step 2

  • Start balancing with the most complex formula.
  • Balance atoms of metals first, then non-metals.
  • Use coefficients (numbers in front of formulas).

🎙️ Next, we strategically pick an element to balance. Often, starting with the element present in the most complex formula helps. Prioritize metals, then non-metals. Remember, we can only add coefficients in front of formulas; never change the subscripts.

Worked Example: Balancing Magnesium Oxide

  • Unbalanced: Mg + O₂ → MgO
  • Balance O: Mg + O₂ → 2MgO
  • Balance Mg: 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO (Balanced!)

🎙️ Let's balance the formation of magnesium oxide. We see oxygen is unbalanced, so we add a '2' in front of MgO. This makes magnesium unbalanced, so we add a '2' in front of Mg. Now, count the atoms: 2 Mg, 2 O on both sides. The equation is balanced!

Classifying Chemical Reactions

🎙️ Just like we classify animals or plants, we classify chemical reactions into different types. This helps us understand the general patterns of how substances react and allows us to predict the outcomes of similar reactions. We'll explore five major categories.

Type 1: Combination Reaction

  • Two or more reactants combine to form a single product.
  • A + B → AB
  • Also known as synthesis reactions.

🎙️ In a combination reaction, multiple simple substances come together to form one more complex substance. Imagine two friends meeting and forming a team. It's all about bringing things together to create something new and unified.

🤝🤝
Joining Together

Example: Burning of Coal (Combination)

  • Carbon (coal) reacts with oxygen to form carbon dioxide.
  • C(s) + O₂(g) → CO₂(g)
  • This reaction also releases heat and light.

🎙️ A common example of a combination reaction is the burning of coal. Carbon, in the presence of oxygen, combines to form carbon dioxide. This process is exothermic, meaning it releases energy in the form of heat and light, which is why we use coal as fuel.

Type 2: Decomposition Reaction

  • A single reactant breaks down into two or more simpler products.
  • AB → A + B
  • Often requires energy (heat, light, electricity) to occur.

🎙️ Decomposition is the opposite of combination. Here, one complex substance breaks down into simpler ones. Think of it like a relationship breaking apart. This type of reaction usually needs an input of energy, such as heating, light, or electricity, to initiate the breakdown.

Type 3: Displacement Reaction

  • A more reactive element displaces a less reactive element.
  • A + BC → AC + B
  • The more reactive element 'takes the place' of the less reactive one.

🎙️ In a displacement reaction, one element literally 'kicks out' another element from its compound. This happens when the displacing element is more reactive than the element it displaces. It's like a stronger player replacing a weaker one in a team.

Example: Iron Nail in Copper Sulphate

  • Iron is more reactive than copper.
  • Fe(s) + CuSO₄(aq) → FeSO₄(aq) + Cu(s)
  • The blue colour fades, and reddish-brown copper deposits on the nail.

🎙️ When an iron nail is placed in a blue copper sulphate solution, the iron, being more reactive, displaces copper from its salt solution. The solution gradually turns light green due to the formation of iron sulphate, and a reddish-brown coating of copper appears on the iron nail.

Type 4: Double Displacement Reaction

  • Two compounds exchange their ions.
  • AB + CD → AD + CB
  • Often results in a precipitate, gas, or water formation.

🎙️ In a double displacement reaction, it's like two couples swapping partners. The positive and negative ions of two different compounds switch places. These reactions frequently lead to the formation of an insoluble solid called a precipitate, or sometimes a gas or water.

Example: Barium Sulphate Precipitation

  • Barium chloride reacts with sodium sulphate.
  • BaCl₂(aq) + Na₂SO₄(aq) → BaSO₄(s) + 2NaCl(aq)
  • A white precipitate of barium sulphate forms.

🎙️ When aqueous solutions of barium chloride and sodium sulphate are mixed, a white, insoluble solid, barium sulphate, is formed. This is a precipitate. The other product, sodium chloride, remains dissolved in the water. This is a classic example of a double displacement reaction forming a precipitate.

Type 5: Oxidation and Reduction

  • Oxidation: Gain of oxygen, loss of hydrogen, loss of electrons.
  • Reduction: Loss of oxygen, gain of hydrogen, gain of electrons.
  • These always occur simultaneously (Redox reactions).

🎙️ Oxidation and reduction are two sides of the same coin. Oxidation means adding oxygen or removing hydrogen or losing electrons. Reduction is the opposite: losing oxygen, gaining hydrogen, or gaining electrons. They never happen alone; they're always paired up.

Redox Reactions: The Combined Effect

  • One substance is oxidized, while another is reduced.
  • The oxidizing agent gets reduced.
  • The reducing agent gets oxidized.

🎙️ When both oxidation and reduction occur in the same reaction, we call it a Redox reaction. The substance that causes oxidation is called the oxidizing agent and itself gets reduced. Conversely, the substance that causes reduction is the reducing agent and gets oxidized.

Redox reactions involve electron transfer between reactants. Oxidation = loss of electrons

Daily Life: Corrosion and Rancidity

  • Corrosion: Metals reacting with air/moisture (e.g., rusting of iron).
  • Rancidity: Fats and oils reacting with oxygen, causing bad smell/taste.
  • Both are oxidation reactions that cause damage or spoilage.

🎙️ Chemical reactions aren't always beneficial. Corrosion, like rusting, weakens metal structures. Rancidity, which spoils food, is another example of an undesirable oxidation reaction. Understanding these helps us prevent them, like by painting iron or storing food properly.

What is a Circle?

  • A collection of all points in a plane.
  • These points are all at a fixed distance from a fixed point.
  • Think of drawing with a compass!

🎙️ Imagine a fixed point, like the tip of your compass. Now, imagine drawing all the points that are exactly 5 cm away from it. What shape do you get? A circle! That fixed point is called the centre, and the fixed distance is the radius.

Key Terms: Centre and Radius

  • Centre: The fixed point in the middle of the circle (O).
  • Radius: The fixed distance from the centre to any point on the circle (r).
  • All radii of the same circle are equal in length.

🎙️ The centre is the heart of the circle, from which all points on the boundary are equally distant. This distance is called the radius. Remember, whether you measure it from the centre to the top, bottom, or side, the radius will always be the same length.

Understanding Diameter

  • A line segment passing through the centre of the circle.
  • Connects two points on the circle's boundary.
  • It is twice the length of the radius (d = 2r).

🎙️ The diameter is the longest straight line you can draw inside a circle. It always cuts through the centre. If you know the radius, you can easily find the diameter by doubling it, and vice-versa.

Bicycle Wheel: Centre, Radius, Diameter

  • Hub of the wheel is the Centre.
  • Spokes are like Radii.
  • The entire wheel's width, passing through the hub, is the Diameter.

🎙️ Look at a bicycle wheel. The central part, where all the spokes meet, is the centre. Each spoke acts like a radius. If you measure the distance across the wheel, passing through the hub, that's the diameter. It's a perfect real-world example!

What is a Chord?

  • A line segment connecting any two points on the circle.
  • It does NOT necessarily pass through the centre.
  • The diameter is the longest chord of a circle.

🎙️ A chord is simply a straight line that joins any two points on the boundary of a circle. It can be short or long. The special thing to remember is that the diameter is also a chord, and it's the longest one because it passes through the centre.

Exploring Arcs

  • A continuous piece of a circle's boundary.
  • Like a curved part of the circle.
  • Can be minor (smaller) or major (larger).

🎙️ An arc is just a part of the circle's circumference. Imagine cutting a piece of string that follows the curve of the circle – that's an arc! We call it a minor arc if it's shorter and a major arc if it's longer.

Pizza Slice: Arc and Chord

  • The curved crust of a slice is an Arc.
  • If you cut straight across the base of the slice, that's a Chord.
  • A whole pizza is a circle, slices show these parts.

🎙️ Next time you eat pizza, notice the geometry! The curved outer edge of your slice is a perfect example of an arc. If you were to draw a straight line connecting the two points where the crust meets the cut edges, that would be a chord.

Understanding Segments

  • The region enclosed by a chord and its corresponding arc.
  • Divides the circle into two parts.
  • Minor segment (smaller) and Major segment (larger).

🎙️ When you draw a chord inside a circle, it cuts the circle into two regions. Each region is called a segment. The smaller region is the minor segment, and the larger one is the major segment.

What is a Sector?

  • The region enclosed by two radii and the arc between them.
  • Looks like a 'slice' of pizza or pie.
  • Minor sector (smaller angle) and Major sector (larger angle).

🎙️ A sector is like a slice of cake! It's formed by two radii that extend from the centre to the circumference, and the arc that connects their endpoints. Similar to segments, we have minor and major sectors.

Segment vs. Sector

  • Segment: Formed by a chord and an arc.
  • Sector: Formed by two radii and an arc.
  • Both divide the circle into two regions.

🎙️ It's easy to confuse segments and sectors, but remember the key difference: a segment uses a chord, while a sector uses two radii. Both are important regions within a circle, so make sure you understand which is which!

Theorem 1: Chords and Distance

  • Equal chords of a circle (or congruent circles) are equidistant from the centre.
  • If AB = CD, then OX = OY (where OX ⊥ AB, OY ⊥ CD).
  • This means they are the same distance away from the middle.

🎙️ This theorem tells us that if two chords have the same length, they will be exactly the same distance from the centre of the circle. It's a fundamental property that helps us solve many problems related to chords.

⚖️
Balanced Distance

Worked Example: Equal Chords

  • In a circle, two parallel chords of lengths 10 cm and 24 cm are on opposite sides of the centre.
  • If the radius is 13 cm, find the distance between the chords.
  • Steps: Use Pythagoras theorem with radius and half-chord.

🎙️ Let's apply this. For a 10cm chord, half is 5cm. With radius 13cm, distance from center is √(13²-5²)=12cm. For 24cm chord, half is 12cm. Distance from center is √(13²-12²)=5cm. Total distance = 12+5 = 17cm.

1Find dist for 10cm chord (x)
2Find dist for 24cm chord (y)
3Add x + y for total distance

Theorem 2: Perpendicular Bisector

  • The perpendicular from the centre of a circle to a chord bisects the chord.
  • If OM ⊥ AB, then AM = MB.
  • It cuts the chord into two equal halves.

🎙️ This is another crucial theorem. If you drop a perpendicular line from the centre straight down to a chord, it will always divide that chord into two perfectly equal parts. This property is very useful in calculations.

📐
Right Angle Split

Worked Example: Perpendicular Bisector

  • A chord of length 16 cm is drawn in a circle of radius 10 cm.
  • Find the distance of the chord from the centre.
  • Steps: Use Pythagoras theorem with radius and half-chord.

🎙️ Here, the chord is 16 cm, so half of it is 8 cm. The radius is 10 cm. We have a right-angled triangle with hypotenuse 10 and one leg 8. Using Pythagoras, the other leg (distance from centre) is √(10² - 8²) = √(100 - 64) = √36 = 6 cm.

1Half chord length (16/2 = 8cm)
2Form right triangle with radius
3Calculate distance using Pythagoras

Theorem 3: Angles at Centre

  • Equal chords of a circle subtend equal angles at the centre.
  • If AB = CD, then ∠AOB = ∠COD.
  • This means chords of the same length create the same angle from the centre.

🎙️ This theorem connects chord length to the angle formed at the centre. If two chords are equal in length, the 'slice' they cut out from the centre will have the same angle. This is a powerful concept for proving congruency.

➕

Worked Example: Angles at Centre

  • In a circle with centre O, chords PQ and RS are equal.
  • If ∠POQ = 70°, find ∠ROS.
  • Steps: Apply Theorem 3 directly.

🎙️ This is a direct application of the theorem. Since chords PQ and RS are equal, they must subtend equal angles at the centre. Therefore, if ∠POQ is 70°, then ∠ROS will also be 70°. Simple, right?

1Identify equal chords (PQ = RS)
2Recall Theorem 3 (equal chords subtend equal angles)
3Conclude ∠ROS = ∠POQ

Cyclic Quadrilateral

  • A quadrilateral whose all four vertices lie on the circumference of a circle.
  • Opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral are supplementary (add up to 180°).
  • Example: A square or rectangle inscribed in a circle.

🎙️ A cyclic quadrilateral is a special type of four-sided figure where all its corners touch the circle's boundary. The most important property to remember is that its opposite angles always add up to 180 degrees. This is a very common question in exams!

Clock Face: Cyclic Points

  • The numbers 12, 3, 6, 9 on a clock face can be considered vertices.
  • These points all lie on the circle of the clock.
  • The entire clock face is a circular boundary.

🎙️ Think about a standard wall clock. The numbers 12, 3, 6, and 9 are points on the circular edge. If you connect them, you form a quadrilateral whose vertices lie on the circle, making it a cyclic quadrilateral.

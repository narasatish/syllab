Rational Numbers (Q)

  • Numbers that can be expressed in the form p/q, where p and q are integers and q ≠ 0.
  • Examples: 1/2, -3/4, 5 (which is 5/1), 0.25 (which is 1/4).
  • Represented by the symbol 'Q' (from 'quotient').

🎙️ Rational numbers are essentially fractions. Any number that can be written as a fraction, where both the numerator and denominator are integers and the denominator is not zero, is a rational number. All integers are also rational numbers because they can be written as p/1.

Properties of Rational Numbers

  • Closure: Sum, difference, product, and quotient (except by zero) of two rational numbers is rational.
  • Commutativity: a+b = b+a and a×b = b×a.
  • Associativity: (a+b)+c = a+(b+c) and (a×b)×c = a×(b×c).

🎙️ Rational numbers behave nicely under basic operations. If you add two rational numbers, you always get another rational number. The order of addition or multiplication doesn't change the result, and grouping doesn't matter either. These properties make them very useful.

Worked Example: Adding Rational Numbers

  • Example: Add 1/3 and 1/2.
  • Find a common denominator: LCM of 3 and 2 is 6.
  • 1/3 = 2/6, 1/2 = 3/6.
  • 2/6 + 3/6 = 5/6.

🎙️ Let's quickly review how to add rational numbers. To add 1/3 and 1/2, we first need to make their denominators the same. The least common multiple of 3 and 2 is 6. So, 1/3 becomes 2/6 and 1/2 becomes 3/6. Adding them gives us 5/6, which is also a rational number.

Irrational Numbers

  • Numbers that cannot be expressed in the form p/q.
  • Their decimal expansion is non-terminating and non-repeating.
  • Examples: √2, √3, π (pi).

🎙️ Now we come to numbers that are a bit more 'mysterious'. Irrational numbers cannot be written as a simple fraction. When you write them as decimals, they go on forever without any repeating pattern. Think of them as having an infinite, unique decimal tail.

Irrational Numbers: Real-life Example

🎙️ You might have encountered pi (π) when calculating the area or circumference of a circle. Its value, approximately 3.14159..., never ends and never repeats. Similarly, the diagonal of a square with side 1 unit is exactly √2, another irrational number.

Representing √2 on the Number Line

  • Draw a number line and mark point O (0) and A (1).
  • At A, draw a perpendicular AB of length 1 unit.
  • Join OB. By Pythagoras theorem, OB = √2.
  • With O as center and OB as radius, draw an arc intersecting the number line at P. P represents √2.

🎙️ Even though irrational numbers have endless decimals, we can still locate them precisely on a number line. For √2, we use the Pythagorean theorem. By constructing a right-angled triangle with sides of length 1 unit, the hypotenuse will be √2, which we can then transfer to the number line using a compass.

Worked Example: Locating √3 on Number Line

  • First, locate √2 on the number line (as done previously).
  • At the point representing √2, draw a perpendicular of length 1 unit.
  • Connect the origin (0) to the end of this perpendicular.
  • This new segment has length √3. Use a compass to mark √3 on the number line.

🎙️ To locate √3, we can build upon our knowledge of locating √2. Once √2 is marked, we construct another right-angled triangle, this time with a base of √2 and a height of 1. The hypotenuse of this triangle will be √3, which can then be transferred to the number line.

Decimal Expansions of Real Numbers

  • Terminating: Decimal ends after a finite number of digits (e.g., 0.5, 2.75). These are rational.
  • Non-terminating Repeating: Decimal goes on forever but has a repeating block of digits (e.g., 0.333..., 1.2727...). These are rational.
  • Non-terminating Non-repeating: Decimal goes on forever with no repeating pattern (e.g., √2, π). These are irrational.

🎙️ The decimal form of a number tells us a lot about its type. Rational numbers either have decimals that stop (terminate) or decimals that repeat a pattern. Irrational numbers are the ones whose decimal expansions never stop and never show a repeating pattern.

Converting Non-terminating Repeating Decimals to p/q form

  • Let x equal the given decimal.
  • Multiply x by 10^k, where k is the number of repeating digits.
  • Subtract the original equation (x) from the new equation.
  • Solve the resulting simple equation for x to get the p/q form.

🎙️ This is a very important skill. We can take a non-terminating but repeating decimal, like 0.333..., and convert it back into its rational (p/q) form. The trick is to use multiplication by powers of 10 and then subtract the original number to eliminate the repeating part.

Worked Example: Convert 0.6̅ to p/q

  • Let x = 0.666... (Equation 1)
  • Multiply by 10: 10x = 6.666... (Equation 2)
  • Subtract (1) from (2): 10x - x = 6.666... - 0.666...
  • 9x = 6 => x = 6/9 = 2/3.

🎙️ Let's work through an example. If we have 0.6 with a bar over it, meaning 0.666..., we set it equal to x. Since one digit is repeating, we multiply by 10. Subtracting the original equation from the new one gives us 9x = 6, which simplifies to x = 2/3. So, 0.6 bar is the same as 2/3.

Operations on Real Numbers

  • Addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division can be performed on real numbers.
  • Rational numbers follow standard arithmetic rules.
  • Operations with irrational numbers often result in irrational numbers (e.g., √2 + √3 is irrational), but sometimes can result in rational numbers (e.g., √2 × √2 = 2).

🎙️ Just like with integers and rational numbers, we can add, subtract, multiply, and divide real numbers. When dealing with irrational numbers, be careful! Adding two irrational numbers might give another irrational number, but sometimes multiplying them can yield a rational number, like when multiplying a square root by itself.

Recap: Our Number System Journey

  • Natural Numbers (N): Counting numbers (1, 2, 3...).
  • Whole Numbers (W): Natural numbers + zero (0, 1, 2...).
  • Integers (Z): Whole numbers + negatives (...-1, 0, 1...).
  • Rational Numbers (Q): p/q form (1/2, -3/4, 5). Terminating/repeating decimals.
  • Irrational Numbers: Non-terminating, non-repeating decimals (√2, π).
  • Real Numbers (R): All rational and irrational numbers.

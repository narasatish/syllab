What Exactly is a Polynomial?

  • A special type of algebraic expression.
  • Exponents of variables must be whole numbers (0, 1, 2, 3...).
  • No variables in the denominator or under a square root.

🎙️ A polynomial is not just any algebraic expression; it has specific rules. The most important rule is that the powers of the variables must always be whole numbers. This means no fractions or negative numbers as exponents!

Building Blocks: Terms, Coefficients, Variables

  • Terms: Parts separated by + or -.
  • Coefficients: Numerical part multiplying the variable.
  • Variables: Letters representing unknown values.

🎙️ Let's break down a polynomial like `5x² + 3x + 7`. Here, `5x²`, `3x`, and `7` are the terms. The numbers `5` and `3` are coefficients, and `x` is our variable. Every part plays a crucial role!

Is It a Polynomial? Let's Check!

  • `4x² + 2x - 1` : Yes (whole number exponents).
  • `3√x + 5` : No (x has power 1/2).
  • `2/x + 7` : No (x has power -1).

🎙️ It's important to be able to identify a polynomial. Remember, the key is to look at the exponents of the variables. If they are all whole numbers, it's a polynomial. If you see square roots of variables or variables in the denominator, it's not!

Understanding the Degree of a Polynomial

  • The highest power of the variable in a polynomial.
  • For `5x³ + 2x² + 7x - 1`, the highest power is 3.
  • So, the degree is 3.

🎙️ The degree of a polynomial tells us its 'highest level'. It's simply the largest exponent you find on any variable in the polynomial. This helps us classify and understand the behavior of different polynomials.

⬆️
Highest Power

Classifying Polynomials by Degree

  • Degree 1: Linear Polynomial (e.g., `2x + 3`)
  • Degree 2: Quadratic Polynomial (e.g., `x² - 5x + 6`)
  • Degree 3: Cubic Polynomial (e.g., `4x³ + x² - 2x + 1`)

🎙️ Polynomials are often classified by their degree. A degree 1 polynomial is 'linear', degree 2 is 'quadratic', and degree 3 is 'cubic'. These names help us understand their graphical shapes and properties.

Classifying Polynomials by Number of Terms

  • One term: Monomial (e.g., `5x²`)
  • Two terms: Binomial (e.g., `3x + 4`)

🎙️ We can also classify polynomials based on how many terms they have. A single-term polynomial is a monomial, two terms make a binomial, and three terms form a trinomial. These categories simplify our discussions.

What About the Zero Polynomial?

  • The polynomial `P(x) = 0` is the zero polynomial.
  • Its degree is undefined.
  • It has no non-zero terms.

🎙️ The zero polynomial is a special case. It's simply `P(x) = 0`. Unlike other polynomials, its degree is not defined, as there's no variable with a specific highest power. It's important to remember this distinction.

Constant Polynomials: Just a Number!

  • A polynomial with only a constant term (e.g., `P(x) = 5`).
  • It can be written as `5x⁰`.
  • The degree of a non-zero constant polynomial is 0.

🎙️ A constant polynomial is just a number, like 7 or -3. We can think of it as `7x⁰`, because `x⁰` is 1. Since the highest power of the variable (even if hidden) is 0, its degree is 0.

Finding the Zeroes of a Polynomial

  • A 'zero' of a polynomial `P(x)` is a value of 'x'.
  • When you substitute this 'x' into `P(x)`, the result is 0.
  • Also called 'roots' of the polynomial equation.

🎙️ Finding the zeroes of a polynomial is like finding the special input values that make the polynomial 'disappear' or become zero. These values are incredibly important in solving equations and understanding functions.

Let's Find a Zero! (Linear Example)

  • Consider `P(x) = 2x - 4`.
  • To find the zero, set `P(x) = 0`.

The Remainder Theorem: A Shortcut!

  • When a polynomial `P(x)` is divided by `(x - a)`.
  • The remainder is `P(a)`.
  • No need for long division!

🎙️ The Remainder Theorem is a powerful shortcut! Instead of doing lengthy polynomial division, you can simply substitute the value 'a' into the polynomial to find the remainder when divided by `(x - a)`. It saves a lot of time!

➗
Division Shortcut

The Factor Theorem: A Special Remainder!

  • A special case of the Remainder Theorem.
  • If `P(a) = 0`, then `(x - a)` is a factor of `P(x)`.
  • If `(x - a)` is a factor, then `P(a) = 0`.

🎙️ The Factor Theorem builds on the Remainder Theorem. If the remainder `P(a)` turns out to be zero, it means `(x - a)` divides the polynomial perfectly, making it a factor. This is extremely useful for factorizing polynomials.

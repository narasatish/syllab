Frequency Distribution

  • A frequency distribution shows how often each value appears.

🎙️ Frequency simply means 'how many times'. If 5 students scored 80 marks, then the frequency of 80 marks is 5. A frequency distribution table lists each value and its frequency.

Grouped Frequency Distribution

  • When data has many different values, a simple frequency table is too long.
  • We group data into class intervals.
  • This makes the data more manageable and easier to interpret.

🎙️ Imagine collecting the heights of 100 students. If everyone has a slightly different height, your simple frequency table would be huge! Grouping data into ranges, like '150-155 cm', makes it much more practical.

Terms for Grouped Data

  • Class Interval: A range of values (e.g., 0-10, 10-20).
  • Lower Limit: The smallest value in a class interval.
  • Upper Limit: The largest value in a class interval.
  • Class Size/Width: The difference between the upper and lower limits.

🎙️ These terms are important for understanding grouped data. For the interval 10-20, 10 is the lower limit and 20 is the upper limit. The class size is 20-10 = 10.

Bar Graphs

  • Bars are of uniform width and separated by equal gaps.
  • The height of each bar represents its frequency.

Histograms

  • Used for continuous data (grouped frequency distribution).
  • Bars are of uniform width and have NO gaps between them.
  • The area of each bar is proportional to its frequency.

🎙️ Histograms are like bar graphs for grouped data. Since the data is continuous, the bars touch each other to show that the values flow from one interval to the next without interruption.

Frequency Polygons

  • Another way to represent grouped frequency distributions.
  • Points are plotted at the mid-points of the upper side of each class interval.
  • These points are then joined by line segments to form a polygon.

🎙️ Frequency polygons give us a smooth curve-like representation of data. We find the middle of each bar in a histogram and connect these midpoints. It helps in understanding the shape of the distribution.

Measures of Central Tendency

  • These are single values that describe the 'center' or 'typical' value of a dataset.
  • They help us summarize a large set of data with one number.
  • The three main measures are Mean, Median, and Mode.

🎙️ Imagine you have many test scores. Instead of listing all of them, wouldn't it be nice to have one number that tells you how the class performed on average? That's what measures of central tendency do.

Mean (Average)

  • The sum of all observations divided by the total number of observations.
  • It's the most commonly used average.
  • Formula: Mean = (Sum of all observations) / (Total number of observations).

🎙️ The mean is what you usually think of as 'average'. If you have marks 10, 20, 30, their sum is 60. There are 3 marks, so the mean is 60 divided by 3, which is 20.

➕
Sum and Divide

Median (Middle Value)

  • The middle value of a dataset when arranged in ascending or descending order.
  • If the number of observations (n) is odd, Median = ((n+1)/2)th observation.
  • If n is even, Median = Average of (n/2)th and ((n/2)+1)th observations.

🎙️ To find the median, always arrange your data first! It's the exact middle point. If you have an even number of values, you take the average of the two numbers in the middle.

Mode (Most Frequent Value)

  • The observation that occurs most frequently in a dataset.
  • A dataset can have one mode (unimodal), multiple modes (multimodal), or no mode.
  • It tells us the most popular or common item.

🎙️ The mode is simply the value that appears most often. Think of it like the 'fashion' of the data – what's most common. If 5 students scored 70 and others scored less, then 70 is the mode.

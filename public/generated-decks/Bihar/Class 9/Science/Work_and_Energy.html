What is Work? Everyday vs. Scientific

  • Everyday: Any mental or physical effort.
  • Example: Studying for an exam, pushing a wall.
  • Scientific: Specific conditions must be met.

🎙️ In our daily language, 'work' can mean anything from studying to doing chores. However, in physics, the definition is much stricter. Simply putting in effort doesn't always count as 'work' scientifically.

💪
Effort vs. Result

Scientific Definition of Work

  • Work is done when a force causes displacement.
  • The object must move.
  • The movement must be in the direction of the applied force.

🎙️ For work to be done in science, two key things must happen: you need to apply a force, and that force must make the object move. If you push a wall and it doesn't move, no work is done, even if you're tired!

➡️
Force & Displacement
4 / 25

Conditions for Work to be Done

  • A force must act on an object.
  • The object must be displaced.
  • The force and displacement must be in the same direction (or have a component in that direction).

🎙️ Think of it like a checklist. Does a force act? Yes. Does the object move? Yes. Is the movement in the direction of the force? Yes. If all three are checked, then work is done!

✅
Checklist for Work

Formula for Work Done

  • Work (W) = Force (F) × Displacement (d)
  • W = F × d
  • This applies when force is in the direction of displacement.

🎙️ The formula for work is quite simple: multiply the force applied by the distance the object moves. Remember, the force and displacement must be aligned for this basic formula.

✖️
Force times Displacement

Units of Work: Joule

  • The S.I. unit of work is Joule (J).
  • 1 Joule is the work done when a force of 1 Newton moves an object by 1 meter.
  • 1 J = 1 N × 1 m.

🎙️ Just like we measure length in meters or mass in kilograms, work is measured in Joules. One Joule is a very specific amount of work, making it easy to compare different tasks.

Types of Work: Positive, Negative, Zero

  • Positive Work: Force and displacement in same direction.
  • Negative Work: Force acts opposite to displacement.
  • Zero Work: Force is perpendicular to displacement, or no displacement.

🎙️ Work isn't always 'good' work! It can be positive, negative, or even zero, depending on the direction of the force relative to the object's movement. Let's see examples for each.

↔️
Direction Matters

Example: Positive Work

  • Pushing a trolley forward.
  • The force you apply and the trolley's movement are in the same direction.
  • Work done by you is positive.

🎙️ When you push a shopping trolley, your force moves it in the direction you're pushing. This is a clear example of positive work, where your effort directly contributes to the object's motion.

Example: Negative Work

  • Friction acting on a moving car.
  • Friction force acts opposite to the car's motion.
  • Work done by friction is negative.

🎙️ Imagine a car braking. The friction from the road acts against the car's movement. This 'opposing' work is called negative work. It slows things down rather than speeding them up.

🚧🚧🚧
Friction opposing motion

Example: Zero Work

  • Carrying a heavy load horizontally.
  • Force of gravity acts downwards, displacement is horizontal.
  • The force and displacement are perpendicular (90 degrees).

🎙️ When you carry a heavy bag and walk on a flat road, you're applying an upward force to hold the bag, but your displacement is horizontal. Since the force and displacement are perpendicular, no work is done by your lifting force on the bag.

What is Energy?

  • Energy is the capacity to do work.
  • Without energy, no work can be done.
  • Everything that does work possesses energy.

🎙️ If work is the 'action', then energy is the 'ability' to perform that action. You need energy to push a trolley, to lift a bag, or even to think! It's the fundamental resource for all activities.

Forms of Energy

  • Energy exists in many forms.
  • Examples: Kinetic, Potential, Heat, Light, Sound, Electrical, Chemical.
  • All these forms can be converted into one another.

🎙️ Energy is like a chameleon, constantly changing its appearance. From the light of a bulb to the heat from a fire, it's all energy, just in different forms. We'll focus on two main mechanical forms today.

🌈

Kinetic Energy (KE)

  • Energy possessed by an object due to its motion.
  • Any moving object has kinetic energy.
  • Example: A rolling ball, a flying bird, flowing water.

🎙️ Kinetic energy is all about movement. If something is moving, it has kinetic energy. The faster or heavier an object is, the more kinetic energy it possesses.

💨
Energy of Motion
15 / 25

Formula for Kinetic Energy

  • Kinetic Energy (KE) = 1/2 × mass (m) × velocity (v)²
  • KE = 1/2 mv²
  • Units: Joule (J).

🎙️ This formula shows that kinetic energy depends on both the mass of the object and its speed. Notice that velocity is squared, meaning speed has a much bigger impact on kinetic energy than mass.

➗
Half mass velocity squared

Example: Kinetic Energy

  • A car moving on the road.
  • The car possesses kinetic energy due to its speed.
  • The faster it moves, the more kinetic energy it has.

🎙️ A moving car is a classic example. Its kinetic energy allows it to do work, like pushing air aside or even causing damage in a collision. That's why high speeds are so dangerous.

Potential Energy (PE)

  • Energy possessed by an object due to its position or state.
  • It's 'stored' energy, ready to be released.
  • Example: A stretched rubber band, water in a dam.

🎙️ Unlike kinetic energy, potential energy isn't about movement, but about potential for movement. It's energy waiting to happen, stored because of where an object is or how it's shaped.

⬆️
Stored Energy

Gravitational Potential Energy (GPE)

  • A specific type of potential energy.
  • Energy stored due to an object's height above the ground.
  • The higher the object, the more GPE it has.

🎙️ This is the most common type of potential energy we study. An object held high has the potential to fall and do work. This potential is due to gravity pulling it downwards.

🌳
Height from Ground

Formula for Gravitational Potential Energy

  • Potential Energy (PE) = mass (m) × acceleration due to gravity (g) × height (h)
  • PE = mgh
  • Units: Joule (J).

🎙️ The formula for GPE tells us that it depends on three factors: the object's mass, the strength of gravity (which is constant on Earth), and its height above a reference point. The higher it is, the more potential energy.

⚖️
Mass, Gravity, Height

Example: Gravitational Potential Energy

  • Water stored in a dam at a height.
  • This water has GPE due to its elevation.
  • It can be released to turn turbines and generate electricity.

🎙️ Hydroelectric power plants are a perfect example. The water held high in a dam stores immense gravitational potential energy. When released, this potential energy converts into kinetic energy, then into electrical energy.

Law of Conservation of Energy

  • Energy can neither be created nor destroyed.
  • It can only be transformed from one form to another.
  • The total energy in an isolated system remains constant.

🎙️ This is one of the most fundamental laws in physics. It means we can't 'make' or 'destroy' energy, only change its form. This principle is crucial for understanding how machines work and how energy is used around us.

🔄
Energy Transformation

Example: Energy Transformation (Pendulum)

  • At its highest point, a pendulum has maximum Potential Energy (PE).
  • As it swings down, PE converts to Kinetic Energy (KE).
  • At its lowest point, it has maximum KE and minimum PE.

🎙️ A swinging pendulum beautifully demonstrates energy transformation. It continuously exchanges potential and kinetic energy, showing how energy changes forms while the total amount remains the same, neglecting air resistance.

Power: The Rate of Doing Work

  • Power is how fast work is done or energy is transferred.
  • Power (P) = Work (W) / Time (t)
  • S.I. unit of power is Watt (W).

🎙️ While work tells us 'how much' is done, power tells us 'how quickly' it's done. A powerful machine can do the same amount of work as a less powerful one, but in much less time. Think of a strong person lifting weights quickly versus slowly.

Recap: Work and Energy

  • Work: Force × Displacement (W=Fd). Unit: Joule.
  • Energy: Capacity to do work. Unit: Joule.
  • Kinetic Energy (KE): Energy of motion (1/2 mv²).
  • Potential Energy (PE): Stored energy (mgh).
  • Conservation of Energy: Energy transforms, not created/destroyed.

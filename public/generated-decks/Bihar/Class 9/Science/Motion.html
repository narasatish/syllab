What is Motion?

  • An object is in motion if its position changes.
  • This change is observed with respect to a reference point.
  • Without a reference point, motion cannot be determined.

🎙️ Simply put, an object is in motion if it changes its location over time. But this change isn't absolute; it's always relative to something else. Think about it: how do you know something has moved?

Motion is Relative

  • An object can be in motion for one observer.
  • The same object can be at rest for another observer.
  • It depends on the chosen reference point.

🎙️ Imagine you are sitting in a moving bus. For you, your co-passenger is at rest. But for someone standing outside on the road, both you and your co-passenger are in motion. This shows that motion is not absolute, but relative to the observer.

How Do We Describe Motion?

  • We need to define the position of an object.
  • Use a reference point (origin) and a coordinate system.
  • Distance and displacement are key terms.

🎙️ To properly describe motion, we need a starting point, like a landmark or a specific location. This is called the reference point or origin. From there, we can use coordinates to pinpoint an object's exact position.

Distance: The Path Travelled

  • Total length of the path covered by an object.
  • It is a scalar quantity (only magnitude).
  • Always positive and never zero for a moving object.

🎙️ Distance is simply how much ground an object has covered during its motion. If you walk from your home to school, the total length of the road you take is the distance. It doesn't care about direction, only the total path.

Displacement: Shortest Straight Path

  • The shortest straight-line distance between initial and final positions.
  • It is a vector quantity (magnitude and direction).
  • Can be positive, negative, or zero.

🎙️ Displacement is a more precise way to describe a change in position. It's the straight-line distance from where you started to where you ended up, along with the direction. If you walk in a circle and return to your starting point, your displacement is zero.

Scalar Quantities

  • Quantities that have only magnitude (size).
  • No direction associated with them.
  • Examples: Distance, speed, mass, time, temperature.

🎙️ Scalar quantities are straightforward; they tell you 'how much' or 'how many' without needing to specify a direction. When you say '5 kg of rice,' the direction isn't important.

🔢
Only Magnitude
9 / 25

Vector Quantities

  • Quantities that have both magnitude and direction.
  • Represented by an arrow pointing in the direction.
  • Examples: Displacement, velocity, acceleration, force.

🎙️ Vector quantities are crucial in physics because direction matters. If you say 'walk 5 meters,' it's incomplete. But 'walk 5 meters North' gives a clear instruction using a vector quantity.

Speed: How Fast an Object Moves

  • Rate at which an object covers distance.
  • Scalar quantity: only magnitude.
  • Formula: Speed = Distance / Time. SI unit: m/s.

🎙️ Speed tells us how quickly an object is moving. If a car covers a large distance in a short time, its speed is high. We often measure it in kilometers per hour or meters per second.

⏱️
Distance per Time
12 / 25

Velocity: Speed with Direction

  • Rate of change of displacement.
  • Vector quantity: magnitude and direction.
  • Formula: Velocity = Displacement / Time. SI unit: m/s.

🎙️ Velocity is like speed, but it also tells us which way the object is going. If a car is moving at 60 km/h towards the East, that's its velocity. A change in direction alone means a change in velocity.

Speed vs. Velocity

  • Speed: Scalar, depends on distance.
  • Velocity: Vector, depends on displacement.
  • Velocity can be zero even if speed is not (e.g., circular path).

🎙️ Here's the key difference: a car can have a constant speed but a changing velocity if it's turning. For example, a car going around a roundabout at a steady 30 km/h has constant speed but constantly changing velocity.

Uniform Motion

  • An object covers equal distances in equal intervals of time.
  • Moves along a straight line with constant speed.
  • Velocity remains constant throughout the motion.

🎙️ Imagine a car moving on a straight, empty road at a constant speed, say 50 km/h. It covers 50 km every hour. This is an example of uniform motion, where both speed and direction are unchanging.

Non-Uniform Motion

  • An object covers unequal distances in equal intervals of time.
  • Speed or direction (or both) changes over time.
  • Most real-life motions are non-uniform.

🎙️ When you drive in city traffic, you speed up, slow down, and turn. This is a perfect example of non-uniform motion. The speed and/or direction are constantly changing, making it more complex than uniform motion.

Acceleration: Change in Velocity

  • Rate of change of velocity.
  • Vector quantity: magnitude and direction.
  • Formula: Acceleration = (Final Velocity - Initial Velocity) / Time. SI unit: m/s².

🎙️ Acceleration tells us how quickly an object's velocity is changing. If a car speeds up, it's accelerating. If it slows down, it's also accelerating, but in the opposite direction. Even changing direction at constant speed is acceleration!

🚀
Change in Velocity

Positive, Negative, Zero Acceleration

  • Positive: Velocity increases (speeding up).
  • Negative (Deceleration): Velocity decreases (slowing down).
  • Zero: Velocity is constant (uniform motion).

🎙️ Understanding these types is key. When you press the accelerator pedal, you get positive acceleration. When you apply brakes, it's negative acceleration. If you're cruising steadily, your acceleration is zero.

Average Speed and Average Velocity

  • Average Speed = Total Distance / Total Time.
  • Average Velocity = Total Displacement / Total Time.
  • Used for non-uniform motion over a period.

🎙️ For non-uniform motion, we often talk about average speed or velocity. This gives us an overall idea of the motion, even if the speed or velocity changed many times during the journey.

Worked Example: Average Speed

  • A car travels 100 km in 2 hours.
  • Then, it travels another 50 km in 1 hour.
  • Calculate its average speed.

🎙️ Let's apply our formula. First, find the total distance covered. Then, find the total time taken. Finally, divide the total distance by the total time to get the average speed.

1Total Distance = 100 + 50 = 150 km
2Total Time = 2 + 1 = 3 hours
3Avg Speed = 150 km / 3 hrs = 50 km/h

Distance-Time Graphs: Uniform Motion

  • For uniform motion, it's a straight line.
  • Slope of the line gives the speed.
  • A horizontal line means the object is at rest.

🎙️ When an object moves uniformly, its distance-time graph is a straight line sloping upwards. The steeper the slope, the higher the speed. If the object is not moving, the line is flat.

Equations of Motion

  • Three main equations for uniformly accelerated motion.
  • Relate initial velocity (u), final velocity (v), acceleration (a), time (t), and displacement (s).
  • v = u + at; s = ut + ½at²; v² = u² + 2as.

🎙️ These three equations are fundamental for solving problems involving objects moving with constant acceleration. We will learn how to derive and apply them in detail in future lessons.

What is Force?

  • A push or a pull.
  • Can change an object's state of motion.
  • Can change an object's shape or size.

🎙️ Imagine you want to move a toy car. You either push it or pull it. That push or pull is what we call force. Force is an interaction that, when unopposed, will change the motion of an object.

Types of Force: Balanced vs Unbalanced

  • Balanced: Net force is zero, no change in motion.
  • Unbalanced: Net force is not zero, causes motion/change.

🎙️ When multiple forces act on an object, we look at the 'net force'. If all forces cancel each other out, it's a balanced force, and the object's motion doesn't change. If there's a net force, it's unbalanced, and the object will accelerate.

Real-life Example: Tug-of-War

  • If teams pull with equal force, the rope doesn't move (balanced).

🎙️ Think about a tug-of-war game. If both teams pull with the same strength, the rope stays still. The forces are balanced. But as soon as one team pulls harder, the forces become unbalanced, and the rope starts moving.

Galileo's Experiment: Understanding Motion

  • Rolling ball on an inclined plane.
  • Observed how speed changed.
  • Concluded objects keep moving without external force.

🎙️ Galileo was a brilliant scientist who challenged old ideas. He showed that if a ball rolls down an incline, it speeds up. If it rolls up, it slows down. On a perfectly flat, frictionless surface, it would keep moving forever!

Inertia: Resistance to Change

  • The natural tendency of an object to resist changes in its state of motion.
  • Objects at rest want to stay at rest.
  • Objects in motion want to stay in motion.

🎙️ Inertia is like an object's stubbornness. If it's sitting still, it wants to keep sitting still. If it's moving, it wants to keep moving at the same speed and in the same direction. It resists any force trying to change that.

🧱
Resisting Motion

Types of Inertia

  • Inertia of Rest: Object resists starting motion.
  • Inertia of Motion: Object resists stopping or changing speed.
  • Inertia of Direction: Object resists changing direction.

🎙️ Inertia isn't just one thing. It applies to objects wanting to stay at rest, wanting to continue moving, and wanting to continue moving in a straight line. All objects possess inertia, proportional to their mass.

Newton's First Law: The Law of Inertia

  • An object at rest stays at rest, and an object in motion stays in motion with the same speed and in the same direction unless acted upon by an unbalanced force.

🎙️ This law basically formalizes Galileo's ideas and our understanding of inertia. It tells us that objects are 'lazy' – they prefer to continue doing whatever they are doing unless a strong, unbalanced force makes them change.

🎙️ Consider a football lying on the ground. It won't move unless you kick it – that's an unbalanced force. Once it's moving, it will eventually stop due to friction and air resistance, which are also unbalanced forces.

Momentum: Quantity of Motion

  • A measure of 'how much motion' an object has.
  • Depends on both mass and velocity.
  • Vector quantity (has magnitude and direction).

🎙️ Momentum is a very important concept in physics. It's not just about speed; it's also about how heavy an object is. A heavy truck moving slowly can have more momentum than a light car moving fast.

💨
Moving Power

Momentum Formula

  • Momentum (p) = mass (m) × velocity (v)
  • Units: kilogram-meter per second (kg m/s).

🎙️ The formula for momentum is quite straightforward: simply multiply the object's mass by its velocity. Remember that velocity includes both speed and direction, so momentum also has a direction.

🎙️ Let's try an example. If a cricket ball has a mass of 0.15 kg and is bowled at 40 m/s, its momentum is 6 kg m/s. This gives us a numerical value for its 'quantity of motion'.

Newton's Second Law: Force and Acceleration

  • The rate of change of momentum of an object is directly proportional to the applied unbalanced force.
  • Force (F) = mass (m) × acceleration (a).

🎙️ This is one of the most famous laws in physics. It tells us that if you apply a force to an object, it will accelerate. The bigger the force, the bigger the acceleration. But also, the heavier the object, the less it will accelerate for the same force.

➡️
Force causes Acceleration

Catching a Ball: Second Law in Action

  • Pulling hands back while catching a fast ball increases the time over which momentum changes.
  • This reduces the force felt by the hands (F = Δp/Δt).

🎙️ When catching a fast-moving cricket ball, you instinctively pull your hands back. Why? Because you are increasing the time over which the ball's momentum changes to zero, thereby reducing the force exerted on your hands.

Example: Calculating Force

  • A car of mass 1000 kg accelerates at 2 m/s².
  • Calculate the force required.
  • F = m × a = 1000 kg × 2 m/s² = 2000 N.

🎙️ Let's apply the second law. If a car with a mass of 1000 kg needs to accelerate at 2 m/s², the engine must generate a force of 2000 Newtons. The unit of force is the Newton (N).

Newton's Third Law: Action-Reaction

  • To every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction.
  • Forces always occur in pairs.
  • These forces act on different objects.

🎙️ This law is about interactions. Whenever one object exerts a force on another object (action), the second object simultaneously exerts an equal and opposite force back on the first object (reaction). These forces never cancel out because they act on different bodies.

↔️
Action & Reaction

Rocket Launch: Third Law in Action

  • The rocket expels hot gases downwards (action).
  • The gases push the rocket upwards with an equal and opposite force (reaction).

🎙️ A rocket launching into space is a perfect example. It pushes hot gases out of its engine downwards (action). In turn, these gases push the rocket upwards with the same magnitude of force, propelling it into the sky (reaction).

Law of Conservation of Momentum

  • In an isolated system, the total momentum remains constant.
  • Momentum before collision = Momentum after collision.
  • No external unbalanced forces acting on the system.

🎙️ This law states that in a closed system, where no external forces are involved, the total momentum before an event (like a collision) is exactly the same as the total momentum after the event. Momentum is neither lost nor gained.

🔄
Momentum Stays Same

Collision: Conservation of Momentum

  • When two billiard balls collide, their individual momenta change.
  • However, the total momentum of the two-ball system remains the same.

🎙️ Imagine two billiard balls hitting each other. Before the collision, each ball has its own momentum. After they hit, they bounce off and have new individual momenta. But if you add them up, the total momentum of the system is conserved.

What is a Polynomial?

  • An expression with variables, constants, and exponents.
  • Exponents must be whole numbers (0, 1, 2, ...).
  • Operations include addition, subtraction, multiplication.

🎙️ Think of a polynomial as a special kind of algebraic expression. It's like a puzzle made of different pieces: numbers, letters, and powers, all connected by basic math operations.

Variables and Constants

  • Variables: Letters (x, y, a) representing unknown values.
  • Constants: Fixed numerical values (3, -5, 1/2).
  • They are the fundamental parts of any expression.

🎙️ Before we dive deeper, let's quickly review variables and constants. Variables are like placeholders that can change, while constants are numbers that always stay the same.

Terms of a Polynomial

  • Parts of a polynomial separated by '+' or '-' signs.
  • Each term is a product of constants and variables.
  • Example: In 3x² + 2x - 5, the terms are 3x², 2x, and -5.

🎙️ Just like a sentence has words, a polynomial has terms. Each term is a distinct part, and we combine them using addition or subtraction to form the whole polynomial.

Coefficients

  • The numerical factor multiplying the variable part of a term.
  • Example: In 5x², 5 is the coefficient of x².
  • If no number is written, the coefficient is 1 (e.g., x = 1x).

🎙️ The coefficient tells us 'how many' of a particular variable term we have. It's the number that stands right next to the variable.

Degree of a Polynomial

  • The highest power of the variable in a polynomial.
  • For a single variable polynomial.
  • Example: In 4x³ + 2x² - 7, the degree is 3.

🎙️ The degree is a very important characteristic of a polynomial. It's simply the largest exponent you find on any variable in the expression.

Types of Polynomials by Degree

  • Linear Polynomial: Degree 1 (e.g., 2x + 3)

Linear Polynomial

  • A polynomial with degree 1.
  • General form: ax + b, where a ≠ 0.
  • Its graph is always a straight line.

🎙️ A linear polynomial is the simplest type. When you plot its values on a graph, you always get a straight line. Think of a ruler – it's linear!

Quadratic Polynomial

🎙️ Quadratic polynomials are a bit more complex. Their graphs are beautiful curves called parabolas, which you might have seen in bridges or fountains.

Cubic Polynomial

  • A polynomial with degree 3.
  • General form: ax³ + bx² + cx + d, where a ≠ 0.
  • Its graph can have more turns than a quadratic.

🎙️ Cubic polynomials have a degree of three. Their graphs can be quite interesting, often having a 'wiggle' or multiple turning points. They are used in volume calculations and more complex designs.

Value of a Polynomial at a Specific Point

  • To find P(k), substitute 'k' for 'x' in the polynomial.
  • Then simplify the expression.
  • This gives the output of the polynomial for a given input.

🎙️ Finding the value of a polynomial means plugging in a specific number for the variable and then calculating the result. It's like finding the output of a function for a particular input.

Worked Example: Value of P(x)

  • If P(x) = 2x² - 3x + 1, find P(2).
  • Step 1: Substitute x=2: P(2) = 2(2)² - 3(2) + 1.
  • Step 2: Calculate: P(2) = 2(4) - 6 + 1 = 8 - 6 + 1 = 3.

🎙️ Let's walk through an example. If we have P(x) = 2x² - 3x + 1, and we want to find P(2), we simply replace every 'x' with '2' and then do the math carefully.

1P(x) = 2x² - 3x + 1
2P(2) = 2(2)² - 3(2) + 1
3P(2) = 8 - 6 + 1 = 3

Zeros of a Polynomial

  • A zero of P(x) is a value 'k' such that P(k) = 0.
  • It's also called a root of the polynomial.
  • These are the x-values where the graph crosses the x-axis.

🎙️ The zeros of a polynomial are special values of 'x' that make the entire polynomial equal to zero. Geometrically, these are the points where the graph of the polynomial touches or crosses the x-axis.

Geometric Meaning of Zeros (Linear)

  • For a linear polynomial (ax+b), the graph is a straight line.
  • It intersects the x-axis at exactly one point.
  • This point's x-coordinate is the only zero: x = -b/a.

🎙️ When you graph a linear polynomial, it's a straight line. This line will cross the x-axis at only one spot. That x-coordinate is the polynomial's zero.

Geometric Meaning of Zeros (Quadratic)

  • For a quadratic polynomial (ax²+bx+c), the graph is a parabola.
  • It can intersect the x-axis at 0, 1, or 2 points.
  • These intersection points give the 0, 1, or 2 zeros.

🎙️ A quadratic polynomial's graph, a parabola, can behave differently. It might not touch the x-axis at all, or it might touch at one point (a double root), or it might cross at two distinct points.

Relationship between Zeros and Coefficients (Linear)

  • For a linear polynomial P(x) = ax + b.
  • The zero is x = -b/a.
  • Relationship: Zero = -(Constant term) / (Coefficient of x).

🎙️ For a simple linear polynomial, there's a direct formula to find its zero using its coefficients. It's the negative of the constant term divided by the coefficient of 'x'.

Relationship between Zeros and Coefficients (Quadratic)

  • For a quadratic P(x) = ax² + bx + c, let zeros be α and β.
  • Sum of zeros (α + β) = -b/a.
  • Product of zeros (αβ) = c/a.

🎙️ This is a very important relationship for quadratic polynomials. Without even finding the zeros, we can know their sum and product just by looking at the coefficients 'a', 'b', and 'c'.

➕✖️
Sum and Product
20 / 25

Worked Example: Zeros and Coefficients (Quadratic)

  • For P(x) = x² - 5x + 6, find sum and product of zeros.
  • Here a=1, b=-5, c=6.
  • Sum = -(-5)/1 = 5. Product = 6/1 = 6.

🎙️ Let's apply these formulas. For the polynomial x² - 5x + 6, we can quickly find the sum and product of its zeros using the coefficients. You can even verify this by finding the actual zeros (2 and 3).

1P(x) = x² - 5x + 6
2a=1, b=-5, c=6
3Sum = -b/a = 5
4Product = c/a = 6

What are Quadratic Equations?

  • Equations where the highest power of the variable is 2.
  • Standard form: ax² + bx + c = 0.
  • Here, 'a' cannot be zero (a ≠ 0).

🎙️ Now, let's meet our main topic! When the highest power of the variable, say 'x', is 2, we call it a Quadratic Equation. The 'a' term is crucial; if 'a' were zero, it would become a linear equation!

The Meaning of 'Quadratic'

  • Comes from the Latin word 'quadratus', meaning square.
  • Refers to the term with x² (x-squared).
  • Degree of the polynomial is 2.

🎙️ The word 'quadratic' might sound fancy, but it simply points to the 'square' term, x². This means our variable is raised to the power of two, making it a polynomial of degree 2.

What are the Roots (or Zeros)?

  • The values of the variable 'x' that satisfy the equation.
  • These are the solutions to the quadratic equation.
  • A quadratic equation can have at most two roots.

🎙️ Just like linear equations have one solution, quadratic equations can have up to two solutions. We call these solutions 'roots' or 'zeros' because they are the values of 'x' that make the equation true, or make the polynomial equal to zero.

Finding Roots: Method 1 - Factorisation

  • Break down the quadratic expression into two linear factors.
  • Set each factor equal to zero to find the roots.
  • This method is often quick if factors are easily found.

🎙️ The first method we'll learn is factorisation. It involves rewriting the quadratic expression as a product of two simpler linear expressions. Once factored, finding the roots becomes straightforward.

1Standard Form
2Split Middle Term

4Set Factors to Zero
12 / 25

Example: Solving by Factorisation

  • Solve: x² - 5x + 6 = 0
  • Split the middle term: x² - 2x - 3x + 6 = 0
  • Factor by grouping: x(x-2) - 3(x-2) = 0
  • (x-2)(x-3) = 0 => x=2 or x=3

🎙️ Let's walk through an example. Here, we split -5x into -2x and -3x because -2 * -3 = 6 (the product of the first and last terms' coefficients) and -2 + -3 = -5. This allows us to group and factor.

1x² - 5x + 6 = 0
2(x-2)(x-3) = 0
3x-2=0 or x-3=0
4x=2, x=3

Finding Roots: Method 2 - Completing the Square

  • Transform the equation into the form (x + k)² = d.
  • This involves adding a specific constant to both sides.
  • It helps convert any quadratic equation into a perfect square.

🎙️ Completing the square is a powerful method that works for all quadratic equations. The idea is to manipulate the equation so that one side becomes a perfect square trinomial, making it easy to isolate 'x'.

⬜
Making a Perfect Square
15 / 25

Example: Solving by Completing the Square

  • Solve: x² + 6x + 5 = 0
  • Move constant: x² + 6x = -5
  • Add (6/2)² = 9 to both sides: x² + 6x + 9 = -5 + 9
  • (x+3)² = 4 => x+3 = ±2 => x = -3 ± 2

🎙️ Here, we first move the constant term. Then, we add (half of the coefficient of x, squared) to both sides to complete the square. This transforms the left side into a perfect square, (x+3)², allowing us to take the square root.

1x² + 6x + 5 = 0
2(x+3)² = 4
3x+3 = ±2
4x = -1, x = -5

Finding Roots: Method 3 - Quadratic Formula

  • A universal formula to find roots of any quadratic equation.
  • For ax² + bx + c = 0, roots are x = [-b ± √(b²-4ac)] / 2a.
  • This method always works, even when factorisation is difficult.

🎙️ The quadratic formula is your best friend when other methods seem too complex or don't work easily. It's a direct formula that gives you the roots of any quadratic equation, provided 'a' is not zero.

➗

How the Formula Comes From

  • The quadratic formula is derived by applying the 'completing the square' method.
  • It's applied to the general form ax² + bx + c = 0.
  • It saves us from repeating the 'completing the square' steps every time.

🎙️ You don't need to memorize the derivation for now, but it's good to know that this powerful formula isn't magic! It comes directly from applying the completing the square method to the general quadratic equation ax² + bx + c = 0.

Example: Solving using Quadratic Formula

  • Solve: 2x² + 5x - 3 = 0
  • Identify a=2, b=5, c=-3.
  • Substitute into formula: x = [-5 ± √(5²-4*2*-3)] / (2*2)
  • x = [-5 ± √(25+24)] / 4 = [-5 ± √49] / 4

🎙️ Let's apply the formula. First, identify the values of a, b, and c from the equation. Then, carefully substitute them into the formula and simplify. Remember the ± sign gives us two potential roots.

12x² + 5x - 3 = 0
2a=2, b=5, c=-3
3x = [-5 ± √49] / 4
4x = 1/2, x = -3

Nature of Roots: The Discriminant

  • The term b² - 4ac is called the Discriminant (D).
  • It tells us about the nature of the roots without actually solving.
  • It's a crucial part of the quadratic formula.

🎙️ Before even solving, we can know what kind of roots a quadratic equation will have by looking at a special part of the quadratic formula: b² - 4ac. This term, called the Discriminant, is very powerful!

🤔
Root Predictor
20 / 25

What Discriminant Tells Us

  • If D > 0: Two distinct real roots.
  • If D = 0: Two equal real roots.
  • If D < 0: No real roots (roots are imaginary).

🎙️ The value of the discriminant determines the nature of the roots. If it's positive, you get two different real numbers. If it's zero, you get two identical real numbers. If it's negative, there are no real number solutions.

D > 0 (Distinct Real)
D = 0 (Equal Real)
D < 0 (No Real Roots)

Key Takeaways from Quadratic Equations

  • Standard form: ax² + bx + c = 0 (a≠0).
  • Roots are solutions (up to two).
  • Methods: Factorisation, Completing the Square, Quadratic Formula.
  • Discriminant (b²-4ac) tells nature of roots.

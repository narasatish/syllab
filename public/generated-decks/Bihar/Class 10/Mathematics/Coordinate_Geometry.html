The Cartesian Plane

  • Two perpendicular number lines.
  • X-axis (horizontal), Y-axis (vertical).
  • Origin: where axes intersect (0,0).

🎙️ The foundation of coordinate geometry is the Cartesian Plane, named after René Descartes. It's simply two number lines crossing each other at a right angle. The horizontal line is the X-axis, the vertical is the Y-axis, and their meeting point is the Origin.

Quadrants

  • The plane is divided into four regions.
  • Numbered I, II, III, IV counter-clockwise.

🎙️ These two axes divide the entire plane into four distinct regions, which we call quadrants. We number them counter-clockwise, starting from the top-right. The signs of the x and y coordinates tell us which quadrant a point lies in.

Deriving the Distance Formula

  • Uses the Pythagorean Theorem.
  • Form a right-angled triangle.
  • Hypotenuse is the distance between points.

🎙️ The distance formula isn't magic; it comes directly from the famous Pythagorean Theorem! We imagine a right-angled triangle where the line segment connecting our two points is the hypotenuse, and the legs are parallel to the axes.

📐
Pythagoras at Work
11 / 25

Distance Formula: The Formula!

  • Distance between (x1, y1) and (x2, y2).
  • d = √[(x2 - x1)² + (y2 - y1)²].
  • Remember the square root!

🎙️ Here it is, the distance formula! If you have two points, (x1, y1) and (x2, y2), you just plug their coordinates into this equation. Make sure you subtract the x-coordinates, square it, do the same for y, add them, and then take the square root.

✨
The Magic Formula
12 / 25

Worked Example: Distance between (2,3) and (5,7)

  • Let (x1,y1) = (2,3) and (x2,y2) = (5,7).
  • x2 - x1 = 5 - 2 = 3.
  • y2 - y1 = 7 - 3 = 4.
  • d = √[3² + 4²] = √[9 + 16] = √25 = 5 units.

🎙️ Let's apply the formula. For points (2,3) and (5,7), the difference in x is 3, and in y is 4. Squaring and adding gives us 25. The square root of 25 is 5. So, the distance is 5 units.

Section Formula Introduction

  • Dividing a line segment.
  • Finding a point between two others.
  • Ratio of division is key.

🎙️ Sometimes, we don't just want to find the distance between two points, but a specific point that lies *on* the line segment connecting them and divides it in a certain ratio. That's where the Section Formula comes in handy.

✂️
Dividing a Line

Section Formula (Internal)

  • Divides line segment AB in ratio m1:m2.
  • Point P(x,y) lies between A and B.
  • x = (m1x2 + m2x1) / (m1 + m2).
  • y = (m1y2 + m2y1) / (m1 + m2).

🎙️ If a point P divides the line segment joining A(x1, y1) and B(x2, y2) internally in the ratio m1:m2, then its coordinates are given by these two formulas. Notice how m1 multiplies the coordinates of the *second* point (x2, y2) and vice versa.

🔗
Connecting Points
16 / 25

Worked Example: Dividing a Line Segment

  • Points A(1,7) and B(4,-3).
  • Ratio m1:m2 = 2:3.
  • x = (2*4 + 3*1) / (2+3) = (8+3)/5 = 11/5.
  • y = (2*-3 + 3*7) / (2+3) = (-6+21)/5 = 15/5 = 3.
  • Point is (11/5, 3).

🎙️ Let's find the point that divides the segment joining (1,7) and (4,-3) in the ratio 2:3. We substitute the values into the formula. Remember to be careful with signs and fractions. The resulting point is (11/5, 3).

Midpoint Formula

  • Special case of Section Formula.
  • Divides a line segment into two equal parts.
  • Ratio is 1:1 (m1=1, m2=1).
  • Midpoint M = ((x1+x2)/2, (y1+y2)/2).

🎙️ The Midpoint Formula is a very common and simpler application of the Section Formula. It finds the exact middle point of a line segment. Since it divides the segment into two equal parts, the ratio is simply 1:1, which simplifies the formula nicely.

🎯
Center Point
18 / 25

Worked Example: Finding Midpoint

  • Find midpoint of A(4, -6) and B(-2, 4).
  • x-coordinate = (4 + (-2))/2 = 2/2 = 1.
  • y-coordinate = (-6 + 4)/2 = -2/2 = -1.
  • Midpoint is (1, -1).

🎙️ Let's quickly find the midpoint of (4, -6) and (-2, 4). We just average the x-coordinates and average the y-coordinates. It's a straightforward calculation that gives us the point (1, -1) as the exact center.

Area of a Triangle Introduction

  • Finding the area of a triangle.
  • Using the coordinates of its vertices.
  • No need for base and height!

🎙️ Traditionally, you calculate the area of a triangle using base and height. But what if you only have the coordinates of its three vertices? Coordinate geometry provides a powerful formula to calculate the area directly from these coordinates.

🔺
Triangle Shape
20 / 25

Area of a Triangle Formula

  • Vertices (x1, y1), (x2, y2), (x3, y3).
  • Area = 1/2 |x1(y2 - y3) + x2(y3 - y1) + x3(y1 - y2)|
  • Remember the absolute value, area is always positive!

🎙️ This formula might look a bit long, but it's very systematic. Just remember to cycle through the x and y values correctly. The absolute value ensures that our area is always positive, as area cannot be negative.

📝
Complex Calculation
21 / 25

Worked Example: Area of Triangle with Vertices

  • Vertices A(1, -1), B(-4, 6), C(-3, -5).
  • Plug into formula carefully.
  • Area = 1/2 |1(6 - (-5)) + (-4)(-5 - (-1)) + (-3)(-1 - 6)|
  • Area = 1/2 |1(11) - 4(-4) - 3(-7)|
  • Area = 1/2 |11 + 16 + 21| = 1/2 |48| = 24 sq units.

🎙️ Let's apply this formula to a triangle with given vertices. It involves careful substitution and arithmetic, especially with negative numbers. After calculation, we find the area of this triangle is 24 square units.

Collinearity of Points

  • Three or more points lie on the same straight line.
  • If points are collinear, they cannot form a triangle.
  • Area of triangle formed by collinear points is ZERO.

🎙️ An important concept is collinearity: when three or more points lie on the same straight line. If points are collinear, they can't form a triangle. This means if you use the area of a triangle formula for collinear points, the result will always be zero.

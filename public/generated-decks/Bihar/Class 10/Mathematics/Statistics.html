Raw Data and Frequency Distribution

  • Raw data is unorganized.
  • Frequency is how many times an item appears.
  • Frequency distribution organizes data by counting.

🎙️ Imagine you have a basket of fruits: apple, banana, apple, orange, banana, apple, orange, apple. This is raw data. To make it useful, we count how many of each fruit there are. This counting is called frequency, and organizing it is frequency distribution.

Frequency Distribution Table

  • Systematic way to present data.
  • Shows each item and its frequency.
  • Often includes tally marks for easy counting.

🎙️ A frequency distribution table has columns for the data item, tally marks (a simple way to count in groups of five), and the final frequency. It helps us quickly see which values are most common and which are rare in our dataset.

Grouped Frequency Distribution

  • Used when data is very large.
  • Data is grouped into intervals or classes.
  • Each group has a frequency.

🎙️ What if you have the marks of 100 students? Listing each mark and its frequency would be too long. In such cases, we group the marks into intervals, like 0-10, 10-20, etc. This makes the data more manageable and understandable.

Class Interval and Class Size

  • Class Interval: A range of values (e.g., 10-20).
  • Lower Limit: The smallest value in an interval.
  • Upper Limit: The largest value in an interval.
  • Class Size: The difference between upper and lower limits.

🎙️ For an interval like 10-20, 10 is the lower limit and 20 is the upper limit. The class size is 20 - 10 = 10. Understanding these terms is crucial for working with grouped data, as they define the boundaries and width of our data groups.

Measures of Central Tendency

  • Single values that represent the center of data.
  • They give a 'typical' value for the dataset.
  • Mean, Median, and Mode are common measures.

🎙️ Once data is organized, we often want to find a single value that summarizes it. This 'typical' value is called a measure of central tendency. Think of it as finding the 'average' or 'middle' point of your data. We will learn three such measures.

Mean: The Average

  • Most common measure of central tendency.
  • Calculated by summing all values and dividing by the count.
  • Formula: Mean (x̄) = (Sum of all observations) / (Total number of observations).

🎙️ The mean is what we commonly call the average. If you want to find the average marks of your class, you add up all the marks and divide by the number of students. It's a simple yet powerful way to understand the typical value.

➕
Sum & Divide
15 / 25

Example: Calculating Mean (Ungrouped)

  • Find the mean of the following numbers: 5, 8, 10, 12, 15.
  • Sum = 5 + 8 + 10 + 12 + 15 = 50.
  • Count = 5.
  • Mean = 50 / 5 = 10.

🎙️ Let's apply the formula. We add all the numbers given, which totals 50. Since there are 5 numbers, we divide 50 by 5. The mean, or average, of this dataset is 10. This gives us a single number to represent the entire set.

Mean for Grouped Data (Direct Method)

  • Find the midpoint (class mark, xᵢ) for each class interval.
  • Multiply each midpoint by its frequency (fᵢxᵢ).
  • Sum all (fᵢxᵢ) values.
  • Divide by the total frequency (Σfᵢ).

🎙️ For grouped data, we don't have individual values. So, we assume the values in a class interval are concentrated at its midpoint. We then use these midpoints as 'xᵢ' and multiply by their frequencies to calculate the mean. This is the direct method.

1Find xᵢ
2Calculate fᵢxᵢ
3Sum fᵢxᵢ
4Divide by Σfᵢ
17 / 25

Example: Mean (Direct Method)

  • Class Interval: 0-10 (f=2), 10-20 (f=5), 20-30 (f=3).
  • Midpoints (xᵢ): 5, 15, 25.
  • fᵢxᵢ: (2*5=10), (5*15=75), (3*25=75).
  • Σfᵢxᵢ = 10+75+75 = 160. Σfᵢ = 2+5+3 = 10.
  • Mean = 160 / 10 = 16.

🎙️ Here, for the interval 0-10, the midpoint is 5. We multiply this by its frequency, 2, to get 10. We do this for all intervals, sum up all these products, and divide by the total frequency. This gives us the mean for the grouped data.

Median: The Middle Value

  • The middlemost observation when data is arranged.
  • First, arrange data in ascending or descending order.
  • If odd number of observations: middle value is the median.
  • If even number of observations: average of the two middle values.

🎙️ The median literally means the middle. Imagine a line of students arranged by height; the median height would be that of the student exactly in the middle. It's important to always sort the data first before finding the median.

↔️
Center Point
19 / 25

Example: Calculating Median (Ungrouped)

  • Odd count: 5, 8, 10, 12, 15. Sorted: 5, 8, **10**, 12, 15. Median = 10.
  • Even count: 5, 8, 10, 12. Sorted: 5, **8, 10**, 12. Median = (8+10)/2 = 9.

🎙️ In the first example, with 5 numbers, the 3rd number (10) is the median. In the second example, with 4 numbers, there are two middle numbers (8 and 10). We take their average to find the median, which is 9.

Mode: The Most Frequent

  • The value that appears most often in a dataset.
  • A dataset can have one mode (unimodal), multiple modes (multimodal), or no mode.
  • Simply count occurrences to find it.

🎙️ The mode is the 'popular' value. If most students in a class scored 70 marks, then 70 is the mode of their marks. It's the value that has the highest frequency. It's very easy to find by just looking at your frequency table.

🏆
Most Popular
21 / 25

Example: Calculating Mode (Ungrouped)

  • Dataset 1: 2, 3, 3, 4, 5, 3, 6. Here, '3' appears 3 times. Mode = 3.
  • Dataset 2: 1, 2, 2, 3, 4, 4, 5. Here, '2' and '4' appear 2 times. Modes = 2 and 4 (bimodal).
  • Dataset 3: 7, 8, 9, 10. Each appears once. No mode.

🎙️ In the first case, 3 is clearly the most frequent. In the second, both 2 and 4 appear twice, which is the highest frequency, so both are modes. If all values appear only once, then there is no mode for that dataset.

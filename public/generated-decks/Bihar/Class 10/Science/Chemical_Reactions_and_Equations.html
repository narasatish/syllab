What is a Chemical Reaction?

  • Process where new substances are formed.
  • Original substances lose their identity.
  • Involves breaking and forming of chemical bonds.

🎙️ A chemical reaction is basically a process where one or more substances transform into entirely new substances with different properties. Think of it as a rearrangement of atoms, where old bonds break and new ones form, leading to something completely new.

Physical vs. Chemical Change

  • Physical: No new substance formed (e.g., melting ice).
  • Chemical: New substance with new properties formed.
  • Chemical changes are often irreversible.

🎙️ It's important to distinguish between physical and chemical changes. In a physical change, like melting ice, the substance remains water, just in a different form. But in a chemical change, like burning paper, you get ash and smoke – entirely new substances. Chemical changes are usually much harder to reverse.

Signs of a Chemical Reaction (Part 1)

  • Change in State: From solid to gas, etc.
  • Change in Color: Reactants and products look different.
  • Example: Mixing lead nitrate and potassium iodide solutions.

🎙️ How do we know a chemical reaction has taken place? We look for certain signs. One common sign is a change in state, like a solid turning into a gas. Another clear indicator is a change in color. For example, when colorless lead nitrate and potassium iodide solutions mix, a bright yellow precipitate forms, indicating a reaction.

Writing Chemical Equations: Reactants & Products

  • Reactants: Substances that undergo change (left side).
  • Products: New substances formed (right side).
  • An arrow (→) separates reactants from products.

🎙️ When writing an equation, the substances that start the reaction are called reactants and are written on the left side. The new substances formed are called products and are written on the right side. An arrow in between shows the direction of the reaction, meaning 'yields' or 'forms'.

Balancing Chemical Equations: Law of Conservation of Mass

  • Mass cannot be created or destroyed in a chemical reaction.
  • Total mass of reactants = Total mass of products.
  • Number of atoms of each element must be same on both sides.

🎙️ Why do we balance equations? Because of the Law of Conservation of Mass, which states that matter cannot be created or destroyed. This means the total mass of the reactants must equal the total mass of the products. To ensure this, the number of atoms of each element must be the same on both sides of the equation.

Steps to Balance a Chemical Equation (Hit and Trial Method)

  • 1. Write the unbalanced equation.
  • 2. Count atoms of each element on both sides.
  • 3. Start balancing with the element that has maximum atoms.
  • 4. Balance metals, then non-metals, then H, then O.
  • 5. Check the balanced equation.

🎙️ Balancing equations can seem tricky, but we use a method called 'hit and trial'. First, write the unbalanced equation. Then, count the atoms of each element on both sides. Start balancing with the element that appears least often or has the largest number of atoms. Generally, balance metals first, then non-metals, then hydrogen, and finally oxygen. Always double-check your final equation.

Worked Example: Balancing Ferric Oxide Reaction

  • Equation: Fe(s) + H₂O(g) → Fe₃O₄(s) + H₂(g)
  • Step 1: Balance Fe (1 on left, 3 on right) → 3Fe + H₂O → Fe₃O₄ + H₂
  • Step 2: Balance O (1 on left, 4 on right) → 3Fe + 4H₂O → Fe₃O₄ + H₂
  • Step 3: Balance H (8 on left, 2 on right) → 3Fe + 4H₂O → Fe₃O₄ + 4H₂
  • Final Balanced Equation: 3Fe(s) + 4H₂O(g) → Fe₃O₄(s) + 4H₂(g)

🎙️ Let's work through an example: the reaction of iron with steam. We start with unbalanced Fe + H₂O → Fe₃O₄ + H₂. First, balance Fe by putting '3' before Fe. Then balance O by putting '4' before H₂O. This makes H unbalanced, so put '4' before H₂. Finally, count all atoms to ensure they are equal on both sides. This systematic approach helps achieve a balanced equation.

1. Combination Reaction

  • Two or more reactants combine to form a single product.
  • General form: A + B → AB
  • It's like two friends joining to form a team.

🎙️ In a combination reaction, as the name suggests, two or more simpler substances combine to form a single, more complex product. Think of it as two ingredients coming together to make one dish. The equation A + B → AB perfectly illustrates this pattern.

Example: Combination Reaction

  • Burning of coal: C(s) + O₂(g) → CO₂(g)
  • Formation of water: 2H₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2H₂O(l)
  • Calcium oxide reacting with water: CaO(s) + H₂O(l) → Ca(OH)₂(aq) (Slaked lime)

🎙️ Let's look at some real examples. When coal burns, carbon combines with oxygen to form carbon dioxide. Hydrogen gas and oxygen gas combine to form water. Even calcium oxide, or quicklime, reacting with water to form slaked lime is a combination reaction, often releasing a lot of heat.

2. Decomposition Reaction

  • A single reactant breaks down into two or more simpler products.
  • General form: AB → A + B
  • Requires energy in the form of heat, light, or electricity.

🎙️ Decomposition reactions are the opposite of combination reactions. Here, a single compound breaks down into two or more simpler substances. This process usually requires energy to break the bonds, which can be supplied as heat, light, or electricity.

Types of Decomposition Reactions

  • Thermal Decomposition: Uses heat energy (e.g., CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂).
  • Electrolytic Decomposition: Uses electrical energy (e.g., 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂).
  • Photolytic Decomposition: Uses light energy (e.g., 2AgCl → 2Ag + Cl₂).

🎙️ Decomposition reactions are further categorized by the type of energy used. Thermal decomposition uses heat, like breaking down limestone. Electrolytic decomposition uses electricity, such as splitting water into hydrogen and oxygen. Photolytic decomposition uses light energy, seen in the breakdown of silver chloride in photography.

3. Displacement Reaction

  • A more reactive element displaces a less reactive element from its compound.
  • General form: A + BC → AC + B
  • The more 'stronger' element kicks out the 'weaker' one.

🎙️ In a displacement reaction, one element literally kicks another element out of its compound. This happens when the displacing element is more reactive than the element it displaces. It's like a stronger player taking the place of a weaker player in a team.

Example: Displacement Reaction

  • Iron nail dipped in copper sulphate solution.
  • Iron is more reactive than copper.
  • Fe(s) + CuSO₄(aq) → FeSO₄(aq) + Cu(s)
  • Iron nail turns reddish-brown (copper deposit), solution fades (iron sulphate).

🎙️ A classic experiment for displacement is putting an iron nail into copper sulphate solution. You'll observe the iron nail becoming reddish-brown as copper gets deposited on it, and the blue copper sulphate solution fades to a greenish iron sulphate solution. This shows iron displacing copper because iron is more reactive.

4. Double Displacement Reaction

  • Exchange of ions between two reactant compounds.
  • General form: AB + CD → AD + CB
  • Often results in a precipitate or gas formation.

🎙️ In a double displacement reaction, there's a mutual exchange of ions between the two reactant compounds. It's like two couples swapping partners. These reactions often lead to the formation of an insoluble product called a precipitate, or sometimes a gas or water.

Example: Double Displacement Reaction

  • Sodium sulphate solution reacts with barium chloride solution.
  • Na₂SO₄(aq) + BaCl₂(aq) → BaSO₄(s) + 2NaCl(aq)
  • A white precipitate of barium sulphate is formed.
  • This is also called a precipitation reaction.

🎙️ Consider mixing solutions of sodium sulphate and barium chloride. Immediately, a white, insoluble substance forms and settles at the bottom. This is barium sulphate, a precipitate. The other product, sodium chloride, remains dissolved. This type of reaction, forming a precipitate, is also known as a precipitation reaction.

Recap: Key Takeaways

  • Chemical reactions form new substances.
  • Look for signs: state, color, gas, temperature change.
  • Equations represent reactions, must be balanced (Law of Conservation of Mass).
  • Four main types: Combination, Decomposition, Displacement, Double Displacement.

🎙️ Let's quickly summarize what we've learned today. Remember that chemical reactions involve the formation of entirely new substances. We can identify them by various signs. Chemical equations are our way to concisely represent these reactions, and balancing them is crucial due to the conservation of mass. Finally, we explored the four fundamental types of reactions.

Laws of Reflection: Angle of Incidence

  • First Law: The angle of incidence is equal to the angle of reflection.
  • Angle of incidence (∠i) is between the incident ray and the normal.
  • Angle of reflection (∠r) is between the reflected ray and the normal.

🎙️ The reflection of light isn't random; it follows specific rules. The first law states that if a light ray hits a surface at a certain angle, it will bounce off at the exact same angle. Think of it like a perfectly aimed shot in billiards!

Laws of Reflection: The Plane

  • Second Law: The incident ray, the reflected ray, and the normal to the surface all lie in the same plane.
  • The 'normal' is an imaginary line perpendicular to the surface.
  • This ensures reflection happens in a predictable, consistent way.

🎙️ The second law tells us that all these important lines – the incoming light, the outgoing light, and the imaginary perpendicular line – are flat on the same surface. This means you can draw them all on a single piece of paper, making them easy to visualize.

Types of Reflection: Regular vs. Diffused

  • Regular Reflection: From smooth, polished surfaces (e.g., mirror).
  • Diffused Reflection: From rough, uneven surfaces (e.g., wall, paper).
  • Most objects we see reflect light diffusely.

🎙️ Reflection isn't always perfectly clear. If a surface is very smooth, like a mirror, light reflects in an organized way, creating a clear image – this is regular reflection. But if the surface is rough, like a painted wall, light scatters in many directions, which is diffused reflection. This is why you can't see your reflection in a wall!

Images Formed by Plane Mirrors

  • Virtual: Image cannot be projected on a screen.
  • Erect: Appears upright, not inverted.
  • Same size as the object.
  • Laterally inverted: Left appears right, right appears left.
  • Formed as far behind the mirror as the object is in front.

🎙️ When you look in a plane mirror, the image you see has specific characteristics. It seems to be behind the mirror (virtual), it's upright (erect), and exactly your size. However, your left hand appears as the right hand of your image – that's lateral inversion. The image also appears to be as far behind the mirror as you are in front.

Concave Mirrors: Converging Light

  • Reflecting surface curves inwards, like the inside of a spoon.
  • They converge (bring together) parallel rays of light.
  • Used in torches, headlights, and shaving mirrors.

🎙️ A concave mirror is like the inside of a bowl or spoon. When parallel light rays hit its surface, it directs them all to a single point. This 'gathering' ability makes them useful for focusing light, like in a flashlight to create a strong beam.

Convex Mirrors: Diverging Light

  • Reflecting surface curves outwards, like the back of a spoon.
  • They diverge (spread out) parallel rays of light.
  • Commonly used as rear-view mirrors in vehicles.

🎙️ Conversely, a convex mirror has its reflecting surface bulging outwards. Instead of bringing light rays together, it spreads them out. This spreading effect gives them a wider field of view, which is why they are perfect for car side mirrors.

Key Terms for Spherical Mirrors

  • Pole (P): The center of the mirror's reflecting surface.
  • Centre of Curvature (C): The center of the sphere from which the mirror is cut.
  • Radius of Curvature (R): The distance between P and C.
  • Principal Axis: The straight line passing through P and C.

🎙️ To understand how spherical mirrors form images, we need to know some key terms. The pole is the mirror's central point. The center of curvature is the center of the imaginary sphere the mirror comes from. The principal axis connects these points, acting as a reference line.

Focus (F) and Focal Length (f)

  • Principal Focus (F): A point on the principal axis where parallel rays actually meet (concave) or appear to meet (convex) after reflection.
  • Focal Length (f): The distance between the pole (P) and the principal focus (F).
  • For spherical mirrors, f = R/2.

🎙️ The principal focus is a very important point for spherical mirrors. For a concave mirror, parallel rays converge at this point. For a convex mirror, they appear to spread out from this point. The distance from the pole to this focus is the focal length, which is always half of the radius of curvature.

Rules for Ray Diagrams

  • Rule 1: A ray parallel to the principal axis passes through F (concave) or appears to diverge from F (convex).
  • Rule 2: A ray passing through F (concave) or directed towards F (convex) becomes parallel to the principal axis after reflection.
  • Rule 3: A ray passing through C (concave) or directed towards C (convex) reflects back along the same path.

🎙️ To draw ray diagrams and understand image formation, we follow three simple rules. These rules help us trace the path of light rays after reflection from spherical mirrors. By using just two of these rays, we can find the position and nature of the image formed.

Image by Convex Mirror: Any Position

  • A convex mirror always forms a virtual, erect, and diminished image.
  • The image is always formed behind the mirror, between P and F.

Refraction of Light: Light Bending

  • The bending of light as it passes from one transparent medium to another.
  • Light changes direction due to a change in its speed.
  • Occurs at the boundary between the two media.

🎙️ Now, let's shift our focus to refraction. Unlike reflection, where light bounces back, refraction is when light passes through a surface and bends. This bending happens because light changes its speed as it moves from one material, like air, into another, like water or glass.

Why Does Light Bend?

  • Light travels at different speeds in different media.
  • It slows down when entering a denser medium (e.g., from air to water).
  • It speeds up when entering a rarer medium (e.g., from water to air).
  • This change in speed causes the change in direction.

🎙️ The core reason for refraction is the change in the speed of light. Imagine running on a smooth road and then suddenly stepping into mud – you'd slow down and change direction! Similarly, light slows down in denser materials, causing it to bend.

Laws of Refraction: Snell's Law

  • First Law: The incident ray, the refracted ray, and the normal all lie in the same plane (similar to reflection).
  • Second Law (Snell's Law): The ratio of the sine of the angle of incidence to the sine of the angle of refraction is constant for a given pair of media.
  • This constant is called the refractive index.

🎙️ Just like reflection, refraction also follows specific laws. The first law is similar to reflection, stating that all rays and the normal are in the same plane. The second, more famous, is Snell's Law, which mathematically describes how much light bends based on the properties of the two materials it's passing through, using the concept of refractive index.

Refraction in Everyday Life: Apparent Depth

  • A pencil appears bent when partially immersed in water.
  • A swimming pool appears shallower than it actually is.
  • Fish in water appear to be closer and larger than they really are.

🎙️ You've probably seen these phenomena! When you put a pencil in water, it looks broken. This is due to refraction; light from the submerged part bends as it exits the water, making it seem like the pencil is in a different position. Similarly, the bottom of a pool or a fish in water appears higher up than it truly is.

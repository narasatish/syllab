What is Carbon?

  • An element with symbol 'C'.
  • Atomic number is 6, meaning it has 6 protons and 6 electrons.
  • It is a non-metal.

🎙️ Carbon is a basic building block of matter, just like oxygen or hydrogen. Its atomic number tells us it has 6 protons in its nucleus, which also means it has 6 electrons orbiting around it. Remember, it's a non-metal.

Where is Carbon Found?

  • In the Earth's crust as minerals (carbonates, coal, petroleum).
  • In the atmosphere as Carbon Dioxide (CO2).
  • In all living organisms (plants, animals, humans).

🎙️ Carbon is incredibly abundant! From the ground we walk on, to the air we breathe, and even within our own bodies, carbon is present in various forms. It truly is a ubiquitous element.

Carbon's Special Power: Catenation

  • The unique ability of carbon atoms to form bonds with other carbon atoms.
  • Forms long chains, branched chains, and ring structures.
  • Leads to a vast number of carbon compounds.

🎙️ Imagine building blocks that can connect to each other endlessly. That's what catenation is for carbon! This self-linking property allows carbon to form incredibly long and complex structures, which is why there are millions of carbon compounds.

🔗
Self-linking property

Carbon's Special Power: Tetravalency

  • Carbon has a valency of 4.
  • It can form four chemical bonds with other atoms.
  • This makes carbon very versatile in forming compounds.

🎙️ Tetra means four, and valency means combining capacity. So, tetravalency means carbon can form four bonds. This allows it to connect with many different atoms at once, like hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, and other carbons, making it incredibly flexible.

How Carbon Bonds: Covalent Bonds

  • Carbon achieves stability by sharing electrons with other atoms.
  • These shared electron pairs form covalent bonds.
  • Unlike ionic bonds, there is no transfer of electrons.

🎙️ Carbon needs four more electrons to complete its outermost shell and become stable. Instead of gaining or losing electrons, which would require a lot of energy, carbon prefers to share them. This sharing forms strong and stable covalent bonds, which are very common in organic chemistry.

Sharing electrons

Allotropes of Carbon: Diamond

  • A crystalline allotrope of carbon.
  • Each carbon atom is bonded to four other carbon atoms in a rigid 3D structure.
  • Extremely hard, non-conductor of electricity, used in jewellery and cutting tools.

🎙️ Diamond is a sparkling example of how carbon atoms can arrange themselves. Its unique structure makes it the hardest natural substance known. Even though it's made of just carbon, its properties are vastly different from other forms of carbon.

💎
Hardest natural substance

Allotropes of Carbon: Graphite

  • Another crystalline allotrope of carbon.
  • Carbon atoms arranged in hexagonal layers, which can slide over each other.
  • Soft, slippery, good conductor of electricity, used in pencils and lubricants.

🎙️ Graphite is the dark, soft material in your pencil. Unlike diamond, its layered structure allows it to be slippery and conduct electricity. It's amazing how the same element, carbon, can form two materials with such different properties, just by changing how its atoms are arranged.

✏️
Used in pencils

Allotropes of Carbon: Fullerene

  • A third allotrope, often spherical or tubular in shape.
  • Common example is C60, also known as 'Buckminsterfullerene' or 'Buckyball'.
  • Has applications in nanotechnology and medicine.

🎙️ Fullerenes are relatively newer discoveries. They look like tiny footballs or cages, hence the nickname 'Buckyballs'. These molecules have exciting potential in fields like medicine for drug delivery and in making super-strong, lightweight materials.

Hydrocarbons: Carbon & Hydrogen Friends

  • Compounds made up only of carbon and hydrogen atoms.
  • They are the simplest organic compounds.
  • Serve as fuels and raw materials for many industries.

🎙️ Now, let's dive into the vast family of carbon compounds called hydrocarbons. As the name suggests, they are compounds of only hydrogen and carbon. Think of petrol or cooking gas – these are all examples of hydrocarbons.

🧪
Basic organic compounds

Saturated Hydrocarbons: Alkanes

  • Hydrocarbons with only single bonds between carbon atoms.
  • They are 'saturated' because they have the maximum possible hydrogen atoms.
  • General formula: CnH2n+2. Example: Methane (CH4).

🎙️ Alkanes are the simplest type of hydrocarbons, containing only single bonds. This makes them very stable and less reactive. Methane, the main component of natural gas, is a common alkane you might know.

➖➖➖➖
Only single bonds
13 / 25

Unsaturated Hydrocarbons: Alkenes

  • Hydrocarbons with at least one double bond between carbon atoms.
  • They are 'unsaturated' because they can add more hydrogen atoms.
  • General formula: CnH2n. Example: Ethene (C2H4).

🎙️ Alkenes introduce a new feature: the double bond. This double bond makes them more reactive than alkanes. Ethene is used to ripen fruits, showcasing its unique properties due to the double bond.

🟰🟰
Contains a double bond
14 / 25

Unsaturated Hydrocarbons: Alkynes

  • Hydrocarbons with at least one triple bond between carbon atoms.
  • Even more unsaturated and reactive than alkenes.
  • General formula: CnH2n-2. Example: Ethyne (C2H2).

🎙️ Alkynes take unsaturation a step further with a triple bond. This makes them highly reactive. Ethyne, also known as acetylene, is used in welding because it produces a very hot flame when burned.

☰
Contains a triple bond

Functional Groups: Property Makers

  • An atom or group of atoms that determines the chemical properties of an organic compound.
  • They replace one or more hydrogen atoms in a hydrocarbon chain.
  • Give rise to different classes of organic compounds.

🎙️ Think of a functional group as a special 'attachment' to a hydrocarbon chain. Even a small change, like adding an oxygen atom in a specific way, can completely change how a compound behaves. These groups are key to understanding organic chemistry.

Functional Groups: Haloalkanes

  • Contain one or more halogen atoms (Fluorine, Chlorine, Bromine, Iodine).
  • Example: Chloromethane (CH3Cl) – used as a refrigerant.
  • Halogen atoms replace hydrogen atoms in an alkane.

🎙️ Haloalkanes are hydrocarbons where one or more hydrogen atoms have been replaced by a halogen. Chloromethane, for instance, is an important industrial chemical. We often see these in refrigerants or as solvents.

Functional Groups: Alcohols

  • Contain the hydroxyl (-OH) functional group.
  • Example: Ethanol (CH3CH2OH) – found in alcoholic beverages.
  • Often used as solvents and fuels.

🎙️ Alcohols are defined by the presence of the -OH group. Ethanol is a very common alcohol that you might know. It's not just for drinks; it's also a popular solvent and can be used as a fuel.

🍷
Contains -OH group

Functional Groups: Carboxylic Acids

  • Contain the carboxyl (-COOH) functional group.
  • Example: Ethanoic acid (CH3COOH) – the main component of vinegar.
  • They are typically weak acids.

🎙️ Carboxylic acids are responsible for the sour taste in many foods. Ethanoic acid is vinegar, which we use in cooking. The -COOH group gives these compounds their acidic properties, even though they are generally weaker acids compared to mineral acids.

Naming Carbon Compounds: IUPAC Rules

  • IUPAC (International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry) provides systematic rules.
  • Ensures a unique name for every compound worldwide.
  • Based on the length of the carbon chain and functional groups present.

🎙️ With millions of carbon compounds, we need a clear way to name them so everyone understands which compound we're talking about. The IUPAC system provides these rules, making communication among scientists consistent globally. We learn to identify the main chain and the functional groups to give it a proper name.

Chemical Properties: Combustion

  • Burning of carbon compounds in the presence of oxygen.
  • Produces carbon dioxide, water, heat, and light.
  • Example: CH4 + 2O2 → CO2 + 2H2O + Heat + Light.

🎙️ Combustion is a very common reaction, especially for hydrocarbons, which are often used as fuels. When you burn wood, petrol, or cooking gas, you're seeing combustion in action. It releases energy, which we use for various purposes.

Chemical Properties: Oxidation

  • Addition of oxygen to a carbon compound.
  • Can be achieved using oxidizing agents like alkaline KMnO4 or acidified K2Cr2O7.
  • Example: Alcohols can be oxidized to carboxylic acids.

🎙️ Oxidation is a process where a compound gains oxygen atoms. This can change the compound significantly. For example, if you leave wine (which contains ethanol) exposed to air, it can oxidize to vinegar (ethanoic acid), making it sour.

Adding oxygen

Chemical Properties: Addition Reaction

  • Unsaturated hydrocarbons (alkenes and alkynes) undergo this reaction.
  • Atoms are added across the double or triple bond, making the compound saturated.
  • Example: Hydrogenation of vegetable oils (adds H2 to double bonds).

🎙️ Addition reactions are characteristic of unsaturated compounds. The double or triple bond 'opens up' to allow new atoms to attach. A real-life example is converting vegetable oil (unsaturated) into vanaspati ghee (saturated) by adding hydrogen, which is called hydrogenation.

Chemical Properties: Substitution Reaction

  • Saturated hydrocarbons (alkanes) typically undergo this reaction.
  • One atom or group replaces another atom or group in the compound.
  • Example: Methane reacting with chlorine in the presence of sunlight (CH4 + Cl2 → CH3Cl + HCl).

🎙️ Substitution reactions, as the name suggests, involve one atom or group replacing another. These are common for alkanes, which are generally less reactive. For example, a hydrogen atom in methane can be replaced by a chlorine atom under specific conditions, leading to new compounds.

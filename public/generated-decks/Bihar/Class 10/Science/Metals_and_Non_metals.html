Physical Property: Luster (चमक)

  • Metals are shiny and have a metallic luster.
  • They reflect light well when polished.
  • Non-metals generally appear dull.

🎙️ One of the first things you notice about metals is their shine. Think of gold ornaments or polished silver. This property, called luster, is due to the free electrons on their surface. Non-metals, like sulfur or carbon, usually do not have this shine.

Physical Property: Hardness (कठोरता)

  • Metals are generally hard and strong.
  • They resist being cut or deformed easily.
  • Non-metals are often soft or brittle.

🎙️ Most metals, like iron or copper, are very hard and strong, which is why they are used in construction. You can't easily break them. In contrast, non-metals like coal or chalk are soft and can break easily.

Physical Property: Malleability (आघातवर्धनीयता)

  • Metals can be hammered into thin sheets.
  • They do not break when hammered.
  • Non-metals are brittle and break upon hammering.

🎙️ Have you ever seen aluminum foil? That's a great example of malleability. Metals like gold and silver are highly malleable, meaning they can be beaten into very thin sheets without breaking. This is not possible with non-metals.

Physical Property: Ductility (तन्यता)

  • Metals can be drawn into thin wires.
  • This property allows for the making of electrical wires.
  • Non-metals cannot be drawn into wires.

🎙️ Think about the copper wires used for electricity. This ability of metals to be stretched or drawn into thin wires is called ductility. Gold is one of the most ductile metals, but copper is commonly used due to its cost-effectiveness.

Physical Property: Thermal Conductivity (ऊष्मा चालकता)

  • Metals are excellent conductors of heat.
  • Heat passes through them quickly and efficiently.
  • Non-metals are generally poor conductors of heat (insulators).

🎙️ Why are cooking pots made of metal? Because metals conduct heat very well, allowing food to cook quickly. Non-metals, like wood or plastic, are poor conductors of heat, which is why handles of cooking utensils are often made from them.

Physical Property: Electrical Conductivity (विद्युत चालकता)

  • Metals are good conductors of electricity.
  • Free electrons allow electric current to flow easily.
  • Non-metals are generally poor conductors (except graphite).

🎙️ Just like heat, electricity also flows easily through metals. This is why electrical wires are made of copper or aluminum. The presence of free electrons in metals makes them excellent electrical conductors.

Physical Property: Sonorous (ध्वनि उत्पन्न करना)

  • Metals produce a ringing sound when struck.
  • This property is called sonority.
  • Non-metals do not produce a ringing sound.

🎙️ Have you ever noticed the sound when a bell is struck? It's a clear, ringing sound. This is because metals are sonorous. Non-metals, when struck, produce a dull, thudding sound, if any.

Non-metals: Opposite Properties

  • Generally lack luster (dull appearance).
  • Are typically soft and brittle.
  • Poor conductors of heat and electricity.
  • Not sonorous, not malleable, not ductile.

🎙️ In summary, non-metals generally exhibit properties opposite to metals. They are dull, brittle, and do not conduct heat or electricity well. They also don't produce a ringing sound when struck. Examples include sulfur, carbon (diamond is an exception to hardness), and oxygen.

Exceptions to the Rules

  • Sodium and Potassium are soft metals, can be cut with a knife.
  • Mercury is a liquid metal at room temperature.
  • Graphite (a form of carbon) is a non-metal but conducts electricity.
  • Iodine is a non-metal but has a lustrous appearance.

🎙️ Science often has exceptions! While we've discussed general properties, it's important to remember these special cases. Sodium and potassium are metals but are surprisingly soft. Mercury is the only metal that is liquid at room temperature. Graphite, a non-metal, is a good conductor of electricity, and iodine, also a non-metal, has a metallic luster.

Chemical Property: Reaction with Oxygen (Oxides)

  • Metals react with oxygen to form metal oxides.
  • Metal oxides are generally basic in nature (e.g., Magnesium Oxide).
  • Non-metals react with oxygen to form non-metal oxides.
  • Non-metal oxides are generally acidic (e.g., Carbon Dioxide).

🎙️ Now, let's explore how metals and non-metals react chemically. When metals burn in air, they combine with oxygen to form metal oxides, which are usually basic. Non-metals also react with oxygen, forming non-metal oxides, which are typically acidic. Think of rusting of iron, it's a slow oxidation process.

Chemical Property: Reaction with Water (Hydroxides)

  • Some metals react with water to form metal hydroxides and hydrogen gas.
  • Reactivity varies: Sodium reacts vigorously, Iron reacts slowly with steam.
  • Non-metals generally do not react with water.

🎙️ The way metals react with water can vary greatly. Highly reactive metals like sodium react explosively with cold water, producing hydrogen gas and heat. Less reactive metals like iron react only with steam. Non-metals, however, typically do not react with water at all.

Chemical Property: Reaction with Acids (Salts & Hydrogen)

  • Metals react with dilute acids to form salt and hydrogen gas.
  • More reactive metals react faster.
  • Non-metals generally do not react with dilute acids.

🎙️ When metals react with dilute acids, they produce a salt and hydrogen gas. You might have seen this in the lab when zinc reacts with hydrochloric acid, producing bubbles of hydrogen. Non-metals usually do not show this reaction with dilute acids.

Chemical Property: Reaction with Bases

  • Some metals react with strong bases.
  • They form salt and hydrogen gas.
  • Example: Aluminium reacts with Sodium Hydroxide.
  • Non-metals react with bases in more complex ways.

🎙️ While metals typically react with acids, some metals, like aluminum and zinc, can also react with strong bases. These are called amphoteric metals as their oxides and hydroxides show both acidic and basic properties. Non-metals also react with bases, but their reactions are often more varied.

Reactivity Series of Metals

  • A list of metals arranged in decreasing order of their reactivity.
  • More reactive metals are at the top.
  • Less reactive metals are at the bottom.

🎙️ The reactivity series is a very important tool in chemistry. It tells us how readily a metal will react. Metals higher in the series are more reactive and can displace metals lower down from their salt solutions. Hydrogen is included for comparison with acids.

Displacement Reactions

  • A more reactive metal displaces a less reactive metal.
  • It replaces the less reactive metal from its salt solution.
  • Example: Iron displaces Copper from Copper Sulphate solution.

🎙️ This concept is crucial! Imagine a stronger person replacing a weaker person from a seat. Similarly, a more reactive metal will 'kick out' a less reactive metal from its compound. For example, if you put an iron nail in a copper sulphate solution, the iron will displace copper, turning the solution green and depositing copper on the nail.

How Metals React: Ionic Bonding

  • Metals tend to lose electrons to achieve stability.
  • They form positively charged ions called cations.
  • Non-metals tend to gain electrons to achieve stability.
  • They form negatively charged ions called anions.

🎙️ Why do metals and non-metals react the way they do? It's all about achieving a stable electron configuration, like noble gases. Metals readily give up their valence electrons to become positive ions, while non-metals readily accept electrons to become negative ions.

Formation of Ionic Compounds

  • Formed by the complete transfer of electrons.
  • Occurs typically between a metal and a non-metal.
  • Example: Sodium (metal) loses an electron, Chlorine (non-metal) gains an electron.
  • Forms Sodium Chloride (NaCl) through electrostatic attraction.

🎙️ Let's take the example of common salt, sodium chloride. Sodium, a metal, donates one electron to chlorine, a non-metal. This creates a positive sodium ion and a negative chloride ion. These oppositely charged ions attract each other strongly, forming an ionic bond and an ionic compound.

Properties of Ionic Compounds

  • Are solid and hard, due to strong electrostatic forces.
  • Have high melting and boiling points.
  • Generally soluble in water.
  • Conduct electricity in molten state or aqueous solution, not in solid state.

🎙️ Because of the strong forces holding ions together, ionic compounds are typically hard solids with high melting points. They dissolve well in water because water molecules can separate the ions. While solid ionic compounds don't conduct electricity, their ions become free to move in molten or dissolved states, allowing conduction.

Everyday Uses of Metals

  • Iron: Construction of buildings, bridges, vehicles.
  • Copper: Electrical wires, plumbing, coins.
  • Aluminium: Aircraft, utensils, packaging foils.
  • Gold/Silver: Jewellery, coinage, electronic components.

🎙️ Metals are indispensable in our daily lives. From the sturdy iron beams in our homes to the delicate gold in jewellery, their unique properties make them suitable for countless applications. Think about the vehicles we travel in, or the electrical appliances we use, all rely heavily on metals.

Everyday Uses of Non-metals

  • Oxygen: Essential for respiration, combustion.
  • Nitrogen: Used in fertilizers, food packaging, inert atmospheres.

  • Carbon (Graphite): Pencil lead, electrodes, lubricants.

🎙️ Non-metals are equally vital, especially for life itself! Oxygen is what we breathe, and carbon is the backbone of all organic life. Many non-metals are crucial in agriculture, water treatment, and various industries, proving their immense importance.

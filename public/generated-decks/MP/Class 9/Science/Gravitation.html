Universal Law of Gravitation

  • Every object in the universe attracts every other object.
  • Force is directly proportional to product of masses.
  • Force is inversely proportional to square of distance.

🎙️ Newton formulated the Universal Law of Gravitation, stating that every object in the universe attracts every other object. The strength of this attraction depends on two main factors: the masses of the objects and the distance between them.

Gravitational Force Formula

  • F = G (m₁m₂ / r²)
  • F = Gravitational Force
  • m₁, m₂ = Masses of objects
  • r = Distance between their centers

🎙️ This formula is the heart of gravitation. It tells us that if you increase the masses of the objects, the force increases. But if you increase the distance between them, the force decreases rapidly, specifically by the square of the distance.

Universal Gravitational Constant (G)

  • G is a proportionality constant.
  • Its value is 6.673 x 10⁻¹¹ Nm²kg⁻².
  • It's constant everywhere in the universe.

🎙️ The 'G' in the formula is called the Universal Gravitational Constant. It's a very small but very important number that ensures the equation gives us the correct force. It was first determined experimentally by Henry Cavendish.

Free Fall

  • An object is in free fall if only gravity acts on it.
  • No air resistance or other forces.
  • All objects in free fall accelerate at the same rate.

🎙️ When an object falls towards the Earth, and the only force acting on it is gravity, we say it is in 'free fall'. This means we ignore air resistance for simplicity. Surprisingly, in a vacuum, a feather and a hammer would fall at the exact same rate!

Acceleration Due to Gravity (g)

  • The acceleration produced by Earth's gravity.
  • Denoted by 'g'.
  • Its average value on Earth's surface is 9.8 m/s².

🎙️ When an object is in free fall, its velocity continuously increases. This increase in velocity is due to an acceleration, which we call the acceleration due to gravity, or 'g'. For practical purposes, we often use 9.8 m/s² as its value on Earth's surface.

Calculating 'g'

  • From Newton's Law: F = GMm/R²
  • From Newton's Second Law: F = mg
  • Equating them: mg = GMm/R²
  • So, g = GM/R²

🎙️ We can derive the value of 'g' using both Newton's Law of Gravitation and his Second Law of Motion. By equating the gravitational force and the force due to acceleration, we find that 'g' depends on the mass of the Earth (M) and its radius (R).

Variation of 'g'

  • Value of 'g' varies with altitude.
  • Decreases as you go higher.
  • Slightly varies with Earth's shape (poles vs. equator).

🎙️ The value of 'g' is not perfectly constant everywhere. It decreases as you move away from the Earth's surface, like climbing a mountain. Also, because the Earth is not a perfect sphere, 'g' is slightly higher at the poles and lower at the equator.

Mass vs. Weight

  • Mass is the amount of matter in an object.
  • Weight is the force of gravity on an object.
  • Mass is constant, Weight changes with 'g'.

🎙️ This is a crucial distinction! Mass is an intrinsic property of an object, measured in kilograms, and it never changes. Weight, however, is a force, measured in Newtons, and it depends on the local acceleration due to gravity. Your mass is the same on Earth and the Moon, but your weight is different.

Worked Example: Weight on Moon

  • Mass of a person = 60 kg.
  • g on Earth = 9.8 m/s².
  • g on Moon ≈ 1/6th of g on Earth.
  • Calculate weight on Earth and Moon.

🎙️ Let's apply what we just learned. On Earth, weight = mass x g = 60 kg x 9.8 m/s² = 588 N. On the Moon, g is about 1/6th of Earth's g, so the weight would be 60 kg x (9.8/6) m/s² = 98 N. You'd feel much lighter on the Moon!

Thrust and Pressure

  • Thrust: Force acting perpendicular to a surface.
  • Pressure: Thrust per unit area (P = F/A).
  • Smaller area leads to higher pressure for same thrust.

🎙️ These concepts are important when dealing with forces in fluids. Thrust is simply the total force applied perpendicular to a surface. Pressure, however, considers how this force is distributed over an area. Think about why a sharp pin can pierce easily – it concentrates the force into a tiny area, creating high pressure.

Buoyancy

  • Upward force exerted by a fluid on an immersed object.
  • This force opposes the object's weight.
  • Makes objects feel lighter in water.

🎙️ Have you ever felt lighter in a swimming pool? That's due to buoyancy! It's the upward push from the water or any fluid that tries to make an object float. This force is always present when an object is submerged in a fluid.

Archimedes' Principle

  • Buoyant force = Weight of fluid displaced.
  • Explains why objects float or sink.
  • Discovered by Greek scientist Archimedes.

🎙️ Archimedes' Principle gives us a way to calculate the buoyant force. It states that the upward buoyant force is equal to the weight of the fluid that the object pushes aside. If an object displaces fluid heavier than itself, it floats; otherwise, it sinks.

Applications of Archimedes' Principle

  • Hydrometers measure liquid density.
  • Helps design hot air balloons.

🎙️ Archimedes' Principle has many practical applications. Ships float because their design displaces a large volume of water, creating enough buoyant force to counteract their weight. Submarines use tanks to take in or expel water to control their buoyancy and dive or surface.

Density and Relative Density

  • Density = Mass / Volume (ρ = m/V).
  • Relative Density = Density of substance / Density of water.
  • Relative density has no units.

🎙️ Density tells us how much 'stuff' is packed into a given space. A dense object has a lot of mass in a small volume. Relative density compares an object's density to that of water, which is a convenient way to determine if it will float or sink in water.

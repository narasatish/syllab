What is Force?

  • A push or a pull
  • Interaction between two objects
  • Measured in Newtons (N)

🎙️ Imagine opening a door, kicking a football, or lifting your school bag. All these actions involve a 'force'. Force is simply a push or a pull that one object exerts on another.

Effects of Force: What Can Force Do?

  • Change speed (start/stop/slow down)
  • Change direction of motion
  • Change shape or size of an object

🎙️ Force isn't just about making things move. It can do many things! It can make a stationary object move, stop a moving object, change its speed, alter its direction, or even deform its shape, like squeezing a sponge.

Balanced vs. Unbalanced Forces

  • Balanced: Net force is zero
  • Unbalanced: Net force is not zero
  • Unbalanced forces cause motion

🎙️ When multiple forces act on an object, we look at the 'net force'. If all forces cancel each other out, it's a balanced force, and the object's motion doesn't change. If there's a leftover force, it's unbalanced, and the object will accelerate.

Example: Tug-of-War

  • Teams pull with equal force: Balanced
  • One team pulls stronger: Unbalanced
  • Unbalanced force causes movement

🎙️ A perfect example is a tug-of-war. If both teams pull with equal strength, the rope doesn't move – balanced forces. But if one team pulls harder, they exert an unbalanced force, and the rope (and the other team) moves in their direction.

Newton's First Law: The Law of Inertia

  • Object at rest stays at rest
  • Object in motion stays in motion
  • Unless acted upon by an external force

🎙️ Sir Isaac Newton gave us three fundamental laws of motion. The first law, also known as the Law of Inertia, states that an object will continue in its state of rest or uniform motion in a straight line, unless an external unbalanced force acts on it.

Momentum: Quantity of Motion

  • Measure of motion of an object
  • Depends on mass and velocity
  • Formula: p = m × v

🎙️ Momentum is a very important concept in physics. It's essentially the 'quantity of motion' an object possesses. A heavy object moving slowly can have the same momentum as a light object moving very fast. It's calculated by multiplying mass and velocity.

💨
Moving Object
11 / 25

Factors Affecting Momentum

  • Mass (m): More mass, more momentum
  • Velocity (v): More velocity, more momentum
  • Both are directly proportional to momentum

🎙️ From the formula p = m × v, it's clear that both mass and velocity play a role. A bowling ball has more momentum than a tennis ball moving at the same speed. A fast-moving car has more momentum than a slow-moving car of the same mass.

Worked Example: Calculating Momentum

  • Problem: A 5 kg ball moves at 10 m/s
  • Mass (m) = 5 kg
  • Velocity (v) = 10 m/s
  • p = m × v = 5 kg × 10 m/s = 50 kg m/s

🎙️ Let's apply the formula. If we have a 5-kilogram ball rolling at 10 meters per second, its momentum would be 5 kilograms multiplied by 10 meters per second, giving us 50 kilogram-meters per second. Remember the units!

Newton's Second Law: Force and Acceleration

  • Rate of change of momentum is proportional to applied force
  • Force (F) = mass (m) × acceleration (a)
  • F = ma is the famous formula

🎙️ Newton's Second Law is incredibly powerful. It directly relates force to the change in an object's motion. It tells us that the greater the force applied, the greater the acceleration, and that a heavier object requires more force to accelerate it.

🚀
Acceleration
16 / 25

Derivation of F=ma (Simplified)

  • Force ∝ change in momentum / time
  • Change in momentum = m(v-u)
  • F ∝ m(v-u)/t
  • Since a = (v-u)/t, F ∝ ma
  • F = kma (k=1 in SI units) => F = ma

🎙️ While the full derivation involves calculus, we can understand its essence. Force is proportional to how quickly momentum changes. Since momentum change depends on mass and velocity change, and velocity change over time is acceleration, we arrive at the simple and elegant relation F=ma.

2Δp = mΔv
3F ∝ mΔv/Δt

Worked Example: Applying F=ma

  • Problem: A 2 kg object accelerates at 3 m/s²
  • Mass (m) = 2 kg
  • Acceleration (a) = 3 m/s²
  • F = m × a = 2 kg × 3 m/s² = 6 N

🎙️ Let's use F=ma. If you have a 2-kilogram object and you want it to accelerate at 3 meters per second squared, you would need to apply a force of 2 times 3, which is 6 Newtons. This formula is used constantly in engineering and physics.

Newton's Third Law: Action and Reaction

  • Every action has an equal and opposite reaction
  • Forces always occur in pairs
  • Act on different objects

🎙️ Newton's Third Law is all about interactions. When you push on something, it pushes back on you with the same amount of force, but in the opposite direction. These forces are always equal in magnitude and opposite in direction, and they always act on different objects.

Law of Conservation of Momentum

  • Total momentum of an isolated system remains constant
  • Before collision = After collision
  • No external force acting on the system

🎙️ This law is incredibly important for understanding collisions and explosions. It states that in a closed system, where no external forces are acting, the total momentum before an interaction (like a collision) is exactly equal to the total momentum after the interaction.

Example: Collision of Billiard Balls

  • Ball 1 hits Ball 2
  • Momentum transferred from Ball 1 to Ball 2
  • Total momentum of both balls stays the same

🎙️ Imagine two billiard balls colliding. Before the collision, each ball has its own momentum. During the collision, momentum is exchanged between them, but the sum of their momenta before the collision will be equal to the sum of their momenta after the collision, assuming no friction.

Chapter Recap: Key Concepts

  • Force: Push or pull, causes change in motion/shape
  • Newton's 1st Law: Inertia (resistance to change)
  • Newton's 2nd Law: F=ma (force causes acceleration)
  • Newton's 3rd Law: Action-Reaction pairs
  • Conservation of Momentum: Total momentum constant in isolated system

Early Ideas About Atoms

  • Ancient Greeks (Democritus) proposed indivisible particles.
  • John Dalton revived this idea in the early 1800s.

🎙️ For centuries, people wondered what matter was made of. Ancient thinkers like Democritus imagined tiny, unbreakable pieces. John Dalton later brought this scientific idea back into focus with experimental observations.

Dalton's Atomic Theory (1808)

  • Matter consists of indivisible atoms.
  • Atoms of the same element are identical in mass and properties.
  • Atoms combine in simple whole-number ratios to form compounds.

🎙️ Dalton proposed that atoms were like tiny, solid, indestructible spheres. He stated that all atoms of an element are exactly alike, and different elements have different types of atoms with different masses.

🧱
Building Blocks

Limitations of Dalton's Theory

  • Couldn't explain how atoms could be divided into smaller particles.
  • Did not account for charged particles (electrons, protons).
  • Failed to explain why different elements have different properties.

🎙️ While revolutionary for its time, Dalton's theory had its limits. It couldn't explain phenomena like static electricity, which suggested that atoms might not be truly indivisible as he thought.

Discovery of Electrons (J.J. Thomson)

  • Cathode ray experiment showed negatively charged particles.
  • These particles were much smaller than atoms.
  • Named 'electrons', they are fundamental constituents of all atoms.

🎙️ In the late 1800s, J.J. Thomson conducted experiments with cathode ray tubes. He observed streams of negatively charged particles, proving that atoms were indeed divisible and contained smaller components.

Properties of Electrons

  • Negatively charged particle.
  • Mass is negligible compared to protons and neutrons.
  • Found outside the nucleus, revolving in orbits.

🎙️ Electrons carry a fundamental negative charge. They are incredibly light and orbit the central part of the atom, much like planets around a star, but in specific energy levels.

Discovery of Protons (E. Goldstein)

  • Anode ray (canal ray) experiment showed positively charged particles.
  • These particles were heavier than electrons.
  • Named 'protons', found in the atom's core.

🎙️ Shortly after electrons were discovered, E. Goldstein, using a modified cathode ray tube, observed streams of positively charged particles. These 'canal rays' led to the identification of protons.

Properties of Protons

  • Positively charged particle.
  • Mass is approximately 1 atomic mass unit (amu).
  • Found inside the nucleus of the atom.

🎙️ Protons carry a positive charge equal in magnitude to the electron's negative charge. They are much heavier than electrons and reside in the atom's dense central core, known as the nucleus.

Thomson's Model of Atom (1904)

  • Atom is a sphere of uniform positive charge.
  • Electrons are embedded in it like seeds in a watermelon.
  • Also known as the 'Plum Pudding' model.

🎙️ Thomson proposed his 'Plum Pudding' model, envisioning the atom as a sphere of positive charge with negatively charged electrons scattered throughout. He believed this arrangement would make the atom electrically neutral.

Rutherford's Alpha-Particle Scattering Experiment

  • Alpha particles (He²⁺ ions) were directed at a thin gold foil.
  • A fluorescent screen detected the scattered particles.
  • Most particles passed straight through the foil.

🎙️ Ernest Rutherford designed an experiment to probe the atom's internal structure. He fired tiny, positively charged alpha particles at a very thin sheet of gold foil, expecting them to pass straight through with minimal deflection.

Observations of Rutherford's Experiment

  • Most alpha particles passed straight through the foil, undeviated.
  • A few particles were deflected at small angles.
  • Very few (about 1 in 12,000) bounced back almost completely.

🎙️ To his surprise, while most particles went straight, some veered off course, and a tiny fraction even rebounded. This indicated something very dense and positively charged was at the atom's center.

Conclusions of Rutherford's Experiment

  • Most of the atom is empty space.
  • A small, dense, positively charged center called the 'nucleus' exists.
  • Electrons revolve around the nucleus in orbits.

🎙️ Rutherford concluded that the atom must be mostly empty space, with a tiny, heavy, positively charged nucleus at its core. The electrons, he proposed, orbit this nucleus, preventing them from falling in due to centrifugal force.

Rutherford's Nuclear Model of Atom

  • Atom has a positively charged nucleus at its center.
  • Almost all mass is concentrated in the nucleus.
  • Electrons revolve around the nucleus in well-defined orbits.

🎙️ This model is often compared to a miniature solar system, where the nucleus is the sun and electrons are the planets. It was a significant step forward in understanding atomic structure, proposing a central dense core.

🪐
Planetary Model

Limitations of Rutherford's Model

  • Could not explain the stability of the atom.
  • Revolving electrons would lose energy and eventually fall into the nucleus.
  • This would make atoms unstable, which contradicts observations.

🎙️ Despite its strengths, Rutherford's model had a major flaw: classical physics predicted that orbiting electrons should continuously lose energy and spiral into the nucleus, causing atoms to collapse. This contradicted the observed stability of atoms.

Bohr's Model of Atom (1913)

  • Electrons revolve in specific, stable orbits called 'discrete energy levels' or 'shells'.
  • Electrons do not radiate energy while in these orbits.
  • Energy is absorbed or emitted only when electrons jump between orbits.

🎙️ Niels Bohr modified Rutherford's model by introducing the concept of fixed energy levels. He proposed that electrons can only exist in certain stable orbits without losing energy, explaining atomic stability.

Bohr's model of the atom proposed that electrons orbit the nucleus in fixed energy levels
17 / 25

Energy Levels (Shells)

  • These orbits are designated as K, L, M, N shells.
  • K shell is closest to the nucleus (n=1), L is next (n=2), and so on.
  • Each shell has a fixed capacity for electrons (2n² rule).

🎙️ The shells are numbered from the nucleus outwards: K (n=1), L (n=2), M (n=3), etc. Each shell can hold a maximum number of electrons, determined by the formula 2n², where 'n' is the shell number.

Discovery of Neutrons (James Chadwick, 1932)

  • Discovered a neutral particle in the nucleus.
  • Explained the 'missing mass' in atoms not accounted for by protons alone.
  • Neutron has no charge but has mass similar to a proton.

🎙️ After protons and electrons, scientists realized there was still 'missing mass' in the nucleus. James Chadwick finally discovered the neutron, completing our understanding of the three main subatomic particles.

🔬
Scientific Discovery

Properties of Neutrons

  • No electric charge (neutral).
  • Mass is approximately 1 amu, slightly more than a proton.
  • Found inside the nucleus, along with protons.

🎙️ Neutrons are crucial for the stability of the nucleus, especially in heavier atoms. Without them, the positive charges of protons would repel each other, causing the nucleus to break apart due to electrostatic repulsion.

Atomic Number (Z)

  • The number of protons in an atom's nucleus.
  • Defines the identity of an element (e.g., all carbon atoms have 6 protons).
  • For a neutral atom, Atomic Number (Z) = Number of electrons.

🎙️ The atomic number is like an element's unique ID card. It's the number of protons, and it's what makes carbon 'carbon' and oxygen 'oxygen'. No two different elements have the same atomic number.

Mass Number (A)

  • The sum of protons and neutrons in the nucleus.
  • Represents the approximate total mass of an atom.
  • Mass Number (A) = Number of Protons + Number of Neutrons.

🎙️ The mass number tells us how 'heavy' an atom is. Since protons and neutrons make up most of the atom's mass, we sum them up to get the mass number. Electrons contribute very little to the overall mass.

Isotopes: Same Element, Different Neutrons

  • Atoms of the same element with the same atomic number (Z).
  • Have different mass numbers (A) due to different number of neutrons.
  • Example: Carbon-12 (6 protons, 6 neutrons) and Carbon-14 (6 protons, 8 neutrons).

🎙️ Isotopes are like siblings of the same element. They have the same number of protons, so they are the same element, but they can have different numbers of neutrons, leading to slightly different masses and properties.

Isobars: Different Elements, Same Mass Number

  • Atoms of different elements with different atomic numbers (Z).
  • Have the same mass number (A).
  • Example: Argon-40 (Z=18, A=40) and Calcium-40 (Z=20, A=40).

🎙️ Isobars are quite different from isotopes. They are atoms of *different* elements, meaning they have different numbers of protons, but they share the same total mass number, hence they have similar atomic weights.

What is Sound?

  • Sound is a form of energy.
  • It produces the sensation of hearing.
  • Sound is produced by vibrations.

🎙️ Sound is not just noise; it's a form of energy, just like light or heat. This energy travels to our ears and allows us to hear. The most important thing to remember is that sound always originates from something vibrating.

🎶
Sound Energy

How is Sound Produced?

  • Objects vibrate to create sound.
  • A vibrating object rapidly moves back and forth.
  • These vibrations disturb the surrounding medium.

🎙️ Think about a guitar string. When you pluck it, you can see it moving very fast, back and forth. This quick movement is called vibration, and it's this vibration that pushes the air around it, creating sound waves.

Propagation of Sound

  • Sound needs a medium to travel.
  • It cannot travel through a vacuum.
  • Sound travels as waves.

🎙️ Unlike light, sound cannot travel through empty space, a vacuum. It requires a material medium – like air, water, or a solid – to carry its vibrations from one place to another. It travels by making the particles of the medium vibrate.

🗣️
Sound Travelling

Sound Travels Through Different Mediums

  • Gases (like air)
  • Liquids (like water)
  • Solids (like a table)

🎙️ Imagine tiny particles in the air. When a sound is made, these particles bump into each other, passing on the vibration. This happens in liquids and solids too, which is why you can hear sounds underwater or through a wall.

Sound Travels as Waves

  • Sound waves are longitudinal waves.
  • Particles vibrate parallel to wave direction.
  • They create compressions and rarefactions.

🎙️ Sound doesn't travel like ripples in a pond. Instead, it pushes and pulls the medium's particles in the same direction the sound is moving. This creates areas where particles are squashed together (compressions) and spread apart (rarefactions).

Key Characteristics of a Sound Wave

  • Wavelength (λ)

  • Time Period (T)

🎙️ To truly understand sound, we need to describe its properties. Just like measuring a car's speed or length, we have specific terms to measure different aspects of a sound wave, such as its length, height, and how often it repeats.

Wavelength (λ)

  • Distance between two consecutive compressions.
  • Or two consecutive rarefactions.
  • Unit: meter (m).

🎙️ Imagine those squashed and spread-out areas. The wavelength is simply the distance from the center of one squashed area to the center of the next squashed area. It tells us how 'long' one complete cycle of the wave is.

📏
Measuring Wavelength
9 / 24

Amplitude (A)

  • Maximum displacement of medium particles.
  • From their mean position.
  • Determines the loudness of sound.

🎙️ Amplitude is like the height of the wave. A bigger amplitude means the particles are pushed and pulled further from their original spot. This greater displacement carries more energy, which we perceive as a louder sound.

↕️
Wave Height

Frequency (f)

  • Number of oscillations per unit time.
  • Determines the pitch of sound.
  • Unit: Hertz (Hz).

🎙️ Frequency tells us how many complete waves pass a point in one second. If many waves pass quickly, the frequency is high. This property is directly related to how high or low a sound appears to our ears, which we call pitch.

⏰
Oscillations per Second

Pitch and Loudness

  • Pitch depends on frequency.
  • Loudness depends on amplitude.
  • High frequency = high pitch (shrill sound).
  • High amplitude = loud sound.

🎙️ Think of a baby's cry – it's high-pitched (high frequency) and often loud (high amplitude). A deep drum beat is low-pitched (low frequency) and can also be very loud. So, frequency and amplitude are what give sounds their distinct character.

Speed of Sound

  • Speed varies in different mediums.
  • Fastest in solids, slowest in gases.
  • Example: Speed in air at 22°C is ~344 m/s.

🎙️ Sound travels at different speeds depending on the material it's passing through. In general, particles in solids are packed closer, allowing vibrations to pass faster. That's why you can hear a train coming by putting your ear to the tracks before you hear it through the air.

Reflection of Sound (Echoes)

  • Sound waves bounce off surfaces.
  • Similar to light reflection.
  • This phenomenon causes echoes.

🎙️ Just like a rubber ball bounces off a wall, sound waves also bounce when they hit a hard surface. This bouncing back of sound is called reflection, and it's the principle behind why we hear echoes.

⛰️
Sound Bouncing
15 / 24

Echoes in Real Life

  • Sound travels to a distant obstacle.
  • It bounces back to the source.
  • Minimum distance for clear echo: 17.2 meters.

🎙️ When you shout in a large, empty hall or near a mountain, you hear your own voice again after a short delay. This is an echo! For a clear echo, the sound needs enough time to travel to the obstacle and return, which requires a certain minimum distance.

🗣️🗣️
Direct & Reflected Sound

Reverberation

  • Multiple reflections of sound.
  • Occurs in large enclosed spaces.
  • Can make sound unclear if excessive.

🎙️ In some large rooms, instead of a single clear echo, you might hear a continuous persistence of sound due to many rapid reflections. This is called reverberation. While some reverberation can make music sound richer, too much can make speech hard to understand.

🗣️
Multiple Sound Paths

Applications of Sound Reflection

  • Megaphones (loudhailers)
  • Stethoscopes
  • Soundboards in auditoriums

🎙️ We use the reflection of sound in many practical ways. Megaphones are designed to reflect and direct sound in one direction, making it louder. Stethoscopes use multiple reflections inside their tubes to carry faint heartbeats to the doctor's ears.

Range of Human Hearing

  • Audible range: 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz.
  • Infrasound: Below 20 Hz (e.g., elephants).
  • Ultrasound: Above 20,000 Hz (e.g., bats, dolphins).

🎙️ Our ears are amazing, but they can only hear sounds within a specific frequency range. Sounds below 20 Hz are infrasound, and those above 20,000 Hz are ultrasound. Many animals, like bats and elephants, can hear frequencies we can't!

Applications of Ultrasound

  • Medical imaging (sonography)
  • Cleaning hard-to-reach parts
  • Detecting flaws in metal blocks

🎙️ Ultrasound, with its high frequency, has incredible uses. In medicine, it's used to see inside the body, like checking on a baby during pregnancy, without harmful radiation. It's also used in industries for precise cleaning and checking materials for defects.

🩺

SONAR: Sound Navigation And Ranging

  • Uses ultrasonic waves.
  • Measures distance, direction, speed of underwater objects.
  • Used in ships to detect submarines, fish schools.

🎙️ SONAR is a powerful technology that uses ultrasound, especially underwater. A device sends out ultrasonic waves, which travel through water, hit an object like a submarine, and bounce back. By measuring the time taken, we can calculate the object's distance.

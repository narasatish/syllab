What is Work in Science?

  • In daily life, 'work' means any activity.
  • In science, 'work' has a specific meaning.
  • It involves applying a force and causing movement.

🎙️ We often say we 'work' when we study or play. But in science, 'work' is more precise. It's not just about effort, but about causing an object to move.

💪
Effort leading to movement

Conditions for Work Done

  • Two main conditions must be met:
  • 1. A force must act on an object.
  • 2. The object must move (be displaced) due to the force.

🎙️ For work to be done, you need both a push or pull, AND the object needs to move because of that push or pull. If either is missing, no work is done.

1Apply Force
2Object Moves

Calculating Work Done

  • Work (W) is the product of force (F) and displacement (s).
  • Formula: W = F × s
  • This applies when force and displacement are in the same direction.

🎙️ This simple formula helps us calculate work. Remember, 's' stands for the distance the object moves in the direction of the applied force.

✖️
Multiply Force by Displacement

Units of Work

  • The SI unit of force is Newton (N).
  • The SI unit of displacement is metre (m).
  • So, the SI unit of work is Newton-metre (N m).
  • This unit is also called Joule (J).

🎙️ Just like we measure length in metres, we measure work in Joules. One Joule of work is done when a force of one Newton moves an object by one metre.

📏
Joule (J)

Example: Pushing a Wall

  • You push a wall with great force.
  • The wall does not move at all.
  • Since displacement (s) is zero, work done (W) is also zero.

🎙️ Even if you exert a lot of effort and feel tired, scientifically, you haven't done any work on the wall because it didn't move. This is a common misconception.

🧱
No Movement = No Work

Example: Lifting a Book

  • You lift a book from the table.
  • You apply an upward force.
  • The book moves upwards (displacement).
  • Work is done!

🎙️ Here, you apply force upwards, and the book moves upwards. Both conditions are met, so work is clearly done. This is an example of positive work.

Positive Work

  • Work is positive when force and displacement are in the same direction.
  • Example: A horse pulling a cart.
  • The force and movement are both forward.

🎙️ When the force you apply helps the object move in that same direction, we call it positive work. It increases the object's energy or speed.

⬆️
Same Direction
9 / 25

Negative Work

  • Work is negative when force and displacement are in opposite directions.
  • Example: Force of friction acting on a moving car.
  • Friction acts backward, but the car moves forward.

🎙️ Think of friction trying to stop a moving object. The force is opposite to the motion, so it does negative work. This usually reduces the object's energy.

⬇️
Opposite Direction
10 / 25

Zero Work

  • Work is zero when:
  • 1. There is no displacement (W = F × 0 = 0).
  • 2. Force is perpendicular to displacement (e.g., 90 degrees).

🎙️ A coolie carrying luggage on his head walks horizontally. The force of gravity on the luggage is downwards, but the displacement is horizontal. They are perpendicular, so no work is done by gravity.

What is Energy?

  • Energy is the capacity to do work.
  • If an object has energy, it can do work.
  • Energy exists in various forms.

🎙️ Energy is like the fuel that allows work to happen. Without energy, no work can be done. Everything that does work possesses energy.

🎙️ Energy isn't just one thing; it comes in many forms. We'll focus mainly on mechanical energy today, but it's good to know about others.

Kinetic Energy

  • Energy possessed by an object due to its motion.
  • Any moving object has kinetic energy.
  • The faster an object moves, the more kinetic energy it has.

🎙️ If something is moving, it has kinetic energy. A running child, a flying bird, a moving car – all have kinetic energy. It's directly related to speed.

🏃‍♂️
Energy of Motion
15 / 25

Calculating Kinetic Energy

  • Kinetic Energy (KE) = ½ × mass (m) × velocity (v)²
  • KE = ½ mv²
  • Unit of Kinetic Energy is Joule (J).

🎙️ This formula shows that kinetic energy depends on both the mass of the object and its speed squared. So, doubling the speed quadruples the kinetic energy!

🚀
Speed and Mass

Example: Moving Car

  • A car moving on the road has kinetic energy.
  • Due to its mass and speed, it can do work.
  • A moving car can push another object or cause damage.

🎙️ Imagine a car hitting something. The damage it causes is due to its kinetic energy. This energy allows it to exert a force over a distance.

Potential Energy

  • Energy stored in an object due to its position or state.
  • It's 'stored' energy, ready to do work.
  • Example: A stretched rubber band or an object held high.

🎙️ Potential energy is like a loaded spring or an object held high up. It has the *potential* to do work when released or allowed to move.

⛰️
Stored Energy
18 / 25

Gravitational Potential Energy

  • Energy stored due to an object's height above the ground.
  • Formula: Potential Energy (PE) = mass (m) × gravity (g) × height (h)
  • PE = mgh
  • Unit is Joule (J).

🎙️ The higher an object is, the more gravitational potential energy it has. This is why things fall when released – their potential energy converts to kinetic energy.

⚖️
Height and Mass

Example: Water in a Dam

  • Water stored at a height in a dam has potential energy.
  • When released, this potential energy converts to kinetic energy.
  • This kinetic energy can turn turbines to generate electricity.

🎙️ This is a fantastic real-world example. The height of the water gives it potential energy, which we then harness for a very useful purpose: electricity generation.

Law of Conservation of Energy

  • Energy can neither be created nor destroyed.
  • It can only be transformed from one form to another.
  • The total energy in the universe remains constant.

🎙️ This is a fundamental law of physics. It means you can't get energy from nothing, and energy doesn't just disappear. It just changes its form.

🔄
Energy never disappears

Energy Transformations

  • Fan: Electrical Energy → Mechanical Energy (and heat).
  • Bulb: Electrical Energy → Light Energy (and heat).
  • Falling object: Potential Energy → Kinetic Energy.

🎙️ Look around you, and you'll see energy transformations everywhere! A simple example is a falling ball, where its height energy turns into motion energy.

What is Power?

  • Power is the rate at which work is done.
  • It tells us how fast work is being done.
  • More power means more work done in less time.

🎙️ Imagine two people lifting the same heavy box. If one lifts it faster, they have more power, even if they do the same amount of work.

⚡
Speed of Work
23 / 25

Calculating Power

  • Power (P) = Work (W) / time (t)
  • Power (P) = Energy (E) / time (t)
  • The SI unit of power is Watt (W).

🎙️ One Watt means one Joule of work is done in one second. A more powerful engine can do the same work in less time, or more work in the same time.

⏱️
Work per Unit Time

Chapter Recap: Work and Energy

  • Work: Force × Displacement (W=Fs)
  • Energy: Capacity to do work (KE=½mv², PE=mgh)
  • Conservation of Energy: Energy transforms, never created/destroyed.
  • Power: Rate of doing work (P=W/t).

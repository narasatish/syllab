What is Matter?

  • Anything that has mass
  • And occupies space (volume)
  • Everything we see and touch is matter.

🎙️ In simple terms, matter is anything that takes up space and has weight. Look around you – your book, your pen, the air you breathe, even your own body – all are examples of matter.

Characteristics of Particles of Matter

  • Matter is made of tiny particles.
  • These particles are very, very small.
  • They have specific properties.

🎙️ Imagine breaking a rock into smaller and smaller pieces. Eventually, you'd reach particles so tiny you can't see them. Matter is fundamentally composed of such minute particles.

Particles have Space Between Them

  • Particles of matter are not tightly packed.
  • There are gaps or spaces between them.
  • We can see this when we dissolve sugar in water.

🎙️ When you add sugar to water, the sugar particles disappear into the spaces between water particles. The water level doesn't rise much, proving there's space!

Particles are Constantly Moving

  • Particles are never at rest.
  • They possess kinetic energy.
  • Diffusion and Brownian motion show this.

🎙️ Think about how the smell of an incense stick spreads across a room. This happens because its particles are constantly moving and mixing with air particles.

Particles Attract Each Other

  • Particles hold onto each other.
  • This force of attraction varies.
  • It's strongest in solids, weakest in gases.

🎙️ Try breaking an iron nail, a piece of chalk, and a rubber band. The iron nail is hardest to break because its particles attract each other very strongly.

States of Matter - Introduction

  • Matter exists in different forms.
  • Primarily three common states: Solid, Liquid, Gas.
  • These states depend on particle arrangement.

🎙️ Based on how their particles are arranged and how strongly they attract each other, matter generally exists in three main states: solid, liquid, and gas.

Matter exists in three states: solid, liquid, and gas. Each state has different properties
8 / 25

Solid State

  • Have a definite shape and volume.
  • Particles are tightly packed.
  • Very little space between particles.

🎙️ A brick is a perfect example of a solid. It keeps its shape and volume, no matter where you put it. The particles are very close and vibrate in fixed positions.

🧱🧱🧱🧱🧱🧱🧱🧱
Tightly Packed
9 / 25

Liquid State

  • Have a definite volume but no definite shape.
  • Take the shape of the container.
  • Particles can slide past each other.

🎙️ Water is a liquid. If you pour water into a glass, it takes the glass's shape. If you pour it into a bowl, it takes that shape. Its particles are still close but can move around.

💧💧💧💧💧💧
Flowing
10 / 25

Gaseous State

  • No definite shape or volume.
  • Particles are far apart and move freely.
  • Highly compressible.

🎙️ Air in a balloon is a gas. It fills the entire space of the balloon and can be easily compressed. Gas particles have very weak attractive forces and move randomly at high speeds.

🎈🎈🎈🎈
Free Moving

Can Matter Change its State?

  • Yes, matter can change from one state to another.
  • This happens by changing temperature or pressure.
  • Ice melting into water is a common example.

🎙️ Have you ever seen ice melt into water, or water turn into steam? These are everyday examples of matter changing its state. Let's see how this happens.

Effect of Temperature Change

  • Increasing temperature adds energy to particles.
  • Particles move faster and overcome attraction.
  • Leads to melting and boiling.

🎙️ When you heat a substance, its particles gain energy and start vibrating more vigorously. If enough heat is supplied, they can break free from their fixed positions or even escape completely.

Melting Point & Fusion

  • Melting is the process of a solid changing to liquid.
  • Melting point is the temperature at which this occurs.
  • Latent heat of fusion is energy absorbed during melting.

🎙️ At its melting point, a solid starts changing into a liquid. For ice, this is 0°C. The energy required to change 1 kg of solid into liquid at its melting point is called the latent heat of fusion.

Boiling Point & Vaporisation

  • Boiling is the process of a liquid changing to gas.
  • Boiling point is the temperature at which this occurs.
  • Latent heat of vaporisation is energy absorbed during boiling.

🎙️ When water reaches 100°C, it starts boiling and turns into steam. This temperature is its boiling point. The latent heat of vaporisation is the energy needed to change 1 kg of liquid into gas at its boiling point.

Sublimation

  • Direct change from solid to gas (or vice-versa).
  • Bypasses the liquid state.
  • Dry ice (solid CO2) is a common example.

🎙️ Some substances don't melt into a liquid when heated; they go directly from solid to gas. Camphor and naphthalene balls show this property. This process is called sublimation.

Effect of Pressure Change

  • Increasing pressure can bring gas particles closer.
  • Can change gases into liquids (liquefaction).
  • Example: LPG in gas cylinders.

🎙️ Just as temperature affects state, so does pressure. By increasing pressure and decreasing temperature, we can liquefy gases. Think of LPG cylinders, where gas is stored as a liquid under high pressure.

🤏
Pressing Together

Evaporation

  • Surface phenomenon where liquid changes to vapour.
  • Occurs below the boiling point.
  • Wet clothes drying is an example.

🎙️ Evaporation is different from boiling. It happens at any temperature below the boiling point, only from the surface of the liquid. This is why puddles dry up without reaching 100°C.

☀️
Liquid to Vapour

Factors Affecting Evaporation

  • Surface area: Larger area, faster evaporation.
  • Temperature: Higher temperature, faster evaporation.
  • Humidity: Lower humidity, faster evaporation.
  • Wind speed: Higher wind speed, faster evaporation.

🎙️ Ever wondered why clothes dry faster on a sunny, windy day? It's because of these factors. A larger surface area, higher temperature, lower humidity, and higher wind speed all increase the rate of evaporation.

Evaporation Causes Cooling

  • Particles absorb energy from surroundings.
  • This energy is used for phase change.
  • Leaves the surroundings cooler.

🎙️ When a liquid evaporates, the particles that escape take energy from the remaining liquid and its surroundings. This removal of energy causes the surroundings to feel cooler. That's why we feel cool after sweating.

Beyond the Three States: Plasma

  • Fourth state of matter.
  • Consists of super energetic and ionised particles.
  • Found in stars, neon signs, and fluorescent tubes.

🎙️ While we focus on solid, liquid, and gas, there are actually other states of matter. Plasma is one such state, often called the 'fourth state'. It's like a superheated, electrically charged gas.

🌟

Beyond the Three States: Bose-Einstein Condensate

  • Fifth state of matter.
  • Formed by cooling gases to extremely low densities.
  • Particles lose individual identity and behave as one.

🎙️ On the other extreme, the Bose-Einstein Condensate is formed at incredibly low temperatures, near absolute zero. It's a state where atoms 'condense' into a single quantum state.

What is Motion?

  • An object is in motion if its position changes with time.
  • Everything around us, from tiny ants to giant planets, is in motion.
  • Even when you sit still, you are moving with the Earth!

🎙️ Think about a car driving on a road or a bird flying in the sky. What do they have in common? Their position is continuously changing. This change in position over time is what we call 'Motion'.

Understanding Position: The Reference Point

  • To describe an object's position, we need a starting point.
  • This fixed point is called the 'Reference Point' or Origin.
  • Motion is always described relative to a reference point.

🎙️ Imagine you're telling a friend where your house is. You might say, 'It's near the big banyan tree.' Here, the banyan tree acts as your reference point. Without it, describing your house's location would be difficult.

Uniform Motion: Steady Pace

  • An object covers equal distances in equal intervals of time.
  • Its speed remains constant throughout the journey.
  • Example: A car moving at a constant speed on a straight, empty road.

🎙️ Think of a train moving smoothly on a straight track without speeding up or slowing down. If it travels 10 km in the first hour, it will travel another 10 km in the next hour. This steady, predictable movement is called uniform motion.

Non-Uniform Motion: Changing Pace

  • An object covers unequal distances in equal intervals of time.
  • Its speed changes, either increasing or decreasing.
  • Example: A bus moving through a crowded city street, stopping and starting.

🎙️ Now, imagine the same car driving through heavy traffic. It has to slow down, speed up, and stop frequently. It won't cover the same distance in every 5-minute interval. This type of motion, where speed changes, is non-uniform motion.

Distance: The Total Path Covered

  • It is the total length of the path covered by an object.
  • It is a scalar quantity (only magnitude, no direction).
  • SI unit: metre (m). Other units: kilometre (km), centimetre (cm).

🎙️ If you walk from your home to the market, the total length of the road you take is the distance. It doesn't matter if you took a winding path or a straight one; the distance is the sum of all the small steps you took.

📏
Total Path Length

Displacement: The Shortest Path

  • It is the shortest straight-line distance between the initial and final positions.
  • It is a vector quantity (has both magnitude and direction).
  • SI unit: metre (m). Can be zero if initial and final positions are same.

🎙️ Continuing the home-to-market example, displacement would be a straight line drawn from your home directly to the market, along with the direction. If you walk around a block and end up back at your starting point, your displacement is zero, even though you covered some distance!

➡️
Start to End Straight

Distance vs. Displacement: Key Differences

  • Distance: Total path, scalar, always positive.
  • Displacement: Shortest path, vector, can be positive, negative, or zero.
  • Example: Walking 5m East, then 5m West. Distance = 10m, Displacement = 0m.

🎙️ This is a crucial distinction. Distance tells you 'how much' ground was covered, while displacement tells you 'how far and in what direction' you are from your starting point. Remember, displacement gives you the 'net' change in position.

Speed: How Fast an Object Moves

  • It is the distance covered per unit time.
  • Formula: Speed = Distance / Time.
  • It is a scalar quantity. SI unit: metre per second (m/s).

🎙️ When you look at the speedometer in a car, it shows you the speed. It tells you how quickly the car is covering distance. A higher speed means more distance covered in the same amount of time.

Average Speed: Overall Pace

  • For non-uniform motion, we often calculate average speed.
  • Formula: Average Speed = Total Distance Covered / Total Time Taken.
  • Example: A car travels 100 km in 2 hours. Average speed = 50 km/h.

🎙️ If a car's speed changes during a journey, its instantaneous speed is constantly varying. Average speed gives us a single value that represents the overall rate of motion for the entire trip. It's like finding the 'typical' speed.

Velocity: Speed with Direction

  • It is the displacement per unit time.
  • Formula: Velocity = Displacement / Time.
  • It is a vector quantity. SI unit: metre per second (m/s).

🎙️ Velocity is similar to speed, but it includes direction. If a car is moving at 60 km/h towards the East, that's its velocity. If it's moving at 60 km/h towards the West, it has a different velocity, even though its speed is the same.

Average Velocity: Net Change with Direction

  • For non-uniform velocity, we calculate average velocity.
  • Formula: Average Velocity = Total Displacement / Total Time Taken.
  • If acceleration is uniform, Average Velocity = (Initial Velocity + Final Velocity) / 2.

🎙️ Just like average speed, average velocity describes the overall rate of change in position, but it considers the net displacement and its direction. It's especially useful when an object changes its speed or direction during the journey.

Speed vs. Velocity: What's the Difference?

  • Speed: Scalar, only magnitude, tells 'how fast'.
  • Velocity: Vector, magnitude and direction, tells 'how fast and where'.
  • An object can have constant speed but changing velocity (e.g., circular motion).

Worked Example: Calculating Speed and Velocity

  • A car travels 100 m East in 10 s, then 50 m West in 5 s.
  • Find: 1. Average Speed, 2. Average Velocity.

🎙️ Let's apply what we've learned. Notice how distance and displacement are calculated differently, leading to different values for average speed and average velocity. Pay attention to the direction for velocity.

1Total Distance = 100m + 50m = 150m
2Total Time = 10s + 5s = 15s
3Avg Speed = 150m / 15s = 10 m/s
4Total Displacement = 100m (E) - 50m (W) = 50m East
5Avg Velocity = 50m East / 15s = 3.33 m/s East

Acceleration: The Rate of Change of Velocity

  • It is the change in velocity per unit time.
  • Formula: Acceleration (a) = (Final Velocity - Initial Velocity) / Time.
  • It is a vector quantity. SI unit: metre per second squared (m/s²).

🎙️ When you press the accelerator pedal in a car, you are increasing its velocity, which means the car is accelerating. Acceleration tells us how quickly the velocity is changing, either in magnitude or direction.

Types of Acceleration: Speeding Up or Slowing Down

  • Positive Acceleration: Velocity increases (e.g., car speeding up).
  • Negative Acceleration (Retardation/Deceleration): Velocity decreases (e.g., braking car).
  • Zero Acceleration: Velocity is constant (uniform motion).

🎙️ Acceleration isn't just about getting faster. If an object is slowing down, it's experiencing negative acceleration. If it's moving at a steady velocity, its acceleration is zero. Understanding these types is key.

Distance-Time Graphs: Uniform Motion

  • For uniform motion, the distance-time graph is a straight line.
  • The slope of the line represents the speed of the object.
  • A steeper slope means higher speed.

🎙️ Imagine plotting the distance covered by a car moving at a constant speed against time. You'll get a perfectly straight line. This line clearly shows that equal distances are covered in equal time intervals.

📈

Distance-Time Graphs: Non-Uniform Motion

  • For non-uniform motion, the distance-time graph is a curved line.
  • The slope changes, indicating a changing speed.
  • A curve bending upwards means increasing speed (acceleration).

🎙️ If the car is speeding up or slowing down, its distance-time graph will no longer be a straight line. It will be a curve, showing that unequal distances are covered in equal time intervals. The curve's shape tells us how the speed is changing.

📉

Velocity-Time Graphs: Uniform Velocity

  • For uniform velocity, the velocity-time graph is a straight line parallel to the time axis.
  • The velocity remains constant.
  • The area under the graph gives the displacement.

🎙️ When an object moves with uniform velocity, its velocity doesn't change. So, if you plot velocity against time, you'll see a horizontal straight line. This graph is very useful for calculating displacement.

➡️

Velocity-Time Graphs: Uniform Acceleration

  • For uniform acceleration, the velocity-time graph is a straight line.
  • The slope of the line represents the acceleration.
  • A positive slope means positive acceleration, negative slope means retardation.

🎙️ If an object is accelerating uniformly, its velocity changes by equal amounts in equal time intervals. Plotting this on a velocity-time graph gives a straight line with a constant slope. The steeper the slope, the greater the acceleration.

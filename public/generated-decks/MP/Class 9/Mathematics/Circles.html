Slide 2: What is a Circle?

  • A collection of all points in a plane.
  • These points are at a fixed distance.
  • From a fixed point in the plane.

🎙️ Imagine a compass with one leg fixed at a point and the other leg drawing a path. The path traced is a circle! The fixed point is called the centre, and the fixed distance is called the radius. Every point on the boundary of the circle is exactly the same distance from the centre.

Slide 4: Parts of a Circle - The Centre

  • The fixed point inside the circle.
  • Usually denoted by 'O' or 'C'.
  • All points on the circle are equidistant from it.

🎙️ The centre is the heart of the circle. It's the reference point from which all measurements within the circle originate. Without a centre, a collection of points cannot be defined as a circle. It's the 'fixed point' we talked about earlier.

The central point

Slide 5: Parts of a Circle - Radius

  • A line segment from the centre to any point on the circle.
  • Denoted by 'r'.
  • All radii of the same circle are equal in length.

🎙️ The radius is that 'fixed distance' from the centre to the boundary of the circle. It's a very important measurement as it defines the size of the circle. Think of it as the arm of the compass that stretches from the pivot point to the pencil tip.

Measuring the radius

Slide 6: Parts of a Circle - Diameter

  • A line segment passing through the centre.
  • Connects two points on the circle.
  • It is twice the length of the radius (D = 2r).

🎙️ The diameter is the longest chord in a circle. It essentially cuts the circle into two equal halves. Remember, it must pass through the centre. If a line segment connects two points on the circle but doesn't pass through the centre, it's just a chord, not a diameter.

Slide 7: Parts of a Circle - Chord

  • A line segment connecting any two points on the circle.
  • The diameter is the longest chord.
  • A chord divides the circle into two segments.

🎙️ Any straight line that connects two points on the circumference of a circle is called a chord. Imagine drawing a straight line inside a circle without going outside. That's a chord! It's important to note that a chord does not necessarily pass through the centre.

Slide 8: Parts of a Circle - Arc

  • A continuous piece of a circle.
  • Part of the circumference.
  • Can be minor (smaller) or major (larger).

🎙️ An arc is simply a curved portion of the circle's boundary. If you cut a piece of string that follows the curve of the circle, that's an arc. Every chord divides the circle into two arcs: a minor arc and a major arc. Think of a rainbow, it's an arc!

Slide 9: Parts of a Circle - Segment

  • The region bounded by a chord and an arc.
  • Minor segment (smaller area).
  • Major segment (larger area).

🎙️ When a chord is drawn in a circle, it divides the circle's area into two parts. Each part is called a segment. The smaller area is the minor segment, and the larger area is the major segment. Think of cutting a pizza with a straight line – the two pieces are segments.

Slide 10: Parts of a Circle - Sector

  • The region bounded by two radii and an arc.
  • Minor sector (smaller angle).
  • Major sector (larger angle).

🎙️ A sector is like a slice of pizza or cake! It's formed by taking two radii and the arc between their endpoints. Just like segments, there are minor and major sectors, depending on the size of the angle formed by the two radii at the centre.

Slide 12: Congruent Circles

  • Circles are congruent if they have the same radii.
  • This means they are identical in size.
  • They can be placed exactly on top of each other.

🎙️ Just like congruent triangles have the same shape and size, congruent circles are circles that are exactly the same. The only condition for two circles to be congruent is that their radii must be equal. If the radii are different, the circles are not congruent.

Slide 13: Theorem 1: Equal Chords and Distance from Centre

  • Equal chords of a circle are equidistant from the centre.
  • Conversely, chords equidistant from the centre are equal.
  • This theorem connects chord length with distance from centre.

🎙️ This is a very important theorem. It tells us that if two chords have the same length, then their perpendicular distance from the centre of the circle will also be the same. And the reverse is also true: if two chords are the same distance from the centre, they must have the same length. This shows a beautiful symmetry in circles.

⚖️
Balance of distances

Slide 14: Worked Example 1: Applying Theorem 1

  • A circle has two equal chords, AB and CD.
  • If the distance of AB from the centre is 4 cm.
  • What is the distance of CD from the centre?

🎙️ Based on Theorem 1, if chords AB and CD are equal, then their distances from the centre must also be equal. So, if AB is 4 cm from the centre, CD will also be 4 cm from the centre. This theorem makes solving such problems very straightforward.

Slide 15: Theorem 2: Perpendicular from Centre to Chord

  • The perpendicular from the centre to a chord bisects the chord.
  • This means it divides the chord into two equal parts.
  • It's a powerful tool for calculations involving chords.

🎙️ This theorem is incredibly useful! If you draw a line from the centre of a circle that meets a chord at a 90-degree angle, that line will always cut the chord exactly in half. This creates right-angled triangles within the circle, which allows us to use Pythagoras theorem for calculations.

📐
Right angle and bisection

Slide 16: Worked Example 2: Applying Theorem 2

  • A chord of length 8 cm is 3 cm from the centre.
  • Find the radius of the circle.
  • Use Pythagoras theorem.

🎙️ Here's how we apply Theorem 2. The perpendicular from the centre bisects the 8 cm chord, making two segments of 4 cm each. Now, we have a right-angled triangle with sides 3 cm (distance from centre), 4 cm (half chord), and the hypotenuse being the radius. Using Pythagoras (3² + 4² = r²), we get r² = 9 + 16 = 25, so r = 5 cm.

Slide 17: Theorem 3: Angle Subtended by an Arc

  • The angle subtended by an arc at the centre.
  • Is double the angle subtended by it at any point.
  • On the remaining part of the circle.

🎙️ This theorem describes a crucial relationship between angles in a circle. Imagine an arc. The angle it forms at the centre is always twice as big as the angle it forms at any point on the circumference, as long as that point is not on the arc itself. This is a fundamental property for solving many angle-related problems.

Slide 18: Worked Example 3: Applying Theorem 3

  • If ∠AOB = 70° (AOB is an angle at the centre).
  • Find ∠ACB (ACB is an angle at the circumference).
  • Both angles are subtended by the same arc AB.

🎙️ According to Theorem 3, the angle at the centre (∠AOB) is double the angle at the circumference (∠ACB) subtended by the same arc. So, if ∠AOB is 70°, then ∠ACB will be half of that, which is 35°. This theorem simplifies finding unknown angles within a circle.

Slide 19: Cyclic Quadrilateral

  • A quadrilateral whose all four vertices.
  • Lie on the circumference of a circle.
  • Has special angle properties.

🎙️ A cyclic quadrilateral is a quadrilateral that fits perfectly inside a circle, with all its corners touching the circle's boundary. Not all quadrilaterals are cyclic; they need to satisfy this specific condition. This special arrangement gives rise to some unique properties regarding its angles.

Slide 20: Theorem 4: Sum of Opposite Angles of Cyclic Quadrilateral

  • The sum of either pair of opposite angles.
  • Of a cyclic quadrilateral is 180°.
  • This is a defining property.

🎙️ This theorem is a cornerstone for understanding cyclic quadrilaterals. If you have a quadrilateral inscribed in a circle, then any pair of its opposite angles will always add up to 180 degrees. For example, if angle A and angle C are opposite, then A + C = 180°. This property is very useful in solving problems related to angles in cyclic quadrilaterals.

Slide 21: Worked Example 4: Applying Theorem 4

  • In a cyclic quadrilateral ABCD, if ∠A = 80°.
  • Find the measure of ∠C.
  • Remember, opposite angles sum to 180°.

🎙️ Since ABCD is a cyclic quadrilateral, we know that the sum of its opposite angles is 180°. So, ∠A + ∠C = 180°. If ∠A is 80°, then ∠C will be 180° - 80°, which is 100°. This theorem makes finding unknown angles in cyclic quadrilaterals quite straightforward.

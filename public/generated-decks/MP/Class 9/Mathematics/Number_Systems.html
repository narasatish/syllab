What are Numbers?

  • Symbols used for counting, measuring, and labeling.
  • They help us quantify and compare things.
  • From simple counting to complex calculations.

🎙️ Think about it – how do we know how many pens we have, or how tall a building is? Numbers help us do exactly that! They are fundamental tools that allow us to make sense of quantities and measurements in our daily lives, from counting your fingers to calculating rocket trajectories.

Natural Numbers (N)

  • The counting numbers: 1, 2, 3, 4, ...
  • Start from 1 and go on infinitely.
  • Represented by the symbol 'N'.

🎙️ Natural numbers are the first numbers we learn as children – when we count objects. If you have a basket of apples, you start counting from one. These numbers are positive and never include zero or negative values. They are infinite, meaning there's no largest natural number.

Whole Numbers (W)

  • Natural numbers including zero: 0, 1, 2, 3, ...
  • Represented by the symbol 'W'.
  • All natural numbers are whole numbers.

🎙️ Whole numbers are just like natural numbers, but with one important addition: zero! Think about a situation where you have nothing. That 'nothing' is represented by zero. So, whole numbers start from zero and continue infinitely, including all positive counting numbers.

Integers (Z)

  • Include all whole numbers and their negatives: ..., -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, ...
  • Represented by the symbol 'Z' (from German 'Zahlen' meaning numbers).
  • No fractional or decimal parts.

🎙️ Integers expand our number system further by including negative numbers. Think about things that can go 'below zero,' like temperature or debt. Integers cover all whole numbers, both positive and negative, as well as zero. They are essential for representing quantities with direction.

Rational Numbers (Q)

  • Numbers that can be expressed as p/q, where p and q are integers and q ≠ 0.
  • Includes all Natural, Whole, and Integers.
  • Examples: 1/2, -3/4, 5 (which is 5/1), 0.75 (which is 3/4).

🎙️ Rational numbers are a big family! They include all the numbers we've discussed so far, plus fractions and decimals that can be written as fractions. The key rule is that the denominator 'q' cannot be zero, because division by zero is undefined. Think of sharing or dividing things.

🎙️ Imagine you have a pizza and you want to share it equally among 4 friends. Each friend gets 1/4 of the pizza – that's a rational number! Cooking recipes also use rational numbers extensively, like half a cup of flour or a quarter teaspoon of salt.

Properties of Rational Numbers

  • They are closed under addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division (except by zero).
  • Commutative and Associative for addition and multiplication.
  • Distributive property holds.

🎙️ Rational numbers behave very nicely with basic arithmetic operations. When you add, subtract, multiply, or divide two rational numbers, the result is always another rational number (as long as you don't divide by zero). They also follow important properties like the commutative and associative laws.

Finding Rational Numbers Between Two Rational Numbers

  • Method 1: Find the average (mean) of the two numbers.
  • Method 2: Convert to equivalent fractions with a common denominator.
  • There are infinitely many rational numbers between any two given rational numbers.

🎙️ This is an interesting concept! Unlike integers where there's a finite number between two integers, with rational numbers, you can always find more. One simple way is to take the average of the two numbers. You can repeat this process to find as many as you need.

1Find common denominator
2Increase numerator/denominator
3List numbers in between

Worked Example: Finding Rational Numbers

  • Find 5 rational numbers between 1/2 and 3/4.
  • Convert to common denominator: 1/2 = 2/4. Now we have 2/4 and 3/4.
  • Multiply numerator/denominator by 6 (or more): 2/4 = 12/24, 3/4 = 18/24.
  • Numbers are: 13/24, 14/24, 15/24, 16/24, 17/24.

🎙️ Let's try an example. We want to find 5 rational numbers between 1/2 and 3/4. First, make their denominators common. Then, multiply both numerator and denominator by a number slightly larger than the count you need (here, 5+1=6) to create space. This gives us many options between the new equivalent fractions.

Irrational Numbers

  • Numbers that CANNOT be expressed in the form p/q (where p,q are integers, q≠0).
  • Non-terminating and non-recurring decimal expansions.
  • Examples: √2, √3, π (Pi), 0.101101110...

🎙️ Now we meet the 'other' type of number – irrational numbers. These are numbers that cannot be written as a simple fraction. Their decimal expansions go on forever without repeating any pattern. Famous examples include the square root of 2 and Pi, which is used in circles.

Real-Life Example: Irrational Numbers

  • The diagonal of a square with side length 1 unit (√2).
  • The ratio of a circle's circumference to its diameter (π).
  • Certain artistic proportions.

🎙️ Irrational numbers appear naturally in geometry. For instance, if you have a square with sides of 1 unit, the length of its diagonal is exactly √2, which is an irrational number. Also, the famous Pi (π) that we use for circles is irrational. These numbers are crucial for precise measurements in the real world.

Representing Irrational Numbers on the Number Line

  • Use the Pythagorean theorem (a² + b² = c²).
  • For √2: construct a right triangle with legs of 1 unit.
  • Use a compass to transfer the length to the number line.

🎙️ It might seem tricky to place a non-terminating, non-repeating number on a line, but we can do it geometrically! For √2, we use Pythagoras theorem. We construct a right-angled triangle with both perpendicular sides of length 1 unit. The hypotenuse will be √2 units long. Then, we transfer this length to the number line using a compass.

1Draw a number line
2Construct unit right triangle
3Use compass to mark length
18 / 25

Worked Example: Locating √2 on the Number Line

  • Start at 0 on the number line.
  • Draw a line segment OA of length 1 unit.
  • At A, draw a perpendicular AB of length 1 unit.
  • Join OB. By Pythagoras, OB = √(1²+1²) = √2.
  • With O as center and OB as radius, draw an arc intersecting the number line at P. P represents √2.

🎙️ Let's walk through the steps for √2. First, draw a standard number line. From the origin (0), mark a point A at 1 unit. Now, from point A, draw a line perpendicular to the number line, also of 1 unit length, and call the end point B. Connect O to B. The length OB is √2. Finally, use a compass centered at O with radius OB to mark √2 on the number line.

Real Numbers (R)

  • The collection of all rational and irrational numbers.
  • Every point on the number line represents a unique real number.
  • Represented by the symbol 'R'.

🎙️ Real numbers are the 'grand total' of all the numbers we've discussed. They include every single number that can be placed on a number line – whether it's a simple counting number, a fraction, a negative number, or a non-repeating decimal like Pi. The number line is essentially a visual representation of all real numbers.

Decimal Expansions: Terminating

  • Decimal representation that ends after a finite number of digits.
  • Example: 1/2 = 0.5, 3/4 = 0.75.
  • These are always rational numbers.

🎙️ When you divide the numerator by the denominator of a fraction, sometimes the division stops, and you get a finite number of decimal places. These are called terminating decimals. For example, 1 divided by 2 is exactly 0.5. All terminating decimals are rational numbers because they can always be written as a fraction.

Decimal Expansions: Non-Terminating Recurring

  • Decimal representation that never ends, but a block of digits repeats.
  • Example: 1/3 = 0.333..., 2/7 = 0.285714285714...
  • These are also rational numbers.

🎙️ Sometimes, when you divide, the decimal part goes on forever, but a certain sequence of digits keeps repeating. These are called non-terminating recurring (or repeating) decimals. For example, 1 divided by 3 is 0.333... These, too, are rational numbers, and we can convert them back into p/q form.

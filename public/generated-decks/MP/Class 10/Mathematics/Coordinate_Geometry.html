The Cartesian Plane

  • Two perpendicular number lines.
  • X-axis (horizontal) and Y-axis (vertical).
  • They intersect at the Origin (0,0).

🎙️ The foundation of coordinate geometry is the Cartesian plane. It's formed by two lines, the X-axis and Y-axis, meeting at a right angle, like the corners of a room.

Dividing the Plane: Quadrants

  • The axes divide the plane into four parts.
  • Named Quadrant I, II, III, IV.
  • Each quadrant has specific sign conventions for coordinates.

🎙️ These four sections are called quadrants. Each quadrant has a unique pattern for the signs of the x and y coordinates, which helps us quickly identify where a point lies.

Coordinates of a Point (x, y)

  • An ordered pair (x, y) defines a point.
  • x is the abscissa (distance from Y-axis).
  • y is the ordinate (distance from X-axis).

🎙️ Every point on the Cartesian plane has a unique address, called its coordinates, written as (x, y). The 'x' tells us how far left or right it is, and the 'y' tells us how far up or down it is from the origin.

Plotting Points: Example (3, 2)

  • Start at the Origin (0,0).
  • Move 'x' units horizontally.
  • Move 'y' units vertically.
  • Mark the point.

🎙️ Let's plot a point, say (3, 2). We start at the center, move 3 steps to the right along the X-axis, and then 2 steps up parallel to the Y-axis. That's where our point (3,2) is!

1Start at (0,0)
2Move 3 units right
3Move 2 units up

  • Measures the length of a line segment.

  • Essential for many geometric problems.

Deriving the Distance Formula

  • Imagine a right-angled triangle.
  • The line segment is the hypotenuse.
  • Use Pythagoras Theorem: a² + b² = c².

🎙️ We can derive this formula using the famous Pythagoras Theorem. By drawing a right-angled triangle with the line segment as its hypotenuse, we can find the lengths of the other two sides using the coordinates.

📐
Pythagoras at Play

The Distance Formula

  • For two points (x1, y1) and (x2, y2).
  • Distance d = √((x2-x1)² + (y2-y1)²).
  • Always gives a positive value.

🎙️ Here is the final formula! Remember to subtract the x-coordinates and y-coordinates, square them, add them up, and then take the square root. Don't forget the square root at the end!

➗
The Key Formula

Worked Example: Distance

  • Find the distance between P(2,3) and Q(6,6).

🎙️ Let's try an example. First, label your points. Then, carefully substitute the values into the formula. Remember to follow the order of operations: parentheses, exponents, then addition, and finally the square root.

1Identify (x1,y1) = (2,3)
2Identify (x2,y2) = (6,6)
3Substitute into formula
4Calculate: √((6-2)² + (6-3)²) = √(4² + 3²) = √(16+9) = √25 = 5

Section Formula: Dividing a Line

  • Finds the coordinates of a point.
  • That divides a line segment in a given ratio.
  • Can be internal or external division.

🎙️ What if a point doesn't lie at the end of a line segment, but somewhere in between, dividing it into a specific ratio? That's where the Section Formula comes in handy.

✂️
Cutting a Line

Section Formula (Internal Division)

  • Point P(x,y) divides A(x1,y1) and B(x2,y2).
  • In ratio m1:m2 internally.
  • x = (m1x2 + m2x1) / (m1 + m2)
  • y = (m1y2 + m2y1) / (m1 + m2)

🎙️ This formula looks a bit complex, but it's logical. Notice how 'm1' multiplies with 'x2' and 'm2' with 'x1'. It's a cross-multiplication pattern that helps us find the weighted average position of the point.

➡️

Midpoint Formula: The Middle Ground

  • A special case of the Section Formula.
  • When the ratio is 1:1 (m1=m2=1).
  • x = (x1 + x2) / 2
  • y = (y1 + y2) / 2

🎙️ The Midpoint Formula is a simpler version of the Section Formula. If a point divides a line segment exactly in half, the ratio is 1:1. We simply average the x-coordinates and y-coordinates to find the midpoint.

Worked Example: Midpoint

  • Find the midpoint of the line segment joining (2,3) and (8,5).

🎙️ This is usually quicker to calculate. Just add the x-coordinates and divide by two, then do the same for the y-coordinates. This gives you the exact center of the line segment.

1Identify (x1,y1)=(2,3), (x2,y2)=(8,5)
2Apply x-coordinate formula
3Apply y-coordinate formula
4Calculate: ((2+8)/2, (3+5)/2) = (5,4)

Area of a Triangle Formula

  • For vertices A(x1,y1), B(x2,y2), C(x3,y3).
  • Area = 1/2 |x1(y2-y3) + x2(y3-y1) + x3(y1-y2)|.
  • The absolute value ensures a positive area.

🎙️ This formula might look long, but there's a pattern to remember it. Start with x1, multiply by (y2-y3), then x2 by (y3-y1), and x3 by (y1-y2). Always remember to take the absolute value at the end, as area cannot be negative.

➕➖
Complex Calculation

Worked Example: Area of Triangle

  • Find the area of the triangle whose vertices are (1,-1), (-4,6) and (-3,-5).

🎙️ This example requires careful substitution and calculation. Pay close attention to the signs, especially when subtracting negative numbers. Break down the calculation into smaller steps to avoid errors.

1Identify (x1,y1), (x2,y2), (x3,y3)
2Substitute values into formula
3Calculate terms: 1(6-(-5)) = 11
4-4(-5-(-1)) = 16
5-3(-1-6) = 21
6Area = 1/2 |11+16+21| = 1/2 |48| = 24 sq. units

Collinearity: Points on a Line

  • Three or more points are collinear.
  • If they lie on the same straight line.
  • Area of triangle formed by collinear points is zero.

🎙️ If three points are in a straight line, they cannot form a triangle. Therefore, a very important application of the Area of Triangle formula is to test for collinearity. If the area comes out to be zero, the points are collinear.

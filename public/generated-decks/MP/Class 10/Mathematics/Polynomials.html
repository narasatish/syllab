Building Blocks: Terms, Coefficients, Variables, Constants

  • Variables: Letters (x, y, z) that can take different values.
  • Constants: Fixed numerical values (3, -7, 10).
  • Coefficients: Numbers multiplying the variables (e.g., 3 in 3x).
  • Terms: Parts of an expression separated by + or - (e.g., 3x, 5y, -7).

🎙️ Every algebraic expression is made up of these four fundamental components. Understanding each one is key to understanding polynomials. For example, in '2x + 5', 'x' is the variable, '5' is the constant, '2' is the coefficient of 'x', and '2x' and '5' are the terms.

What is a Polynomial?

  • A special type of algebraic expression.
  • The powers of the variables must be non-negative integers (0, 1, 2, 3...).
  • Example: x² + 2x + 1.

🎙️ Now, let's define our main topic. A polynomial is essentially a 'well-behaved' algebraic expression. The most important rule is that the powers of the variables cannot be negative or fractions. They must be whole numbers like 0, 1, 2, and so on.

When is an Expression NOT a Polynomial?

  • If a variable has a negative power (e.g., x⁻¹ or 1/x).
  • If a variable has a fractional power (e.g., x¹ᐟ² or √x).
  • If a variable is under a square root or in the denominator.

🎙️ It's equally important to know what doesn't qualify as a polynomial. Expressions with variables in the denominator, under a square root sign, or with negative/fractional exponents are not polynomials. For instance, 1/x or √x are not polynomials.

The Degree of a Polynomial

  • The highest power of the variable in a polynomial.
  • It tells us about the complexity of the polynomial.
  • Example: In 4x³ + 2x² - 5, the degree is 3.

🎙️ The degree of a polynomial is a very important characteristic. It's simply the largest exponent of the variable present in the polynomial. For example, if you have 'x to the power of 5' as the highest power, then the degree is 5.

Classifying Polynomials by Degree

  • Linear Polynomial: Degree 1 (e.g., 2x + 3).
  • Quadratic Polynomial: Degree 2 (e.g., x² - 4x + 7).
  • Cubic Polynomial: Degree 3 (e.g., 5x³ + x² - 2x + 1).

🎙️ We classify polynomials based on their degree. A polynomial with degree 1 is linear, degree 2 is quadratic, and degree 3 is cubic. These classifications help us understand their properties and graphs.

Classifying Polynomials by Number of Terms

  • Monomial: One term (e.g., 5x, 7).
  • Binomial: Two terms (e.g., 2x + 3, x² - 4).
  • Trinomial: Three terms (e.g., x² - 4x + 7).

🎙️ Polynomials can also be classified by how many terms they have. A single term is a monomial, two terms make a binomial, and three terms form a trinomial. This classification is less about properties and more about structure.

Finding the Value of a Polynomial

  • To find P(a), simply substitute 'a' for 'x' in the polynomial P(x).
  • Example: If P(x) = x² + 2x + 1, then P(2) = 2² + 2(2) + 1 = 4 + 4 + 1 = 9.

🎙️ Finding the value of a polynomial at a specific point is like plugging a number into a formula. You just replace every instance of the variable with that number and then perform the calculations to get a single numerical output.

What are the Zeroes of a Polynomial?

  • A real number 'k' is a zero of a polynomial P(x) if P(k) = 0.
  • These are the values of 'x' that make the polynomial expression equal to zero.
  • Also known as roots of the polynomial equation.

🎙️ The zeroes of a polynomial are incredibly important! They are the special values of 'x' that, when substituted into the polynomial, make the entire expression evaluate to zero. Think of them as the 'solutions' to the polynomial equation P(x) = 0.

Finding Zeroes: Linear Polynomials

  • For a linear polynomial P(x) = ax + b (where a ≠ 0).
  • Set P(x) = 0, so ax + b = 0.
  • Solve for x: x = -b/a. A linear polynomial has exactly one zero.

🎙️ Finding the zero of a linear polynomial is straightforward. You just set the expression equal to zero and solve for 'x'. For example, if P(x) = 2x - 6, setting it to 0 gives 2x = 6, so x = 3. Thus, 3 is the zero.

Zeroes Geometrically: Linear Polynomial

  • The graph of a linear polynomial (ax+b) is a straight line.
  • The zero of the polynomial is the x-coordinate where the line intersects the x-axis.
  • A linear polynomial always intersects the x-axis at exactly one point.

🎙️ Visually, the zero of a linear polynomial is where its graph crosses the x-axis. Since a linear polynomial always forms a straight line that isn't parallel to the x-axis (unless a=0, which isn't a linear poly), it will always intersect the x-axis at exactly one point.

Zeroes Geometrically: Quadratic Polynomial

  • The graph of a quadratic polynomial (ax²+bx+c) is a parabola.
  • The zeroes are the x-coordinates where the parabola intersects the x-axis.
  • A quadratic polynomial can have 0, 1, or 2 distinct zeroes.

🎙️ For quadratic polynomials, the graph is a U-shaped curve called a parabola. This parabola can either not touch the x-axis (0 zeroes), touch it at one point (1 zero), or cross it at two distinct points (2 zeroes). This visual helps understand the number of solutions.

Zeroes Geometrically: Cubic Polynomial

  • The graph of a cubic polynomial (ax³+bx²+cx+d) is a curve with 'wiggles'.
  • The zeroes are the x-coordinates where the curve intersects the x-axis.
  • A cubic polynomial can have 1, 2, or 3 distinct zeroes.

🎙️ Cubic polynomials have more complex graphs, often with turns or 'wiggles'. They will always cross the x-axis at least once, but can cross it up to three times. This means a cubic polynomial will have at least one real zero and at most three real zeroes.

Zeroes and Coefficients: Quadratic Polynomials

  • For P(x) = ax² + bx + c, let α and β be the zeroes.
  • Sum of zeroes (α + β) = -b/a.
  • Product of zeroes (αβ) = c/a.

🎙️ This is a very important relationship for quadratic polynomials. Without even finding the zeroes, we can relate their sum and product directly to the coefficients 'a', 'b', and 'c' of the polynomial. This saves a lot of time in problem-solving.

🤝

Example: Zeroes and Coefficients (Quadratic)

  • Polynomial: x² - 5x + 6.
  • Zeroes: (x-2)(x-3) = 0, so x=2 and x=3.
  • Sum of zeroes: 2 + 3 = 5. From formula: -(-5)/1 = 5. Verified!
  • Product of zeroes: 2 × 3 = 6. From formula: 6/1 = 6. Verified!

🎙️ Let's put the relationship to practice. For the polynomial x² - 5x + 6, we can find the zeroes by factorization as 2 and 3. Now, let's check if our sum and product formulas match these values. Indeed, they do! This confirms the relationship.

Zeroes and Coefficients: Cubic Polynomials

  • For P(x) = ax³ + bx² + cx + d, let α, β, γ be the zeroes.
  • Sum of zeroes (α + β + γ) = -b/a.
  • Sum of products of zeroes taken two at a time (αβ + βγ + γα) = c/a.
  • Product of zeroes (αβγ) = -d/a.

🎙️ Similar to quadratic polynomials, there are relationships between the zeroes and coefficients for cubic polynomials as well. Here we have three zeroes, alpha, beta, and gamma, and three such relationships involving their sum, sum of pairwise products, and overall product.

Example: Verifying Relationship (Cubic)

  • Polynomial: x³ - 6x² + 11x - 6. Given zeroes: 1, 2, 3.
  • Sum: 1+2+3 = 6. Formula: -(-6)/1 = 6. Verified!
  • Pairwise Product Sum: (1×2)+(2×3)+(3×1) = 2+6+3 = 11. Formula: 11/1 = 11. Verified!
  • Product: 1×2×3 = 6. Formula: -(-6)/1 = 6. Verified!

🎙️ Let's verify these relationships for a cubic polynomial. If the zeroes are 1, 2, and 3 for the polynomial x³ - 6x² + 11x - 6, we can see that all three relationships hold true. This demonstrates the power of these formulas.

Division Algorithm for Polynomials

  • If P(x) and G(x) are two polynomials with G(x) ≠ 0.
  • Then we can find polynomials Q(x) (quotient) and R(x) (remainder).
  • Such that P(x) = G(x) × Q(x) + R(x).
  • Where R(x) = 0 or degree of R(x) < degree of G(x).

🎙️ Just like how we divide numbers, we can also divide polynomials. The division algorithm states that for any two polynomials, we can find a unique quotient and remainder. This is a fundamental concept used for factorization and finding roots.

Example: Polynomial Division

  • Divide P(x) = x² + 3x + 2 by G(x) = x + 1.
  • Steps: Divide leading terms, multiply divisor by quotient term, subtract, bring down next term, repeat.
  • Result: Quotient Q(x) = x + 2, Remainder R(x) = 0.

🎙️ Let's do a quick example. If we divide x² + 3x + 2 by x + 1 using long division, we find that the quotient is x + 2 and the remainder is 0. This means x + 1 is a factor of x² + 3x + 2.

2Divide leading terms
3Multiply and Subtract
4Repeat until remainder degree is less than divisor

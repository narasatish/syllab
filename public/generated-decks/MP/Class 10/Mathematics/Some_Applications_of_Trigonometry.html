What is Trigonometry, Anyway?

  • Study of relationships between sides and angles of triangles.
  • Especially useful for right-angled triangles.
  • Helps us find unknown lengths or angles.

🎙️ Before we dive into applications, let's quickly recall what trigonometry is. It's all about understanding how the angles in a triangle relate to the lengths of its sides, particularly in right-angled triangles.

The Foundation: Right-Angled Triangles

  • One angle is exactly 90 degrees.
  • Hypotenuse: Longest side, opposite the right angle.
  • Opposite and Adjacent sides depend on the reference angle.

🎙️ Remember, the core of our study lies in the right-angled triangle. Its unique properties, especially the 90-degree angle, allow us to apply trigonometric ratios effectively. Identify the hypotenuse, opposite, and adjacent sides carefully.

Revisiting Trigonometric Ratios

  • Sine (sin): Opposite / Hypotenuse
  • Cosine (cos): Adjacent / Hypotenuse
  • Tangent (tan): Opposite / Adjacent

🎙️ These three fundamental ratios are our tools. They link angles to side lengths. Make sure you remember SOH CAH TOA, as we will be using these ratios extensively today.

1SOH (Sin = Opp/Hyp)
2CAH (Cos = Adj/Hyp)
3TOA (Tan = Opp/Adj)

Key Concept 1: The Line of Sight

  • Imaginary line from observer's eye to the object.
  • Forms the basis for measuring angles.
  • Crucial for setting up your trigonometric problem.

🎙️ The line of sight is simply the direct path your eyes take when looking at an object. It's the hypotenuse of our imaginary right-angled triangle and is fundamental to understanding the angles we'll discuss next.

Line of Sight: An Example

  • You (observer) are looking at a bird (object).
  • The line from your eye to the bird is the line of sight.

🎙️ Think of yourself standing on the ground, watching a bird fly high above. The invisible line connecting your eye to that bird is your line of sight. This is the first step in visualizing any problem.

Key Concept 2: Angle of Elevation

  • Angle between the horizontal level and the line of sight.
  • Formed when the observer looks UP at an object.
  • The object is above the horizontal level.

🎙️ When you look up at something, like a skyscraper or an airplane, the angle your line of sight makes with the horizontal ground is called the angle of elevation. Remember, 'elevation' means going higher.

🎙️ Imagine you're holding a balloon string and looking up at your balloon. The angle formed between your horizontal gaze and your line of sight to the balloon is the angle of elevation. It's a common everyday occurrence.

Key Concept 3: Angle of Depression

  • Angle between the horizontal level and the line of sight.
  • Formed when the observer looks DOWN at an object.
  • The object is below the horizontal level.

🎙️ Conversely, when you're at a height and look down at an object, the angle formed with the horizontal is the angle of depression. Think of 'depression' as looking downwards from a higher point.

Application 1: Finding Heights of Objects

  • Measure distance from base and angle of elevation.
  • Use tangent ratio (Opposite/Adjacent).
  • Common for towers, trees, buildings.

🎙️ One of the most common applications is finding the height of objects that are too tall to measure directly. By knowing your distance from the base and the angle you look up, you can calculate the height using trigonometry.

Application 2: Finding Distances to Objects

  • Measure height of observer and angle of depression.
  • Use tangent ratio (Opposite/Adjacent) or cosine.
  • Useful for ships, cars, objects on ground.

🎙️ Another practical use is determining how far away an object is. If you're observing from a known height, like a cliff or a lighthouse, the angle of depression can help you find the horizontal distance to the object.

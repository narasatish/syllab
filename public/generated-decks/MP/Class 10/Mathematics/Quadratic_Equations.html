Defining a Quadratic Equation

  • A polynomial equation of degree 2.
  • Standard form: ax² + bx + c = 0.
  • Here, 'a', 'b', 'c' are real numbers, and a ≠ 0.

🎙️ A quadratic equation is essentially a quadratic polynomial set equal to zero. The 'a' coefficient is crucial; if 'a' were zero, the x² term would vanish, and it would become a linear equation.

Checking if an Equation is Quadratic

  • Simplify the given equation.
  • Rearrange into the standard form: ax² + bx + c = 0.
  • Check if the highest power of the variable is 2 and a ≠ 0.

🎙️ To determine if an equation is quadratic, always simplify it first. Expand any brackets, combine like terms, and then bring all terms to one side. Finally, look at the highest power of the variable.

Worked Example 1

  • Is (x+1)² = 2(x-3) a quadratic equation?

🎙️ Let's solve this step-by-step. First, expand (x+1)² to x² + 2x + 1. Then, expand 2(x-3) to 2x - 6. Equating them gives x² + 2x + 1 = 2x - 6. Simplifying, we get x² + 7 = 0, which is indeed a quadratic equation as a=1, b=0, c=7.

Roots of a Quadratic Equation

  • The values of the variable that satisfy the equation.
  • Also called 'solutions' or 'zeros'.
  • A quadratic equation has at most two roots.

🎙️ The roots are the specific values of 'x' that make the equation true, similar to how roots of a plant anchor it. Since the highest power is 2, a quadratic equation can have up to two distinct roots.

Finding Roots by Factorisation Method

  • Factorise the quadratic polynomial into two linear factors.
  • Set each factor equal to zero.
  • Solve for x to find the roots.

🎙️ This method relies on the property that if the product of two numbers is zero, then at least one of them must be zero. We break down the quadratic expression into simpler linear expressions.

1Factorise
2Set Factors to Zero
3Solve for x
11 / 25

Worked Example 2

  • Find the roots of x² - 3x - 10 = 0 by factorisation.

🎙️ We need two numbers that multiply to -10 and add to -3. These numbers are -5 and 2. So, we can write (x-5)(x+2) = 0. Setting each factor to zero, we get x-5=0, so x=5, and x+2=0, so x=-2. The roots are 5 and -2.

Completing the Square Method

  • Transforms the equation into a perfect square trinomial.
  • Helps solve equations that are not easily factorisable.

🎙️ This method is a powerful technique to solve any quadratic equation. The idea is to manipulate the equation algebraically so that one side becomes a perfect square, making it easy to isolate 'x'.

🧩
Building a Square

Steps for Completing the Square

  • Divide by 'a' if a≠1.
  • Move constant 'c/a' to RHS.
  • Add (b/2a)² to both sides.
  • Factor LHS as (x + b/2a)².
  • Take square root and solve.

🎙️ These steps systematically convert the quadratic expression into a form where we can easily take the square root. Remember to perform operations on both sides of the equation to maintain balance.

1Adjust 'a'
2Move 'c'
3Add (b/2a)²
4Factor
5Solve
15 / 25

Worked Example 3

  • Solve 2x² - 7x + 3 = 0 by completing the square.

🎙️ Divide by 2: x² - (7/2)x + 3/2 = 0. Move constant: x² - (7/2)x = -3/2. Add (7/4)²: x² - (7/2)x + (7/4)² = -3/2 + (7/4)². This gives (x - 7/4)² = 25/16. Taking square root: x - 7/4 = ±5/4. So, x = 7/4 ± 5/4, giving roots x=3 and x=1/2.

The Quadratic Formula

  • A direct formula to find roots.
  • Derived by applying completing the square to ax² + bx + c = 0.
  • Formula: x = [-b ± √(b² - 4ac)] / 2a

🎙️ The quadratic formula is a universal tool. It's a powerful shortcut derived from the completing the square method. You can use it to solve any quadratic equation, regardless of whether it can be easily factorised.

Using the Quadratic Formula

  • Identify 'a', 'b', 'c' from the standard form.
  • Substitute values into the formula.
  • Calculate the two possible values for x.

🎙️ Always ensure your equation is in the standard form ax² + bx + c = 0 before identifying 'a', 'b', and 'c'. Pay close attention to the signs of the coefficients when substituting them into the formula.

1Identify a,b,c
2Substitute
3Calculate x
18 / 25

Worked Example 4

  • Solve 3x² - 5x + 2 = 0 using the quadratic formula.

🎙️ Here, a=3, b=-5, c=2. Substitute into the formula: x = [5 ± √((-5)² - 4*3*2)] / (2*3). This simplifies to x = [5 ± √(25 - 24)] / 6 = [5 ± √1] / 6. So, x = (5+1)/6 = 1 and x = (5-1)/6 = 2/3. The roots are 1 and 2/3.

Nature of Roots: Discriminant (D)

  • The term (b² - 4ac) inside the square root.
  • Denoted by 'D' or 'Δ'.
  • Helps determine the type of roots without actually solving.

🎙️ The discriminant is a crucial part of the quadratic formula. It tells us a lot about the roots, specifically whether they are real or imaginary, and if they are distinct or equal, all before calculating them.

🤔
Predicting Roots
20 / 25

Cases of Discriminant

  • D > 0: Two distinct real roots.
  • D = 0: Two equal real roots.
  • D < 0: No real roots (complex roots).

🎙️ These three cases cover all possibilities for the nature of roots. If D is positive, you'll get two different real numbers. If D is zero, the two roots will be identical. If D is negative, you won't find any real number solutions.

D > 0 (Distinct)
D = 0 (Equal)
D < 0 (No Real)
21 / 25

Worked Example 5

  • Find the nature of roots for 2x² - 4x + 3 = 0.

🎙️ Here, a=2, b=-4, c=3. Let's calculate the discriminant D = b² - 4ac = (-4)² - 4*2*3 = 16 - 24 = -8. Since D = -8, which is less than 0, the equation has no real roots. It has complex roots.

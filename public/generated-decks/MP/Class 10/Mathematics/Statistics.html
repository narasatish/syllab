Measures of Central Tendency

  • Single value representing the center of data.
  • Mean, Median, and Mode are key measures.
  • Helps summarize large datasets.

🎙️ When we have a lot of data, we often want a single number that can represent the 'typical' or 'central' value. These are called measures of central tendency. Mean, Median, and Mode are the three most common tools we use for this.

Mean (Arithmetic Mean)

  • The average of all observations.
  • Sum of all observations divided by the number of observations.
  • Formula: Mean (x̄) = Σx / n.

🎙️ The mean is what most people commonly refer to as the 'average'. You add up all the values in your data set and then divide by how many values there are. It's like sharing a total amount equally among everyone.

➕
Sum and Divide
10 / 25

Example: Mean for Ungrouped Data

  • Marks of 5 students: 10, 15, 20, 25, 30.
  • Sum = 10 + 15 + 20 + 25 + 30 = 100.
  • Number of students = 5.
  • Mean = 100 / 5 = 20.

🎙️ Let's calculate the mean for a simple set of marks. We sum all the marks together, which gives us 100. Since there are 5 students, we divide 100 by 5, and we get 20. So, the average mark for these 5 students is 20.

Mean for Grouped Data: Direct Method

  • For data organized into frequency distribution.
  • Find class mark (midpoint) for each interval.
  • Formula: x̄ = Σ(fᵢxᵢ) / Σfᵢ.

🎙️ When data is grouped, we can't just sum individual values. Instead, we use the class mark, which is the midpoint of each class interval. We then multiply each class mark by its frequency, sum these products, and divide by the total frequency.

3Sum Products

Example: Mean (Direct Method)

  • Marks (0-10, 10-20, etc.) and Frequencies (fᵢ).
  • Calculate Class Marks (xᵢ) for each interval.
  • Multiply fᵢxᵢ for each row, then sum.
  • Divide Σ(fᵢxᵢ) by Σfᵢ.

🎙️ Let's walk through an example. For each mark range, we find the midpoint. Then, we multiply this midpoint by the number of students in that range. We add all these products and finally divide by the total number of students to get the mean.

Mean for Grouped Data: Assumed Mean Method

  • Simplifies calculations for large numbers.
  • Choose an 'assumed mean' (A) from class marks.
  • Calculate deviations (dᵢ = xᵢ - A).
  • Formula: x̄ = A + [Σ(fᵢdᵢ) / Σfᵢ].

🎙️ Sometimes, the numbers in our data can be very large, making direct calculations tedious. The assumed mean method helps simplify this. We pick a value, usually from the middle of our class marks, as our 'assumed mean' to make the numbers smaller and easier to work with.

Median: The Middle Value

  • The middle value of a dataset.
  • Data must be arranged in ascending or descending order.
  • Divides the data into two equal halves.

🎙️ The median is literally the middle value in a set of data. To find it, the most important step is to first arrange all your numbers in order, either from smallest to largest or largest to smallest. Once ordered, the number right in the middle is your median.

↔️
Finding the Middle
16 / 25

Example: Median for Ungrouped Data

  • Odd number of observations: 10, 12, 15, 18, 20. Median = 15.
  • Even number of observations: 10, 12, 15, 18, 20, 22. Median = (15+18)/2 = 16.5.
  • Always arrange data first!

🎙️ If you have an odd number of data points, the median is simply the value in the exact middle after ordering. If you have an even number, there will be two middle values. In this case, the median is the average of those two middle values. Remember, ordering is key!

Median for Grouped Data

  • Uses cumulative frequency to find the median class.
  • Formula: Median = L + [((n/2) - cf) / f] * h.
  • L = lower limit of median class, n = total frequency.

🎙️ For grouped data, we can't pinpoint an exact middle value directly. Instead, we first find the 'median class' using cumulative frequency. Once we identify this class, we use a specific formula that incorporates its lower limit, frequency, and the cumulative frequency of the class before it.

Mode: The Most Frequent

  • The value that appears most often in a dataset.
  • Can have one mode (unimodal), multiple modes (multimodal), or no mode.
  • Very useful for categorical data.

🎙️ The mode is the easiest to understand: it's simply the value that shows up most frequently in your data. Think of it as the 'most popular' item. A dataset can have one mode, several modes if multiple values tie for the highest frequency, or even no mode if all values appear only once.

Mode for Grouped Data

  • First identify the 'modal class' (class with highest frequency).
  • Formula: Mode = L + [(f₁ - f₀) / (2f₁ - f₀ - f₂)] * h.
  • L = lower limit of modal class, f₁ = frequency of modal class.

🎙️ For grouped data, we can't just pick a single number. We first find the 'modal class,' which is the class interval with the highest frequency. Then, we use a specific formula that considers the frequency of the modal class and the frequencies of the classes immediately before and after it.

Understanding Surface Area

  • The total area of all the faces or surfaces of a 3D object.
  • It's like the 'skin' or outer covering of the object.
  • Measured in square units (e.g., cm², m²).

🎙️ Imagine you want to paint a gift box. The amount of paint you'd need to cover the entire outside of the box is its surface area. It's the sum of the areas of all its visible faces.

Units of Surface Area

  • Surface area is always measured in square units.
  • Common units: square centimetres (cm²), square metres (m²), square kilometres (km²).
  • Always remember the 'square' part!

🎙️ Just like we measure length in centimetres, we measure area in square centimetres. This signifies that area covers a two-dimensional space.

Understanding Volume

  • The amount of space occupied by a 3D object.
  • It's the 'capacity' or how much a container can hold.
  • Measured in cubic units (e.g., cm³, m³, litres).

🎙️ Now, think about how much water a bottle can hold, or how much sand fills a bucket. That 'amount of space' inside or occupied by the object is called its volume.

Units of Volume

  • Volume is always measured in cubic units.
  • Common units: cubic centimetres (cm³), cubic metres (m³), litres (L).
  • Remember 1 litre = 1000 cm³.

🎙️ For volume, because it's three-dimensional, we use cubic units. So, a small cube with 1 cm sides has a volume of 1 cubic centimetre.

Calculating Surface Area of Combined Solids

  • Identify the individual basic shapes.
  • Calculate the surface area of only the *exposed* parts of each shape.
  • Sum up all the exposed surface areas to get the total.

🎙️ When combining solids, remember that the surfaces where they join are hidden and are not part of the total surface area. We only consider the surfaces that are visible from the outside.

Worked Example: Surface Area of a Toy Top

  • Total height of toy = 5 cm, diameter = 3.5 cm.
  • Radius (r) = 1.75 cm. Height of hemisphere = 1.75 cm.
  • Height of cone (h_cone) = 5 - 1.75 = 3.25 cm.
  • Slant height of cone (l) = √(r² + h_cone²) = 3.6 cm (approx).
  • CSA of hemisphere = 2πr² = 19.25 cm² (approx).
  • CSA of cone = πrl = 19.8 cm² (approx).
  • Total SA = 19.25 + 19.8 = 39.05 cm².

🎙️ Here, we first break down the toy into its component shapes: a cone and a hemisphere. Then, we calculate their respective curved surface areas and add them up, being careful with the dimensions.

Calculating Volume of Combined Solids

  • Identify the individual basic shapes.
  • Calculate the volume of *each* individual shape.
  • Sum up all the individual volumes to get the total volume.

🎙️ Unlike surface area, when calculating volume for combined solids, we simply add the volumes of the individual components. The space occupied by each part contributes directly to the total space.

Worked Example: Volume of a Medicine Capsule

  • Total length = 14 mm, diameter = 5 mm.
  • Radius (r) = 2.5 mm. Height of each hemisphere = 2.5 mm.
  • Height of cylindrical part (h_cyl) = 14 - (2.5 + 2.5) = 9 mm.
  • Volume of cylinder = πr²h_cyl = π(2.5)²(9) = 176.78 mm³ (approx).
  • Volume of 2 hemispheres = 2 * (2/3)πr³ = (4/3)π(2.5)³ = 65.45 mm³ (approx).
  • Total Volume = 176.78 + 65.45 = 242.23 mm³.

🎙️ In this example, the key is to correctly identify the height of the cylindrical part by subtracting the radii of the two hemispheres from the total length. Then, apply the volume formulas for each part.

Conversion of Solids from One Shape to Another

  • When a solid is melted, reshaped, or recast into another form.
  • The *volume* of the material used remains constant.
  • This principle helps find unknown dimensions of the new shape.

🎙️ Imagine you have a block of clay and you reshape it into a different form. The amount of clay doesn't change, only its shape. This is the core idea behind conversion of solids.

Key Principle: Volume Conservation

  • Volume of the original solid = Volume of the new solid.
  • This is true regardless of the change in shape.
  • We use this equality to solve problems.

🎙️ This principle is fundamental. The material itself doesn't disappear or get created, so its total volume stays the same, even if its shape changes dramatically.

Worked Example: Cone to Sphere Conversion

  • A metallic cone (h=24 cm, r=6 cm) is melted and recast into a sphere.
  • Volume of cone = (1/3)πr²h = (1/3)π(6)²(24) = 288π cm³.
  • Let radius of sphere be R. Volume of sphere = (4/3)πR³.
  • Equating volumes: 288π = (4/3)πR³.
  • Solving for R: 288 = (4/3)R³ => R³ = 216 => R = 6 cm.

🎙️ Here, we set the formula for the volume of the cone equal to the formula for the volume of the sphere. The 'pi' cancels out, simplifying the calculation for the unknown radius of the sphere.

Introducing the Frustum of a Cone

  • A frustum is a portion of a cone when it's cut by a plane parallel to its base.
  • It has two circular bases of different radii.
  • Common examples include buckets, drinking glasses, and lampshades.

🎙️ Imagine taking a large ice cream cone and cutting off its top part parallel to the base. What remains is a frustum. It's like a cone with its tip removed.

🗑️

Formulas for Frustum of a Cone

  • Volume = (1/3)πh(R² + r² + Rr) (where R, r are radii, h is height).
  • Curved Surface Area (CSA) = πl(R + r) (where l is slant height).
  • Total Surface Area (TSA) = CSA + πR² + πr².

🎙️ These are the specific formulas for a frustum. R is the radius of the larger base, r is the radius of the smaller base, and h is the perpendicular height. 'l' is the slant height.

Worked Example: Frustum Volume

  • A bucket has radii R=28 cm, r=7 cm and height h=45 cm.
  • Volume = (1/3)πh(R² + r² + Rr).
  • V = (1/3) * (22/7) * 45 * (28² + 7² + 28*7).
  • V = (1/3) * (22/7) * 45 * (784 + 49 + 196).
  • V = (1/3) * (22/7) * 45 * 1029 = 48510 cm³.

🎙️ This example demonstrates how to apply the frustum volume formula directly. Carefully substitute the given radii and height, then perform the calculations step by step.

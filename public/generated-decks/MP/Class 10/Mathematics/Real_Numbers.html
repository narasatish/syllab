Rational Numbers: Expressing as Fractions

  • Numbers that can be expressed in the form p/q, where p and q are integers and q ≠ 0.
  • Examples: 1/2, -3/4, 5 (which is 5/1).
  • Their decimal expansions are either terminating or repeating.

🎙️ Rational numbers are essentially fractions. If you can write a number as a fraction of two integers, it's rational. This includes all integers, as any integer 'a' can be written as 'a/1'.

Irrational Numbers: Never-Ending Decimals

  • Numbers that cannot be expressed in the form p/q.

🎙️ Irrational numbers are the 'outsiders' that cannot be written as simple fractions. Their decimal forms go on forever without any repeating pattern. Famous examples include the square root of 2 and pi.

Euclid's Division Lemma (EDL): The Basics

  • Given positive integers 'a' and 'b', there exist unique integers 'q' (quotient) and 'r' (remainder).
  • Such that a = bq + r, where 0 ≤ r < b.
  • Essentially a restatement of the long division process.

🎙️ Euclid's Division Lemma is a fundamental concept. It formally states what happens when you divide one positive integer by another. You always get a unique quotient and a unique remainder, and the remainder is always smaller than the divisor.

Real-Life Example: EDL in Everyday Situations

  • Distributing sweets: If you have 17 sweets and want to give 3 to each child, how many children get sweets and how many are left?
  • Packing items: Packing 25 books into boxes that hold 4 books each.
  • Time calculation: How many full hours are in 150 minutes?

🎙️ Let's say you have 17 sweets (a) and want to give 3 sweets (b) to each child. You can give sweets to 5 children (q) and 2 sweets (r) will be left over. So, 17 = 3 × 5 + 2. This is a perfect example of EDL.

Euclid's Division Algorithm (EDA): Finding HCF

  • A technique to compute the Highest Common Factor (HCF) of two positive integers.
  • It involves applying Euclid's Division Lemma repeatedly.
  • The HCF is the divisor when the remainder becomes zero.

🎙️ Euclid's Division Algorithm is a systematic way to find the HCF of two numbers. We keep dividing the previous divisor by the remainder until we get a remainder of zero. The last non-zero divisor is our HCF.

1Apply EDL to a and b
2If r = 0, b is HCF
3If r ≠ 0, apply EDL to b and r
4Continue until remainder is 0
15 / 25

Worked Example: HCF of 135 and 225

  • Step 1: Apply EDL to 225 and 135. 225 = 135 × 1 + 90.
  • Step 2: Since remainder 90 ≠ 0, apply EDL to 135 and 90. 135 = 90 × 1 + 45.
  • Step 3: Since remainder 45 ≠ 0, apply EDL to 90 and 45. 90 = 45 × 2 + 0.
  • The remainder is now 0. The divisor at this stage is 45. Therefore, HCF(135, 225) = 45.

🎙️ Let's work through an example. We start by dividing 225 by 135. Then, we take the divisor (135) and the remainder (90) and repeat the process. We continue until the remainder is zero, and the last divisor is our HCF.

The Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic (FTA): Prime Factorization

  • Every composite number can be expressed (factorized) as a product of primes.
  • This factorization is unique, apart from the order in which the prime factors occur.
  • Example: 12 = 2 × 2 × 3 or 2² × 3.

🎙️ The Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic is a cornerstone of number theory. It tells us that every composite number can be broken down into a unique set of prime numbers, like building blocks. The order of these prime factors doesn't change the uniqueness.

Worked Example: LCM and HCF using FTA

  • Find HCF and LCM of 6 and 20 using prime factorization.
  • Prime factorization: 6 = 2 × 3; 20 = 2² × 5.
  • HCF: Smallest power of common prime factors. HCF(6, 20) = 2¹ = 2.
  • LCM: Highest power of all prime factors. LCM(6, 20) = 2² × 3¹ × 5¹ = 4 × 3 × 5 = 60.

🎙️ To find HCF and LCM using FTA, first write down the prime factors for each number. For HCF, take the common prime factors with the lowest power. For LCM, take all prime factors with their highest powers.

Important Relation: Product of Numbers

  • For any two positive integers 'a' and 'b', the product of their HCF and LCM is equal to the product of the numbers themselves.
  • Formula: HCF(a, b) × LCM(a, b) = a × b.
  • This relation is useful for verifying calculations or finding one value if others are known.

🎙️ This is a very important property that connects HCF and LCM with the original numbers. It's a handy tool for checking your answers or quickly finding a missing value if you know the other three. Remember, this applies only to two numbers.

Revisiting Decimals: Terminating vs. Non-terminating

  • Rational numbers have decimal expansions that are either terminating (e.g., 0.5, 0.25) or non-terminating repeating (e.g., 0.333..., 0.142857...).
  • Irrational numbers have decimal expansions that are non-terminating and non-repeating (e.g., √2 ≈ 1.4142135..., π ≈ 3.1415926...).

🎙️ The nature of a number's decimal expansion tells us whether it's rational or irrational. Rational numbers either stop or repeat a pattern, while irrational numbers go on forever without any repeating pattern.

Proving Irrationality: By Contradiction

  • A common method to prove that a number is irrational.
  • Assume the number is rational (can be written as p/q in simplest form).
  • Show that this assumption leads to a contradiction (e.g., p and q have a common factor, but they were assumed coprime).
  • Therefore, the initial assumption must be false, and the number is irrational.

🎙️ Proving irrationality often involves a technique called 'proof by contradiction'. We start by assuming the opposite – that the number IS rational – and then show that this assumption creates a logical inconsistency, proving our initial assumption wrong.

What is an Equation?

  • A mathematical statement showing two expressions are equal.
  • It contains an unknown value, called a variable.
  • Example: x + 5 = 10

🎙️ Think of an equation like a balanced scale. Whatever is on the left side must be equal to what's on the right side. We use equations to find the value of an unknown, usually represented by letters like x, y, or z.

What makes an Equation 'Linear'?

  • The highest power (degree) of the variable is exactly 1.
  • When graphed, a linear equation always forms a straight line.
  • Example: 2x + 7 = 15

🎙️ The term 'linear' comes from the word 'line'. This means that if you were to plot the solutions of such an equation on a graph, they would all fall on a single straight line. The key is that the variable is never squared or cubed, just to the power of one.

Understanding 'Two Variables'

  • Instead of one unknown, we have two different unknowns.
  • Commonly represented by 'x' and 'y'.
  • Each variable can take different values.

🎙️ Sometimes, in real-life problems, we need to find two different unknown quantities simultaneously. For instance, the cost of two different items. This is where 'two variables' come into play, allowing us to represent each unknown independently.

Linear Equation in Two Variables (LEITV)

  • Combines all three concepts: linear, equation, two variables.
  • Standard form: ax + by + c = 0.
  • Here, a, b, c are real numbers, and a ≠ 0, b ≠ 0.

🎙️ So, a linear equation in two variables is an equation where we have two variables, typically x and y, and the highest power of each variable is one. The standard form helps us organize and recognize these equations easily. Remember, a and b cannot be zero, otherwise, it wouldn't be a two-variable equation!

What is a 'Pair' of Linear Equations?

  • When we have two linear equations with the same two variables.
  • We seek values for 'x' and 'y' that satisfy BOTH equations.
  • Standard form: a₁x + b₁y + c₁ = 0 and a₂x + b₂y + c₂ = 0.

🎙️ The 'pair' is the core of this chapter. Having two equations allows us to find a unique solution for 'x' and 'y' in most cases. We are looking for values that work simultaneously for both conditions or scenarios described by the equations.

🎙️ Here, we have two different conditions, each forming a linear equation. To find the exact cost of 1kg of apples and 1kg of oranges, we need to solve this 'pair' of equations. This is where the methods we learn will be very useful.

Graphical Method: Visualizing Solutions

  • Each linear equation represents a straight line on a graph.
  • The solution to the pair of equations is the point where the lines intersect.
  • Plotting points helps us draw the lines accurately.

🎙️ The graphical method is a great way to understand the concept of a solution. When we plot both lines on the same graph, their intersection point, if any, gives us the values of x and y that satisfy both equations. It's like finding a common meeting point for two paths.

📏

Graphical Method - Case 1: Intersecting Lines

  • The two lines cross each other at exactly one point.
  • This point is the unique solution (x, y) to the pair of equations.
  • Condition: a₁/a₂ ≠ b₁/b₂.

🎙️ This is the most common scenario. When the lines intersect, it means there's one specific pair of x and y values that satisfies both equations. We call this a 'consistent' system with a 'unique' solution.

✖️
One Point
12 / 25

Worked Example: Intersecting Lines

  • Solve: x + y = 5 and x - y = 1.
  • Plot points for each equation (e.g., for x+y=5: (0,5), (5,0)).
  • Observe where the lines cross on the graph.

🎙️ Let's try an example. For x+y=5, points like (0,5) and (5,0) work. For x-y=1, points like (0,-1) and (1,0) work. If you plot these and draw the lines, you'll see they intersect at (3, 2). This means x=3, y=2 is the unique solution.

Graphical Method - Case 2: Parallel Lines

  • The two lines never cross, they run parallel to each other.
  • There is no common point, hence no solution to the pair.
  • Condition: a₁/a₂ = b₁/b₂ ≠ c₁/c₂.

🎙️ In this case, the equations describe two conditions that can never be simultaneously true. The lines represent these conditions, and since they never meet, there's no solution that satisfies both. We call this an 'inconsistent' system.

Worked Example: Parallel Lines

  • Solve: x + 2y = 4 and 2x + 4y = 12.
  • Plot points for each equation.
  • Notice that the lines will be parallel and never intersect.

🎙️ For x+2y=4, points like (0,2) and (4,0) work. For 2x+4y=12, points like (0,3) and (6,0) work. When you plot these, you will clearly see two distinct parallel lines, indicating no solution.

Graphical Method - Case 3: Coincident Lines

  • The two lines lie exactly on top of each other.
  • Every point on the line is a common solution; infinitely many solutions.
  • Condition: a₁/a₂ = b₁/b₂ = c₁/c₂.

🎙️ This happens when the two equations are essentially the same, or one is a multiple of the other. Since they represent the exact same line, every single point on that line is a solution. We call this a 'consistent' system with 'infinitely many' solutions.

♾️

Worked Example: Coincident Lines

  • Solve: x + y = 3 and 2x + 2y = 6.
  • Plot points for each equation.
  • Observe that the points for both equations fall on the same line.

🎙️ For x+y=3, points like (0,3) and (3,0) work. For 2x+2y=6, points like (0,3) and (3,0) also work! This confirms they are the same line. Any point on this line, like (1,2) or (2,1), is a solution to both equations.

Quick Check! Identify the Solution Type

  • Equation 1: 3x + 2y = 5
  • Equation 2: 6x + 4y = 10
  • What type of solution does this pair have?

🎙️ Look at the ratios of the coefficients (a₁/a₂, b₁/b₂, c₁/c₂). For this pair, 3/6 = 1/2, 2/4 = 1/2, and 5/10 = 1/2. Since all ratios are equal, it means the lines are coincident, having infinitely many solutions. Excellent!

Algebraic Methods: Solving with Equations

  • Provide exact solutions without relying on graphs.
  • Three main methods: Substitution, Elimination, and Cross-Multiplication.
  • Essential for efficiency and accuracy.

🎙️ While graphical methods are great for understanding, algebraic methods are often faster and more precise, especially when solutions are not whole numbers. We'll now dive into how to solve these pairs of equations using step-by-step calculations.

➕

Method 1: Substitution Method

  • Step 1: Express one variable in terms of the other from one equation.
  • Step 2: Substitute this expression into the second equation.
  • Step 3: Solve the resulting single-variable equation.
  • Step 4: Substitute the found value back to get the other variable.

🎙️ The substitution method is like replacing one piece of information with another. You pick one equation, get 'x' in terms of 'y' (or vice versa), and then literally 'substitute' that entire expression into the other equation. This simplifies the problem to a single variable.

1Isolate a variable
2Substitute into 2nd eq
3Solve for 1st variable
4Find 2nd variable
20 / 25

Worked Example: Substitution Method

  • Solve: 2x + 3y = 11 (Eq 1) and 2x - 4y = -24 (Eq 2).
  • From Eq 1, 2x = 11 - 3y. Substitute 2x in Eq 2.
  • (11 - 3y) - 4y = -24. Solve for y.
  • Find x using the value of y.

🎙️ Following the steps: 11 - 7y = -24. So, -7y = -35, meaning y = 5. Now substitute y=5 back into 2x = 11 - 3y, which gives 2x = 11 - 15 = -4, so x = -2. The solution is x = -2, y = 5.

➡️
Step-by-Step

Method 2: Elimination Method

  • Step 1: Multiply equations to make coefficients of one variable equal.
  • Step 2: Add or subtract the equations to 'eliminate' that variable.
  • Step 3: Solve the resulting single-variable equation.
  • Step 4: Substitute the found value back to get the other variable.

🎙️ The elimination method aims to get rid of one variable by making its coefficients the same (or opposite) in both equations. Then, by adding or subtracting the equations, that variable 'disappears', leaving you with a simpler equation to solve.

1Equalize coefficients
2Add/Subtract equations
3Solve for 1st variable
4Find 2nd variable
22 / 25

Worked Example: Elimination Method

  • Solve: 3x + 4y = 10 (Eq 1) and 2x - 2y = 2 (Eq 2).
  • Multiply Eq 2 by 2: 4x - 4y = 4.
  • Add new Eq 2 to Eq 1: (3x + 4y) + (4x - 4y) = 10 + 4.
  • Solve for x, then find y.

🎙️ Adding the equations: 7x = 14, so x = 2. Now substitute x=2 into Eq 2: 2(2) - 2y = 2. This gives 4 - 2y = 2, so -2y = -2, and y = 1. The solution is x = 2, y = 1.

✂️
Cutting Out

Method 3: Cross-Multiplication Method

  • A direct formula-based method for solving a₁x + b₁y + c₁ = 0 and a₂x + b₂y + c₂ = 0.
  • Formula: x / (b₁c₂ - b₂c₁) = y / (c₁a₂ - c₂a₁) = 1 / (a₁b₂ - a₂b₁).
  • Requires careful handling of coefficients and signs.

🎙️ The cross-multiplication method is a powerful shortcut once you understand the formula. It's derived from the elimination method and gives you the solution directly. However, it's crucial to correctly identify the coefficients a₁, b₁, c₁, a₂, b₂, c₂ and apply the formula meticulously to avoid errors.

Chapter Recap: Pair of Linear Equations

  • A pair of LEITV has two equations with two variables.
  • Graphical methods show three solution types: unique (intersecting), no solution (parallel), infinitely many (coincident).
  • Algebraic methods: Substitution, Elimination, and Cross-Multiplication provide precise solutions.

🎙️ We've covered a lot in this chapter! Remember that understanding the relationship between the lines on a graph helps interpret the solutions. For practical problem-solving, the algebraic methods are your best tools. Keep practicing, and you'll master solving any pair of linear equations!

What is Heredity?

  • Passing of traits from parents to offspring.
  • Ensures continuity of species.
  • The basis of family resemblances.

🎙️ Heredity is simply the process where characteristics or 'traits' are passed from one generation to the next. Think of it like a family recipe that gets passed down, ensuring some features remain consistent across generations.

Variation: Differences within a Species

  • Offspring are not exact copies of parents.
  • Small differences are called variations.
  • Crucial for adaptation and survival.

🎙️ Even though we inherit traits, no two individuals (except identical twins) are exactly alike. These small differences, or variations, are what make each of us unique and play a very important role in how species survive and evolve over time.

How Variations Accumulate

  • Variations arise during DNA copying.
  • Sexual reproduction combines variations.
  • Leads to greater diversity over generations.

🎙️ Variations don't just appear out of nowhere. They start with tiny errors during DNA copying in asexual reproduction, and in sexual reproduction, they combine traits from two parents, leading to even more unique mixes in the offspring.

Why Variation is Important

  • Increases chances of survival.
  • Helps species adapt to changing environments.
  • Drives evolution over long periods.

🎙️ Imagine a sudden change in climate. If all individuals were identical, they might all be wiped out. Variation ensures that some individuals might have traits that help them survive, thus saving the entire species from extinction. It's nature's way of hedging bets!

  • Austrian monk and scientist.
  • Experimented with pea plants (Pisum sativum).
  • Discovered fundamental laws of inheritance.

🎙️ Gregor Mendel, an Austrian monk, is considered the 'Father of Genetics'. Through his meticulous experiments with pea plants, he laid the foundation for understanding how traits are passed down, even before DNA was fully understood.

Genes: Units of Heredity

  • Segments of DNA.
  • Carry information for specific traits.
  • Passed from parents to offspring.

🎙️ Think of genes as tiny instruction manuals within our cells. Each gene contains the code for a particular characteristic, like eye colour or height. They are the fundamental units that determine what we are like.

Chromosomes: Gene Carriers

  • Thread-like structures in the nucleus.
  • Made of DNA tightly coiled around proteins.
  • Contain thousands of genes.

🎙️ If genes are the instructions, then chromosomes are like the books that hold these instructions. They are found in the nucleus of our cells and are essentially long strands of DNA packed neatly. Humans have 23 pairs of chromosomes.

Dominant and Recessive Traits

  • Dominant: Trait that shows up.
  • Recessive: Trait that is hidden.
  • Example: Tall (dominant) vs. Dwarf (recessive) pea plants.

🎙️ When two different versions of a gene are present, one often 'dominates' and expresses itself, while the other remains 'recessive' or hidden. For instance, in pea plants, the gene for tallness is dominant over the gene for dwarfness.

Monohybrid Cross: One Trait

  • Study of inheritance of one trait.
  • Mendel crossed tall and dwarf pea plants.
  • Resulted in all tall F1 generation.

🎙️ Mendel's monohybrid cross involved tracking a single trait, like height. He found that when he crossed pure tall plants with pure dwarf plants, all the offspring in the first generation (F1) were tall, demonstrating the dominant nature of tallness.

Phenotype vs. Genotype

  • Phenotype: Observable characteristics (e.g., Tall).
  • Genotype: Genetic makeup (e.g., TT, Tt, tt).
  • Genotype determines phenotype.

🎙️ Phenotype is what you can actually see – like someone's blue eyes or their tall stature. Genotype, on the other hand, refers to the actual genetic code or combination of genes responsible for that trait. The genotype is the blueprint, and the phenotype is the final building.

How Traits are Expressed

  • Genes provide instructions for making proteins.
  • Proteins control cell functions and development.
  • This leads to observable traits.

🎙️ Genes don't directly make you tall or have blue eyes. Instead, they carry instructions to make specific proteins. These proteins then perform various functions in the body, ultimately leading to the development and expression of the observable trait.

Real Example: Inheriting Eye Colour

  • Determined by multiple genes.
  • Brown eye colour is generally dominant.
  • Blue/green eye colours are recessive.

🎙️ Eye colour is a great real-life example of heredity. While it's a bit more complex than a single gene, generally, brown eyes are dominant over blue or green eyes. This means if you inherit a brown eye gene and a blue eye gene, you'll likely have brown eyes.

Dihybrid Cross: Two Traits

  • Studies inheritance of two traits simultaneously.
  • Example: Seed shape (round/wrinkled) and colour (yellow/green).
  • Mendel observed independent assortment.

🎙️ A dihybrid cross is when we track two different traits at the same time, like the shape and colour of pea seeds. Mendel found that these traits were inherited independently of each other, meaning the inheritance of one didn't affect the other.

Sex Determination in Humans

  • Determined by sex chromosomes.
  • Females: XX chromosomes.
  • Males: XY chromosomes.

🎙️ In humans, our sex (male or female) is determined by a special pair of chromosomes called sex chromosomes. Females have two X chromosomes (XX), while males have one X and one Y chromosome (XY).

The Process of Sex Determination

  • Female produces only X-egg cells.
  • Male produces X and Y sperm cells.
  • Fertilisation determines offspring's sex.

🎙️ Since females only have X chromosomes, all their egg cells carry an X. Males, however, produce two types of sperm: some carry an X and some carry a Y. It's the sperm that fertilizes the egg that determines if the baby will be a boy or a girl.

1Female (XX) -> X eggs
2Male (XY) -> X or Y sperm
3X egg + X sperm = Girl (XX)
4X egg + Y sperm = Boy (XY)

Acquired vs. Inherited Traits

  • Inherited: Traits passed through genes (e.g., eye colour).
  • Acquired: Traits developed during lifetime (e.g., learned skills, muscle growth).
  • Acquired traits are NOT passed to offspring.

🎙️ It's important to distinguish between traits you're born with (inherited) and traits you develop during your life (acquired). Only inherited traits, those encoded in your genes, can be passed on to your children.

Inherited (Genes)
Acquired (Experience)

Example: Acquired vs. Inherited

  • Inherited: Natural hair colour, blood group.
  • Acquired: A scar from an injury, learning to play a musical instrument.
  • Your children will not inherit your scar or musical talent genetically.

🎙️ For instance, if you work out and develop strong muscles, that's an acquired trait. Your children won't automatically be born with strong muscles because you worked out. However, your natural hair colour or blood group *is* inherited.

Evolution and Heredity

  • Variations are the raw material for evolution.
  • Natural selection acts on these variations.
  • Heredity ensures beneficial traits are passed on.

🎙️ Heredity and variation are crucial for evolution. Variations provide the differences upon which natural selection can act. If a variation gives an organism an advantage, heredity ensures that this beneficial trait is passed to future generations, leading to evolutionary change.

Genetic Disorders

🎙️ Sometimes, there can be errors or mutations in genes or chromosomes, leading to genetic disorders. These conditions can be inherited and affect various bodily functions, like colour blindness where a person cannot distinguish certain colours.

What is a Chemical Reaction?

  • Process where new substances are formed.
  • Reactants transform into Products.
  • Atoms rearrange, but are not lost.

🎙️ A chemical reaction is essentially a process where one or more substances, called reactants, change into different substances, called products. Think of it like Lego bricks: you can take apart one structure and build a completely new one with the same bricks.

How to Identify a Chemical Reaction?

  • Change in state (solid, liquid, gas).
  • Change in color or temperature.
  • Evolution of a gas (bubbles).
  • Formation of a precipitate (solid).

🎙️ We can observe several signs that indicate a chemical reaction has taken place. These include changes like a solid forming in a liquid, a new gas bubbling out, a noticeable color change, or even a change in temperature, making the container feel hotter or colder.

Example: Burning Magnesium Ribbon

  • Magnesium (Mg) reacts with Oxygen (O₂).
  • Forms Magnesium Oxide (MgO).

  • Powdery white ash is the product.

🎙️ One classic example is burning a magnesium ribbon. When magnesium metal reacts with oxygen from the air, it produces a dazzling white flame and forms a white powder called magnesium oxide. This shows a change in state, color, and temperature.

The Law of Conservation of Mass

  • Mass cannot be created nor destroyed.
  • Total mass of reactants = Total mass of products.
  • Number of atoms of each element must be equal.
  • Unbalanced equations are skeletal equations.

🎙️ Balancing chemical equations is crucial because of the Law of Conservation of Mass. This law states that atoms are merely rearranged during a chemical reaction; they are neither created nor destroyed. Therefore, the number of atoms of each element must be the same on both sides of the equation.

Hit and Trial Method

  • Start with the most complex formula.
  • Balance atoms one by one.
  • Often start with metals, then non-metals, then O, then H.
  • Adjust coefficients (numbers in front of formulas).

🎙️ The hit and trial method involves systematically adjusting the coefficients in front of chemical formulas until the number of atoms for each element is equal on both sides. It's like a puzzle where you try different numbers until everything fits perfectly.

Worked Example: Balancing H₂ + O₂ → H₂O

  • H₂ + O₂ → H₂O (Unbalanced)
  • Oxygen: 2 on left, 1 on right. Put '2' before H₂O.
  • H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O
  • Hydrogen: 2 on left, 4 on right. Put '2' before H₂.
  • 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O (Balanced)

🎙️ Let's balance the formation of water. We see 2 oxygen atoms on the left and 1 on the right. So, we place a '2' in front of H₂O. Now we have 4 hydrogen atoms on the right, but only 2 on the left. Placing a '2' in front of H₂ balances the hydrogen. Now, all atoms are equal on both sides!

When Two Become One

  • Two or more reactants combine to form a single product.
  • A + B → AB
  • Example: Burning of coal (C + O₂ → CO₂).
  • Always exothermic (releases heat).

🎙️ In a combination reaction, two or more simpler substances join together to form a single, more complex substance. Think of it as two friends meeting and becoming one team. These reactions often release energy.

Joining Pieces

Hydrogen and Oxygen Combine

  • Hydrogen gas (H₂) reacts with Oxygen gas (O₂).
  • Forms liquid Water (H₂O).
  • 2H₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2H₂O(l)
  • A common and vital combination reaction.

🎙️ A perfect example is the formation of water from hydrogen and oxygen gases. Two molecules of hydrogen combine with one molecule of oxygen to produce two molecules of water. This is a fundamental reaction that sustains life.

Breaking Down Complex Substances

  • A single reactant breaks down into two or more simpler products.
  • AB → A + B
  • Requires energy (heat, light, electricity).
  • Opposite of combination reactions.

🎙️ Decomposition reactions are the opposite of combination reactions. Here, a single compound breaks down into two or more simpler substances. This process usually requires an input of energy, which can be in the form of heat, light, or electricity.

Limestone to Quicklime

  • Calcium Carbonate (limestone) is heated.
  • Breaks down into Calcium Oxide (quicklime) and Carbon Dioxide.
  • CaCO₃(s) + Heat → CaO(s) + CO₂(g)
  • Used in cement manufacturing.

🎙️ When limestone, which is calcium carbonate, is heated strongly, it decomposes into calcium oxide, also known as quicklime, and carbon dioxide gas. This reaction is very important in industries like cement production.

The "More Reactive" Takes Over

  • A more reactive element displaces a less reactive element.
  • From its compound solution.
  • A + BC → AC + B
  • Example: Zinc displaces Copper from CuSO₄.

🎙️ In a displacement reaction, a more reactive element kicks out a less reactive element from its compound. Imagine a stronger person taking the seat of a weaker person. The reactivity series helps us predict which element will displace another.

Iron Displaces Copper

  • Iron (Fe) is more reactive than Copper (Cu).
  • Iron displaces Copper from Copper Sulphate (CuSO₄).
  • Fe(s) + CuSO₄(aq) → FeSO₄(aq) + Cu(s)
  • Blue solution fades, reddish-brown copper forms.

🎙️ When an iron nail is placed in a blue copper sulphate solution, the iron displaces the copper. The iron nail gets coated with reddish-brown copper, and the blue color of the solution fades as iron sulphate is formed. This clearly shows one element replacing another.

Swapping Partners

  • Two different compounds exchange their ions.
  • Forms two new compounds.
  • AB + CD → AD + CB
  • Often results in precipitate or water formation.

🎙️ In a double displacement reaction, there's a mutual exchange of ions between two compounds. It's like two couples swapping partners. These reactions often lead to the formation of an insoluble solid called a precipitate, or sometimes water.

Partners Exchange

Barium Sulphate Precipitate

  • Barium Chloride (BaCl₂) reacts with Sodium Sulphate (Na₂SO₄).
  • Forms white precipitate of Barium Sulphate (BaSO₄).
  • Also forms Sodium Chloride (NaCl).
  • BaCl₂(aq) + Na₂SO₄(aq) → BaSO₄(s) + 2NaCl(aq)

🎙️ When solutions of barium chloride and sodium sulphate are mixed, a white, insoluble solid, barium sulphate, is formed. This solid settles down at the bottom and is called a precipitate. This is a classic example of a double displacement reaction.

The Dance of Electrons

  • Oxidation: Gain of oxygen OR Loss of hydrogen OR Loss of electrons.
  • Reduction: Loss of oxygen OR Gain of hydrogen OR Gain of electrons.
  • Both occur simultaneously (Redox).

🎙️ Oxidation and reduction are fundamental concepts. Oxidation can be thought of as gaining oxygen or losing hydrogen or electrons. Conversely, reduction is losing oxygen or gaining hydrogen or electrons. These two processes always happen together, hence 'Redox' reactions.

Redox in Our Daily Lives

  • Rusting of iron: Iron gets oxidized by oxygen and water.
  • Respiration: Glucose is oxidized to release energy.
  • Browning of fruits: Fruits oxidize when exposed to air.
  • Combustion: Fuels oxidize to produce heat and light.

🎙️ Redox reactions are everywhere! The rusting of iron is an oxidation process. The energy you get from food comes from the oxidation of glucose during respiration. Even the browning of a cut apple is due to oxidation.

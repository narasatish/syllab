What is Light?

  • A form of energy that enables us to see.
  • Travels in straight lines (rectilinear propagation).
  • Does not require a medium to travel (can travel in vacuum).

🎙️ Light is everywhere around us, allowing us to perceive the world. It's a type of energy that helps our eyes detect objects. Remember, light travels incredibly fast and always in a straight line.

Key Properties of Light

  • Travels at 3 x 10^8 m/s in vacuum.
  • Exhibits both wave and particle nature.
  • Can be reflected, refracted, diffracted, and scattered.

🎙️ Light has many interesting properties. Its speed in a vacuum is the fastest known speed in the universe. It also behaves sometimes like a wave and sometimes like tiny particles, which explains its various interactions with matter.

Reflection of Light

  • The bouncing back of light when it strikes a surface.
  • Occurs from smooth, polished surfaces like mirrors.
  • Essential for us to see non-luminous objects.

🎙️ When light hits a surface and bounces off, we call this reflection. Think about looking at yourself in a mirror – that's reflection in action. It's how we see most objects around us that don't produce their own light.

Laws of Reflection

  • Angle of incidence equals angle of reflection.
  • Incident ray, reflected ray, and normal lie in the same plane.

🎙️ There are two fundamental laws governing how light reflects. The first law states that the angle at which light hits a surface is equal to the angle at which it bounces off. The second law ensures that all these rays and the normal are in the same flat surface.

Types of Mirrors: Plane Mirror

  • A flat, smooth reflecting surface.
  • Forms virtual, erect, and same-size images.
  • Image distance equals object distance.

🎙️ A plane mirror is simply a flat mirror. The image you see in a plane mirror appears to be behind the mirror, is upright, and is the same size as you. It's also laterally inverted, meaning left appears right.

Image Formation by Plane Mirror

  • Virtual image: Light rays do not actually meet.
  • Erect image: Upright, not inverted.
  • Laterally inverted: Left appears right.

🎙️ When you look into a plane mirror, the image you see is virtual because the light rays only appear to diverge from a point behind the mirror. It's also erect, meaning it's not upside down, but your left and right sides are swapped.

Spherical Mirrors: Concave & Convex

  • Mirrors that are part of a hollow sphere.
  • Concave: Reflecting surface curved inwards.
  • Convex: Reflecting surface curved outwards.

🎙️ Spherical mirrors are curved mirrors, unlike flat plane mirrors. They are essentially cut from a larger sphere. Concave mirrors curve inwards, like a spoon, while convex mirrors bulge outwards.

Key Terms for Spherical Mirrors

  • Pole (P): Center of the mirror's reflecting surface.
  • Center of Curvature (C): Center of the sphere it's part of.
  • Principal Focus (F): Point where parallel rays meet (or appear to meet).

🎙️ To understand how spherical mirrors work, we need to know a few key terms. The pole is the mirror's center. The center of curvature is the center of the imaginary sphere from which the mirror is cut. The principal focus is where parallel light rays converge or appear to diverge from after reflection.

Image Formation by Convex Mirror

  • Always forms virtual, erect, and diminished images.
  • Image is always formed behind the mirror, between P and F.
  • Regardless of object position.

🎙️ Convex mirrors are much simpler in terms of image formation. They always produce a smaller, upright, and virtual image. This consistent behavior makes them ideal for certain applications.

Refraction of Light

  • The bending of light as it passes from one medium to another.
  • Caused by change in speed of light.
  • Occurs at the interface of two transparent media.

🎙️ Now let's move on to refraction. Imagine a pencil in a glass of water – it looks bent. This bending of light when it travels from one material to another, like air to water, is called refraction. It happens because light changes its speed.

Laws of Refraction

  • Incident ray, refracted ray, and normal lie in the same plane.
  • Ratio of sine of angle of incidence to sine of angle of refraction is constant (Snell's Law).

🎙️ Similar to reflection, refraction also has two laws. The first is about the rays and normal being in the same plane. The second, known as Snell's Law, quantifies how much light bends based on the properties of the two media.

Refractive Index

  • A measure of how much light bends when entering a medium.
  • Ratio of speed of light in vacuum to speed of light in the medium.
  • Higher refractive index means more bending.

🎙️ The refractive index tells us how 'optically dense' a material is. A higher refractive index means light slows down more in that material, causing it to bend more significantly when it enters or leaves. It's a crucial concept for understanding lenses and prisms.

Lenses: Concave & Convex

  • Transparent materials that refract light.
  • Convex Lens (Converging): Thicker in middle, converges light.
  • Concave Lens (Diverging): Thinner in middle, diverges light.

🎙️ Lenses are key optical instruments that use refraction to focus or spread light. Convex lenses are like magnifying glasses, bringing light rays together. Concave lenses spread light rays out.

Key Terms for Lenses

  • Optical Centre (O): Central point of the lens.
  • Principal Focus (F): Point where parallel rays converge or appear to diverge.
  • Focal Length (f): Distance between O and F.

🎙️ Just like mirrors, lenses also have important points and distances. The optical center is the geometric center of the lens. The principal focus is where parallel light rays interact with the lens, and the focal length is a measure of the lens's power.

Recap: Light — Reflection and Refraction

  • Light is a form of energy that allows us to see.
  • Reflection: Bouncing back of light (mirrors).
  • Refraction: Bending of light (lenses, water).
  • Both phenomena follow specific laws.

🎙️ To summarize, we've explored the fascinating world of light, understanding how it reflects off surfaces like mirrors and refracts, or bends, as it passes through different materials like lenses. These principles are fundamental to how we perceive the world and how many optical instruments work.

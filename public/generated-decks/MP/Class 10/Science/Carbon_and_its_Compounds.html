Meet Carbon: The Versatile Element

  • Atomic Number 6, Symbol C.
  • Found in all living things.
  • Forms millions of compounds.

🎙️ Carbon is everywhere! From the air we breathe (CO2) to the food we eat, from the clothes we wear to the fuel we use. It's the backbone of life itself.

Where Do We Find Carbon?

  • Earth's crust (0.02% as minerals like carbonates).
  • Atmosphere (0.03% as Carbon Dioxide).
  • All organic matter (plants, animals, fossil fuels).

🎙️ Though a small percentage in the Earth's crust and atmosphere, Carbon's impact is immense. It's the core component of all organic compounds, which means everything living!

The Power of Sharing: Covalent Bonds

  • Carbon achieves stability by sharing electrons.
  • Forms strong bonds with other atoms.
  • Unlike ionic bonds, no transfer of electrons.

🎙️ Carbon is unique because it doesn't usually gain or lose electrons to form ions. Instead, it shares its electrons with other atoms, forming very stable covalent bonds. This sharing is key to its versatility.

Carbon's Special Number: Tetravalency

  • Carbon has 4 valence electrons.
  • It needs 4 more electrons to complete its octet.
  • Forms 4 covalent bonds.

🎙️ The word 'tetra' means four. Carbon's ability to form four bonds makes it incredibly versatile. Think of it like a central hub that can connect to four other things simultaneously.

Chains and Rings: Catenation

  • Carbon atoms can link with other Carbon atoms.
  • Forms long chains, branched chains, and rings.
  • Results in a huge variety of compounds.

🎙️ Catenation is a unique property of carbon. Imagine building with LEGO blocks – carbon atoms can connect endlessly with each other, creating incredibly complex structures. This is why there are so many carbon compounds!

⛓️
Carbon Chains

Why is Carbon So Special?

  • Tetravalency (forms 4 bonds).
  • Catenation (self-linking property).
  • Ability to form multiple bonds (single, double, triple).

🎙️ These three properties – tetravalency, catenation, and forming multiple bonds – are the 'superpowers' that make carbon the most versatile element. They allow it to form millions of different compounds.

Introducing Hydrocarbons: Carbon's Best Friends

  • Compounds made only of Carbon and Hydrogen.
  • Simplest organic compounds.
  • Found in fossil fuels like petrol and natural gas.

🎙️ Hydrocarbons are the fundamental building blocks of many organic compounds. As their name suggests, they contain only hydrogen and carbon atoms. They are crucial to our energy needs.

Saturated Hydrocarbons: All Single Bonds (Alkanes)

  • Carbon atoms are linked by only single bonds.
  • They are very stable and less reactive.
  • General formula: CnH2n+2.

🎙️ In saturated hydrocarbons, every carbon atom is 'saturated' with hydrogen atoms, meaning it has the maximum possible number of single bonds. Alkanes are a prime example of saturated hydrocarbons.

➖
Single Bond
10 / 25

Unsaturated Hydrocarbons: Double & Triple Bonds

  • Contain at least one double or triple bond.
  • More reactive than saturated hydrocarbons.
  • Alkenes (double bond): CnH2n; Alkynes (triple bond): CnH2n-2.

🎙️ Unsaturated hydrocarbons have 'room' for more hydrogen atoms because of their double or triple bonds. This makes them more reactive and useful for various chemical reactions.

Example: Methane (CH4) - The Simplest Alkane

  • One carbon atom bonded to four hydrogen atoms.
  • Main component of natural gas.
  • Used as a fuel and in chemical synthesis.

🎙️ Methane is the simplest hydrocarbon and a very common example of an alkane. It's a powerful greenhouse gas and a vital energy source for homes and industries.

Natural Gas

Example: Ethene (C2H4) - An Alkene

  • Two carbon atoms with a double bond between them.
  • Each carbon is also bonded to two hydrogen atoms.
  • Used for ripening fruits and making plastics (polyethene).

🎙️ Ethene is a crucial alkene. It's naturally produced by plants and is used commercially to ripen fruits like bananas. It's also the building block for many common plastics.

🍌🍌
Fruit Ripening

Example: Ethyne (C2H2) - An Alkyne

  • Two carbon atoms with a triple bond between them.
  • Each carbon is also bonded to one hydrogen atom.
  • Commonly known as acetylene, used in welding torches.

🎙️ Ethyne, or acetylene, is an important alkyne. When mixed with oxygen, it produces an extremely hot flame, making it ideal for welding and cutting metals.

  • Attach to the hydrocarbon chain.

🎙️ Imagine a hydrocarbon chain as a car chassis. A functional group is like the engine – it dictates how the car will behave. These groups are responsible for the distinct chemical reactions of different organic compounds.

Homologous Series: A Family of Compounds

  • Series of compounds with the same functional group.
  • Differ by a -CH2- unit.
  • Show similar chemical properties, gradual change in physical properties.

🎙️ Think of a homologous series as a family where all members share the same last name (functional group) and have similar traits. As you go down the series, the size increases by a fixed unit, leading to predictable changes in properties.

Isomerism: Same Formula, Different Structure

  • Compounds with the same molecular formula.
  • But different structural arrangements of atoms.
  • Leads to different physical and chemical properties.

🎙️ Isomers are like twins who look different even though they share the same genetic code. In chemistry, the same atoms can be arranged in different ways, creating entirely different compounds with distinct properties.

Chemical Properties: Combustion (Burning)

  • Carbon compounds burn in oxygen.
  • Produce carbon dioxide, water, heat, and light.
  • Example: CH4 + 2O2 → CO2 + 2H2O + Heat + Light.

🎙️ Combustion is a common reaction for many carbon compounds, especially hydrocarbons. It's how we get energy from fuels like petrol, LPG, and natural gas. Remember the products: CO2 and water.

Chemical Properties: Oxidation (Adding Oxygen)

  • Addition of oxygen to a substance.
  • Can be achieved by oxidizing agents (e.g., alkaline KMnO4).
  • Example: Ethanol (alcohol) can be oxidized to Ethanoic acid (carboxylic acid).

🎙️ Oxidation is a fundamental chemical process. In organic chemistry, it often involves converting an alcohol into an aldehyde or a carboxylic acid. Oxidizing agents help facilitate this process.

Chemical Properties: Addition Reaction (Unsaturated Compounds)

  • Unsaturated hydrocarbons (alkenes, alkynes) undergo this.
  • Atoms are added across the double or triple bond.
  • Example: Hydrogenation of vegetable oil (adding H2).

🎙️ Addition reactions are characteristic of compounds with double or triple bonds. The bonds 'open up' to accommodate new atoms. A common example is converting liquid vegetable oils into solid fats by adding hydrogen.

🤝
Joining Together

Chemical Properties: Substitution Reaction (Saturated Compounds)

  • Saturated hydrocarbons (alkanes) undergo this.
  • One atom or group is replaced by another.
  • Example: Methane reacts with chlorine in sunlight to form chloromethane.

🎙️ Unlike unsaturated compounds, alkanes are quite stable due to their single bonds. They react by substitution, where one atom is swapped out for another. This usually requires specific conditions like sunlight.

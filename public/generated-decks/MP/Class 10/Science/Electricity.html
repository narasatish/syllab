What is Electricity?

  • It's the flow of tiny particles called electrons.
  • It's a form of energy that powers devices.
  • Think of it like water flowing through pipes.

🎙️ Simply put, electricity is the movement of charge. These charges are carried by extremely small particles called electrons. This movement generates the energy we use every day.

Electric Charge

  • Exists in two types: positive (+) and negative (-).
  • Like charges repel, unlike charges attract.

🎙️ Every atom contains charged particles. Protons have positive charge and electrons have negative charge. The balance of these charges determines if an object is neutral or charged.

Electric Current (I)

  • Formula: I = Q/t (Charge / time).
  • Its SI unit is Ampere (A).

🎙️ Electric current tells us how much charge passes through a point in a given amount of time. A larger current means more charge is flowing per second. It's like the flow rate of water in a river.

Electric Potential (V)

🎙️ Electric potential is a fundamental concept. Imagine a water tank placed at a height; the water at the top has higher potential energy. Similarly, electric potential is the 'electrical pressure' at a point in a circuit.

Potential Difference (V)

  • Work done to move a unit charge between two points.
  • Formula: V = W/Q (Work / Charge).
  • It's the 'push' that makes charges move.

🎙️ For current to flow, there must be a difference in electric potential between two points. This difference is what we call potential difference, often simply called voltage. A battery creates this potential difference.

Resistance (R)

  • The opposition offered to the flow of electric current.
  • Its SI unit is Ohm (Ω).
  • Conductors have low resistance, insulators have high resistance.

🎙️ Imagine electrons trying to move through a crowded street; resistance is like the obstacles they face. Every material offers some resistance to current flow. Good conductors have very few obstacles.

🚧

Factors Affecting Resistance

  • Length (L): R ∝ L (longer wire, more resistance).
  • Area of cross-section (A): R ∝ 1/A (thicker wire, less resistance).
  • Nature of material: Different materials have different resistivity.
  • Temperature: Resistance generally increases with temperature.

🎙️ The resistance of a conductor is not fixed; it depends on its physical properties. A longer, thinner wire will have more resistance than a shorter, thicker one of the same material.

Resistors in Series

  • Resistors connected end-to-end, forming a single path.
  • Total resistance (Rs) = R1 + R2 + R3 + ...
  • Current is the same through all resistors.

🎙️ When resistors are connected in series, they act like a single, longer resistor. The total resistance increases, and the current has to pass through each resistor sequentially.

Resistors in Parallel

  • Resistors connected across the same two points, creating multiple paths.
  • 1/Rp = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + 1/R3 + ...
  • Potential difference is the same across all resistors.

🎙️ In a parallel connection, current can choose different paths. This effectively reduces the total resistance, as there are more ways for the current to flow. Household circuits are often wired in parallel.

Worked Example: Series & Parallel

  • Two resistors, 2Ω and 4Ω, are connected to a 6V battery.
  • If in series: Rs = 2+4 = 6Ω. Current I = V/Rs = 6/6 = 1A.
  • If in parallel: 1/Rp = 1/2 + 1/4 = 3/4 => Rp = 4/3 Ω. Current I = V/Rp = 6/(4/3) = 4.5A.

🎙️ Let's apply what we learned. Notice how the total resistance and current change significantly depending on whether the resistors are in series or parallel. This is a common type of problem you'll encounter.

Heating Effect of Electric Current

  • When current flows through a resistor, electrical energy converts to heat.
  • Known as Joule's Law of Heating.
  • Formula: H = I²Rt (Heat = Current² × Resistance × Time).

🎙️ You might have noticed that wires get warm when current flows through them. This is due to the heating effect. The resistance of the wire causes some energy to be lost as heat, a principle used in many appliances.

Electrical Energy: Commercial Unit

  • The total work done by electric current.
  • Commercial unit is kilowatt-hour (kWh).
  • 1 kWh = 3.6 × 10⁶ Joules (J).

🎙️ While Joules is the SI unit for energy, for practical purposes like electricity bills, we use kilowatt-hour. This unit represents the energy consumed by a 1kW appliance running for 1 hour.
